AIBuildAI-2.5: Efficient Autonomous AI Model Development Through LLM-Guided Tree Search

Peijia Qin · Ruiyi Zhang · Qi Cao · Han Guo, et al. (UC San Diego) 2026-08-31 · cs.AI

Key Metric
73.3%
Medal rate across MLE-Bench's 75 Kaggle tasks — first on the official leaderboard (runner-up 70.7%, tree-search baseline MLEvolve 61.3%), under a 24-hour, 1×A100 budget.
Why This Matters

Autonomous ML-building agents waste scarce compute on bad candidates; smarter selection and scheduling squeeze more real training runs from the same 24-hour, one-GPU budget.

⚠ Critical Edge

No ablations, no error bars: the 2.6-point medal-rate lead over the runner-up equals roughly two medals on 75 tasks — within plausible run-to-run noise for stochastic LLM agents.

01 Methodology — How It Works

  • The regime: tree search over candidate training pipelines where every evaluation costs hours of GPU training — unlike seconds-scale code search — so executed candidates are few, rewards scarce and noisy.
  • Agent division of labor: a designer proposes 7 varied initial strategies, a coder implements and debugs them, a reviser proposes revision instructions; a data-split agent fixes one global validation set so rewards stay comparable across the tree.
  • LLM-guided selection (the core): a local judge scores each pending leaf 0–5 on expected improvement over its parent, grounding in measured sibling/parent results, and single-slot feasibility — automatic zero for reward hacking (fabricated metrics, validation leakage). A global selector re-ranks the pool from judge scores plus search state: favor diversity, discount dead-end lineages, suppress near-duplicates, shift explore→exploit as budget shrinks.
  • Resource-aware scheduler: launches ranked training jobs whenever GPU memory/utilization and CPU thresholds leave headroom, with a stabilization window per launch.
  • Per-role model routing: a router assigns each agent role the cheapest sufficient model from Claude Haiku 4.5 / Sonnet 4.6 / Opus 4.7 per node, using per-run lineage memory plus a cross-run knowledge system of distilled "model X suffices for role Y under Z" cards.
  • Protocol: MLE-Bench (official leaderboard, 24h, 24 vCPUs, 256 GB RAM, 1×A100) and AIRS-Bench (six research tasks) against MLEvolve (UCT selection, Opus 4.7 backbone) under identical budget and hardware.

Strengths

  • Clear problem decomposition: three concrete failure modes (sparse rewards, idle hardware, uniformly expensive inference) mapped to three targeted mechanisms, each well argued.
  • Judge + selector is a sensible answer to reward scarcity — an LLM prior prices unexecuted candidates before training cost is paid, then fuses with measured rewards; a real departure from pure UCT.
  • Strong empirics on third-party infrastructure: public leaderboard first place, and 6/6 controlled wins spanning molecules, text, and time series.
  • Routing study reports metric AND USD cost, showing per-node assignment plus escalation can even beat the strongest fixed tier at half price.
  • Honest scoping of the scheduler as heuristic, with a concrete research agenda; official grading protocol rather than self-reported metrics.

Limitations

  • No ablation on MLE-Bench decomposing judge/selector, scheduler, routing, and the inherited knowledge base — you cannot tell which mechanism buys 73.3% vs 61.3%.
  • Single runs, no seeds, repeats, or confidence intervals; several margins are small enough that noise matters.
  • AIRS-Bench generalization tested against exactly one baseline agent; leaderboard numbers may not be apples-to-apples on backbone or compute.
  • Self-referential lineage: cross-run accumulated knowledge plausibly inflates results, never quantified.
  • Judge and router verdicts are uncalibrated LLM behaviors; the reward-hacking guard has no reported detection or false-negative rate on leak-prone tasks.
  • High replication cost: 75 tasks × 24-hour A100 runs plus commercial Claude API spend, tied to model versions that may be deprecated.

03 Questions for Further Exploration

  1. How much does each mechanism contribute on MLE-Bench — an ablation swapping judge+selector for UCT, or disabling routing/scheduling, would attribute the 73.3% vs 61.3% gap.
  2. Is the 2.6-point lead robust across repeated runs, or within run-to-run noise of stochastic LLM agents?
  3. How often does the judge's 0–5 score correlate with realized reward, and what is the empirical reward-hacking detection (and false-positive) rate on leak-prone Kaggle tasks?
  4. Does the cross-run router knowledge system keep paying off as the task distribution shifts away from what it accumulated knowledge on?
  5. Could the scheduler combine with pre-execution resource prediction and GPU sharing (MPS, MUX-style colocation), and would that shift the selector's exploration–exploitation trade-off?
  6. Does LLM-guided selection still help when evaluations are cheap and rewards less noisy (AlphaEvolve/FunSearch-style program discovery) than full model training?
