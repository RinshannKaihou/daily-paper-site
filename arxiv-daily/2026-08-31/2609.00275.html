The Irreversibility Budget: Fleet-Level Risk Accounting and Admission Control for Agent Operating Systems
Bardia Mohammadi, Laurent Bindschaedler · MPI-SWS · arXiv 2609.00275 · AgenticOS 2026 workshop
agent-operating-systems LLM-agent-safety risk-accounting admission-control simulation-study
Key metric
48×
Overdraw of the $250k tolerance at 1,000 agents under local per-effect gates (2.4× at 50, 9.7× at 200) — while the budget ledger holds near 0.48× at every fleet size.
Why this matters

Per-effect safety is not fleet safety: as autonomous agents gain the power to act irreversibly, budget-style admission control becomes necessary infrastructure.

Critical edge

The headline is partly built-in: with honest 95th-percentile charges and reserve-before-commit, the ledger must hold — “0 overdraws” mostly confirms the escrow arithmetic.

01 Methodology

Treat irreversible exposure as a metered OS resource: price it, reserve it atomically before commit, and deny the marginal effect that would overdraw.

Resource model

  • An effect’s charge is the q-quantile of its residual-loss distribution (loss surviving after compensation, recovery, revocation) — supplied by a pricing service from the tool spec, never from the model.
  • Each principal (agent → workflow → tenant) holds a windowed budget; admission is value-at-risk control — Algorithm 1 reserves atomically across the whole path or denies.

Trusted runtime

typed effect spec → registry canonicalization (tool identity + endpoint + receipts) → conservative charge → offline calibration → settlement audit → dependency-driven repricing
  • Type cannot be chosen by the caller: a compromised workflow cannot declare a final transfer refundable; unresolvable effects are charged at the most expensive matching class.
  • Risk pressure doubles as a scheduling signal: tighten confidence, demote low-value spenders, expire stale reservations, escalate to rate-limited audited authority.

Evaluation setup

  • Discrete-event procurement simulation, 300 seeded runs ($250k tolerance, c₀.₉₅ = 0.52v), against local gates, face-value/pooled caps, static partitions, a reactive circuit breaker, hierarchical sub-budgets.
  • Trace study of 38,452 public τ-bench/AgentDojo trajectories tests the shared-trigger premise; simulator, seeds, and trace analysis released.

02 Results

Every agent passing its own gate is not the fleet passing the limit — and the gap widens with scale, exactly what per-call controls structurally cannot see.

Liveness & cost

  • 1.8× the value of a face-value cap and 1.7× the strongest pooled cap with honest typed charges; B=R/2 still preserves 93% of routine traffic; B=2R reintroduces overdraw in up to 33% of runs.
  • Scheduling arms: attacker value $459k → $200k (holds vs 40 colluders); sibling throughput 48% → 89%; audited re-authorization restores 93% post-burst liveness.
  • Trace premise: external effects are 22% of tool calls; timing is task-determined (z up to 198); one planted injection propagates the same effect across 84% of a fleet on average.
  • Reserve-then-confirm across three ledgers: 2.6 µs median, 240 bytes per live reservation (in-memory, single-host bound).

03 Strengths

What holds up
  • The composition failure is real and structurally explained — no per-call gate can see the running sum — and quantified at four fleet sizes, not one.
  • RQ4 is exemplary honesty: it quantifies exactly when the mechanism fails and derives the design rule that charges should fail conservative (visible throttling, not silent overdraw).
  • A two-layer contract stated up front: unconditional declared-charge bound vs conditional realized-loss bound; §6 names its own biases.
  • Liveness is engineered, not denied: per-workflow ledgers, fair-share admission, and audited re-authorization keep the budget from being deny-everything.
  • Premise tested, not assumed — 38,452 public trajectories; artifact (simulator, seeds, trace analysis) released for verification.

04 Limitations

What does not
  • Accounting only — no deployed agent OS; refunds, capability delegation, vector charges, and the full reserve-confirm-cancel lifecycle are unexercised.
  • Pricing is the open core — hand-constructed in one financial domain; misdeclaration, correlated recovery, and a five-tick repricing lag all break the realized-loss bound. No calibration method offered.
  • Distributed enforcement unresolved — the 2.6 µs microbenchmark excludes persistence, replication, and partition behavior.
  • VaR is non-subadditive and asserted rather than argued; budget-size selection left as a business problem.
  • Partial trace evidence — keyword-based effect typing with no noise estimate; the τ-bench permutation null mostly shows task determinism; only 35 curated AgentDojo injections.
  • Workshop-level review; the closest baselines in Table 1 include the authors’ own prior systems.

05 Questions for further exploration

  • Can residual-loss charges be learned from settlement receipts with a guarantee that pricing errors fail conservative (visible throttling) rather than silently (overdraw)?
  • Does a coherent risk measure such as expected shortfall change the cross-principal aggregation result, given VaR’s non-subadditivity across hierarchical ledgers?
  • What is the measured latency/availability cost of a durable, coordinated budget authority across hosts — and where does the CALM boundary between hard and advisory budgets fall?
  • Can shared-trigger detection reprice before the burst commits exposure, or is dependency-aware pricing inherently reactive?
  • How do vector budgets over incommensurable harms (money, data loss, availability, legal exposure) change the liveness trade-off seen in the scalar financial setting?
  • What happens to registry canonicalization against real prompt-injection defenses — and when the pricing service itself is compromised or slow?
