Zero-ablate distractor-language latents and amplify target latents to steer language best — frequency-selected latents win, and no single canonical language direction exists.

Latent Mechanisms of Language Control in Multilingual Language Models
Ryo Mitsuhashi, Sabri Boughorbel, Majd Hawasly · Princeton / QCRI · arXiv 2609.00325
mechanistic-interpretability multilingual-LLM language-steering cross-layer-transcoders code-switching
Key metric
99.07 vs 79.83
FreqSel’s total top-1 logit-margin change on Gemma-2-2B Antonyms under Zero+Amp — +19.2 over ValSel; target amplification alone reaches only 90.83.
Why this matters

Language control in multilingual LLMs is causally localized in sparse dictionary latents — inference-time steering can exploit it without any retraining.

Critical edge

No cheap PCA / difference-in-means baseline is ever run, so “FreqSel wins” never establishes whether CLT latents are even the right tool for steering.

Methodology Results Strengths & Limits Questions

01 Methodology

Three ways to find language latents in cross-layer transcoders, four ways to intervene with them, and two new controlled code-switching benchmarks to score it all.

Setup

  • Pretrained CLTs: Gemma Scope for Gemma-2-2B-pt (26 layers × 16,384 latents) and Qwen3-4B (36 × 163,840); 7 languages (en/fr/de/es/zh/ja/ko) from FLORES+.
  • Before porting ValSel/FreqSel from single-layer SAEs to CLTs, the original papers’ validation experiments are replicated (Appendix C).

Three selectors

  • ValSel — activation-magnitude “monolinguality” score, top-K=50 per language.
  • FreqSel — activation-frequency thresholds (T=0.8, N=98%, M=10%).
  • AnnSel (new) — Circuit Tracer attribution graphs → greedy path pruning → keep latents whose Neuronpedia description names the language (freq ≥ 0.1×max): interpretable by construction.

Interventions & evaluation

amplify target · zero distractors · direction ablation (1L / multi-L) · Zero+Amp · 1L+Amp
  • Antonyms: 100 pairs/language, 42 ordered context→target pairs; metric is top-1 logit-margin change against the same word’s translations in the other six languages.
  • Enumerations: open-ended list completion, scored by length-normalized logprob change. Plus knock-out, sweeps, fixed-budget, and GPU-h/kWh cost accounting.

02 Results

The “crude” zero ablation beats the nominally stronger direction ablation — and the knock-out experiments show the methods find overlapping functional subsets, not one direction.

Costs and hidden failures

  • AnnSel costs ~10×: 54 GPU-h (4.4 kWh) vs 5 for ValSel/FreqSel on Gemma-2-2B; 107 vs 19–23 GPU-h on Qwen3-4B.
  • Fixed-budget twist: capped at equal set sizes, AnnSel wins (13.73 vs 11.50 / 10.57) despite as few as 7 latents for Korean — but in one model/task setting only.
  • AnnSel’s Korean collapse (avg Δ 4.81 vs FreqSel ~35.9) tracks annotation yield (7 latents vs 45+), not language structure; on Qwen3-4B it finds zero effective English amplification signal.

03 Strengths

What holds up
  • First systematic head-to-head of language-latent selection on CLTs, with the original methods’ validation replicated to justify the port.
  • Clean crossed design — 42 ordered context→target pairs, and the margin metric competes against the same word’s translations: a sensible adversarial competitor set.
  • Practically useful strategy findings — Zero+Amp > 1L+Amp and zero-ablation > direction-ablation, mechanistically motivated by latent overlap.
  • The knock-out experiment is a clever probe of set redundancy that goes beyond “does steering work”.
  • Exceptional appendix transparency — full sweeps, per-language tables, cost in GPU-h and kWh, held-out generations; code and benchmarks released.

04 Limitations

What does not
  • Circular comparison — no PCA / language-neuron baselines, so CLT latents are never tested against ~free alternatives.
  • AnnSel’s fragility is a keyword artifact — “Non-English characters” selected as an English latent; Korean yields 7 latents; performance tracks annotation coverage, not language structure.
  • Synthetic, templated evaluation — one sentence frame and list completion; free generation is qualitative only, with no language-ID or fluency metric.
  • Aggregate numbers hide backfiring — the unitless 99.07 sum conceals ValSel’s −6.17 on Qwen English and the −165.01 direction-ablation failure.
  • Knock-out asymmetry unexplained — no overlap statistics and set-size confound uncontrolled.
  • Thin generalization by the authors’ own admission — two small base models, 7 mostly high-resource languages, findings tied to CLT availability.

05 Questions for further exploration

  • Would the FreqSel > AnnSel > ValSel ranking hold on standard single-layer SAEs and larger instruction-tuned models, or is it an artifact of these two transcoder releases?
  • Does a near-free PCA / difference-in-means direction match CLT-latent steering on the same protocol — and without it, is the 54 GPU-h AnnSel pipeline ever worth it?
  • What exactly drives the knock-out asymmetry (90.63 vs 5.20) — set size, direction norms, or genuine subsumption of AnnSel’s latents by FreqSel’s?
  • Can AnnSel be made robust by replacing keyword matching with embedding similarity or LLM-judged relevance — fixing the Korean and “Non-English characters” failures?
  • Why does ValSel backfire on Qwen3-4B English — transcoder quality, or the monolinguality score picking cross-lingual latents?
  • Do these interventions preserve task ability on non-templated text, and do they behave differently in genuine code-switching contexts?
