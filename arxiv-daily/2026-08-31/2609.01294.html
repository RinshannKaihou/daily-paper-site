When deep-research agents branch on K hypotheses instead of committing early, they beat Best-of-N while using 22–35% fewer tool calls.

Explore Before Committing: Hypothesis-Guided Search for Deep Research Agents

Ruochen Zhou, Zhengyu Chen, Luan Zhang, Siyang Gao, et al. — CityU HK · Meituan · Oxford 2026-09-01 6 min read

deep-research agentstest-time scaling hypothesis generationagentic searchSFT data selection

Key metric

+13.3

Qwen3.5-122B on BC-small: 46.7 → 60.0, above 5-way Best-of-N (58.3). Kimi-K25 +8.4 and DeepSeek-V3.2 +10.0 over Pass@1 on the same benchmark — gains replicate across all three backbones.

Why this matters

A working closed loop of hypothesis generation, parallel evidence gathering, and comparison — plus behavioral signals that make SFT data curation measurable.

The failure it targets is real and measured: ~78% of failed BrowseComp trajectories die from picking the wrong direction, not from bad tools or bad synthesis.

Critical edge

The baseline set is the weakest point: no compute-matched Best-of-N, tree search, or verifier comparison is ever run — and half the benchmark suite omits MV/BoN entirely.

01Methodology

  • Diagnosis before prescription: 300 problems × 5 ReAct rollouts per benchmark (BrowseComp, FRAMES, GAIA), each failure labeled at its earliest unrecoverable stage (Exploration / Tool-use / Synthesis / Length) by gpt-4.1 plus human experts. Query similarity across independent runs decays 0.53 → 0.16 by Round 3 on BrowseComp — divergence is intrinsic, so early one-path commitment is fragile.
  • Two behavior metrics: CIR — fraction of queries probing a concrete candidate; SSR — direction-switch rate. On BrowseComp, high-CIR + high-SSR trajectories succeed 88% of the time; grounding and movement must co-occur.
  • HypoSearch, three stages: a prompt-based detector routes direct states to single-path search and divergent states to branching; K natural-language hypotheses (soft hints, not claims) each spawn an isolated bounded branch collecting support, contradictions, verified constraints, and open issues; a comparative aggregation agent weighs cross-branch evidence and answers with rejection reasons — or refines hypotheses for another round. Comparison happens at the evidence level, not the answer level.
  • Evaluation: 4 benchmarks (BC-small, BrowseComp-zh, FutureX, ResearchRubrics) × 3 backbones (Kimi-K25, Qwen3.5-122B, DeepSeek-V3.2); baselines Pass@1, Majority Voting (N=5), Best-of-N (N=5); ablation over K ∈ {1..5}.
  • Pilot SFT study: rank 80K LongCat trajectories by Q = 0.8·CIR + 0.2·SSR, fine-tune Qwen3-32B on the top 10K, and compare against full-80K training on search and reasoning evals.

  • Tool-call cost: ~132.5 calls/question on Kimi-K25 vs Majority Voting’s ~205 (−35.4%); −29.1% (Qwen3.5-122B), −22.1% (DeepSeek-V3.2) — branches split the space instead of duplicating whole trajectories.
  • K-sensitivity: at K = 1–3 HypoSearch can fall below MV/BoN; the method only clearly wins at K = 5 — a narrow hypothesis set over-constrains the search.
  • Pilot SFT: top-10K-by-Q training matches full-80K on BC-small with 1/8 of the data; on AIME25, Full-80K collapses to 26.7 while Filtered-10K retains 43.3 (base model ≈ 70) — filtering mitigates but does not remove the reasoning tax.

+Strengths

  • Diagnosis before prescription: the failure taxonomy and Jaccard-divergence analysis give the method a data-driven motivation and cleanly explain why pre-commit allocation beats post-hoc verification on BrowseComp-style tasks.
  • CIR/SSR are a contribution in their own right — simple, model-agnostic, computable from query logs, and strongly predictive (88% vs 3% success spread); useful for evaluation, data curation, possibly reward shaping.
  • Evidence-level aggregation, not answer voting: branches preserve per-direction evidence and the aggregator outputs rejection reasons — more interpretable and auditable than majority votes.
  • Concrete efficiency argument: 22.1–35.4% fewer tool calls than MV while scoring higher, with the mechanism explained.
  • Consistency: gains hold across three very different backbones and four benchmarks, and the pilot connects test-time behavior back to training-data selection — an unusually complete loop.

−Limitations

  • Fixed branching configuration: K and per-branch budgets do not adapt to state uncertainty — ambiguous states may get redundant hypotheses while hard ones stay under-explored.
  • Fallible router, unquantified: the prompt-based divergent-state detector has no reported accuracy; misrouting silently changes the method.
  • Small-K instability: below K ≈ 4 it can underperform simple MV/BoN; the effective minimum budget is ~5 branches.
  • No empirical comparison with verifier-based or tree-search scaling, despite Table 1 arguing they are complementary.
  • gpt-4.1 is judge and annotator: the same model scores answers and defines CIR/failure labels; no judge-bias controls or inter-annotator agreement reported. BC-small is self-constructed — full BrowseComp is never evaluated.
  • Pilot scope: heuristic Q weights, one base model, SFT only; AIME25 still degrades severely (43.3 vs ~70 base).

?Questions for Further Exploration

  • Would a compute-matched Best-of-N (N = 9–12, matching HypoSearch’s total tool-call budget) close the accuracy gap on BC-small and BrowseComp-zh?
  • Can the prompt-based divergent-state detector be replaced with a learned or confidence-aware router — and what fraction of states does it currently misroute?
  • Does HypoSearch compose with post-hoc verification, and does CIR/SSR transfer as an RL reward rather than an SFT data filter?
  • GAIA failures are mostly downstream (tool-use/synthesis) — does HypoSearch help there, or waste compute, given ResearchRubrics gains are under +5?
  • Can K-sensitivity be removed by adaptive hypothesis merging/splitting — expanding K only when branch evidence conflicts remain after aggregation?
