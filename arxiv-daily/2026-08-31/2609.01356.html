arXiv:2609.01356 / Mechanistic interpretability · multilingual LLMs / 2026-08-31

Multilingual LLMs commit to target word order before surface language: syntax is a third, independently mechanized translation stage.

“Separating Syntax from Language: A Mechanistic Account of Translation in Multilingual LLMs”

M. Sonkin, T. Baeumel, D. Gurgurov, J. van Genabith et al. (Saarland University / DFKI) · Aug 31, 2026 activation patching LogitLens attention heads machine translation

Key metric

8 / 9

Model–dataset combinations follow the syntax → language → content layer ordering; only Llama 3 on SVO collapses the two stages (both at layer 14).

Why this matters

Core reference for multilingual mechanistic interpretability — and its staged-commitment framework informs how long-running agent systems form internal state.

Critical edge

No variance, confidence intervals, or significance tests anywhere — and the 27-sentence natural-data check flips Llama 3 to a collapsed strategy.

01 Methodology

  • AModels spanning the English-bias spectrum: mGPT 1.3B (balanced multilingual), Aya Expanse 8B (mix undisclosed), Llama 3 8B (English-dominant).
  • BThree minimal-sentence datasets, each isolating one word-order contrast: NP — adjective/noun (197 sentences, 8 languages), SVO/SOV (134, 5), modal–infinitive (31, 4). ~24K one-shot translation prompts; DeepL translation with manual word-order correction, built from 200 picture-nouns.
  • CBase/plant activation patching (pyvene, extended for Aya) crossing syntax S, language L, and content C in all 8 combinations; the headline metric Δmax is the maximal next-token probability shift and its layer.
  • DHead analysis inside each model’s S-switch layer (mGPT 11, Aya 15, Llama 3 14), scored by influence ratio R(h,t) = P(t | patch head h) / mean over heads.
  • ELanguage-independence tests: mean-activation patching from different-order vs. same-order languages (KL on part-of-speech vs. language contrasts), a cosine-similarity geometry check, and a 27-sentence FLORES-200 natural control.

02 Results

Syntax only (S)0.10 @ 110.09 @ 14
Syntax + language (S+L)0.52 @ 190.21 @ 16
Full (S+L+C)0.65 @ 310.55 @ 25

Strictly increasing layers mean syntax commits first. The ordering holds in 8 of 9 model–dataset combinations; the exception (Llama 3 on SVO) resolves S and L at the same layer 14.

  • Syntax-specific effects are real but modest: syntax-only Δmax ranges 0.00–0.10, yet consistently precedes the language switch — weakest in balanced-multilingual mGPT.
  • English intermediates already carry target word order (LogitLens). For German→French, Llama 3 and Aya prefer the English noun “apple” over the adjective, matching French noun-first “pomme rouge”; the inverse holds for French→German. mGPT shows no English intermediates at all.
  • Single-head localization: Llama 3 H14.25 and Aya H15.0 drive the word-order switch (R(h,t) > 1 for all plant tokens; patch effects R ≈ 3–5 vs < 2 for mGPT’s distributed heads, whose top head also shifts language).
  • Word order is encoded independently of language: KL divergence is high when base and patch languages differ in order and low when they share order — patching shifts part of speech without raising patch-language probability. Exception: the effect vanishes when German is the base target (Aya, Llama 3).
  • No geometric clustering: S-head activations do not cluster by word order across languages — functionally order-sensitive, but no shared word-order subspace.
  • Fragile control: on the 27 FLORES sentences mGPT and Aya keep S→L→C, but Llama 3 collapses to S,L→C.

03 Strengths

  • Elegant disentangling: base/plant prompts cross S, L, C in all 8 combinations; the same-order vs. different-order mean-activation control is a clean causal test most probing studies lack.
  • Four converging methods (LogitLens, layer patching, head patching, activation geometry) tell one consistent story.
  • Model selection spans the English-bias spectrum — the English-anchor claim is not an artifact of one English-centric model.
  • Honest, specific limitations; code and complete data recipes released; per-model layer indices (11/15/14) enable direct replication.

04 Limitations

  • Only three models, and only mGPT is verifiably non-English-dominant; Aya’s pretraining mix is undisclosed, so the “bias spectrum” is partly assumed.
  • Three word-order contrasts in template sentences only — case, agreement, and long-distance dependencies may recruit entirely different mechanisms.
  • Small data (197 / 134 / 31 sentences; 27 FLORES controls) and no statistical uncertainty on any reported number.
  • mGPT’s deviation (distributed heads, no English intermediates) is reported but unexplained; single-head localization is confined to Aya and Llama 3.

05 Questions for further exploration

  1. Does S→L→C staging survive on natural parallel text at scale, given the FLORES check already flipped Llama 3?
  2. Why does word-order steering vanish when German is the base target language in Aya and Llama 3 — relatedness, resources, or prompt format?
  3. Do morphology-bearing phenomena (case, agreement) recruit the same S-heads, and does ablating them change free-form translation quality?
  4. Is single-head localization an artifact of 8B-scale dense models — how do larger, sparse, or MoE multilingual models organize syntax?
  5. If S-heads are functional but not geometric word-order encoders, what representation do they pass downstream — and could it steer multilingual behavior better than token-level interventions?
