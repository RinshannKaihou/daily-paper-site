Key Finding · Multilingual Interpretability

On identical hidden states, representation and decoding probes systematically disagree — the “English pivot” inside LLMs is partly an artifact of probe choice.

Lingua Franca or Probing Artifact? Rethinking Latent Language in Multilingual LLMs · Deniz Bayazit, Badr AlKhamissi, Antoine Bosselut (EPFL) · 2026-08-31

Key Metric

Across 10 token-aligned checkpoints per family (OLMo-2, Apertus-8B), representation–decoding agreement on the dominant language stays low the entire way — near zero for Apertus-8B.
Why This Matters

Never report latent-language findings from a single probe family — probe disagreement, not any English lingua franca, is the stable phenomenon.

在同一组隐状态上系统比较不同探针对“内部语言”读数的分歧，是探针方法学与表征几何分析的直接参考。

Critical Edge

Purely observational — with no causal interventions, the paper documents probe disagreement but cannot say which probe, if either, tracks the computation actually used.

— Critical Assessment

Methodology

  • Reframe: latent language Z is a latent variable; from a hidden state h at a given layer and position, each estimator emits a diagnostic distribution q(ℓ) ≈ Pr(Z=ℓ|h) — explicitly not ground truth.
  • Four metrics: entropy (diffuseness), dominance (max-language probability), pivot rate (dominant ≠ task-relevant language), inter-probe agreement; a confident-pivot variant requires dominance ≥ 0.6.
  • Breadth: 12 decoder-only 7–9B models, up to 27 candidate languages, controlled copy/cloze/translation (16,146 translation prompts), open-ended PUD + INCLUDE QA (882 prompts, 10 languages × 3 domains), and 10 token-aligned pretraining checkpoints per family (~210B→4T tokens).
  • Compute & release: NVIDIA A100 80GB (1–6 h per run); public code (github.com/bayazitdeniz/latent-lid) with documented GMM and tuned-lens fitting details.

  • —The central disagreement: on open-ended INCLUDE prompts (50–75% layer window) the GMM probe assigns substantially more mass to the task-relevant language while raw-logit top-p decoding stays English-biased — the gap is largest for moderately multilingual models (Llama-3.1) and nearly closes for Apertus.
  • —Controlled translation (16,146 prompts): the English-pivot effect weakens as multilinguality increases — Llama-2-7B keeps high mass on English lexicalizations; Apertus-8B’s target-language probability rises earlier and dominates later layers.
  • —Robustness: domain and language-inventory changes (PUD9/21, UD6, 3 QA domains) shift estimates only modestly; layerwise trajectories are model-specific, not predictable from multilinguality alone.
  • —Instruction tuning is a non-event for LLID: base→Instruct shifts are small and not systematically toward English — far smaller than checkpoint-to-checkpoint differences.

Strengths

  • Reframes a heated debate (“do LLMs think in English?”) into a tractable measurement question, with estimator-agnostic metrics future probe papers can adopt directly.
  • Unusually broad scope for interpretability: 12 models, up to 27 languages, two regimes, three QA domains, and real pretraining checkpoints rather than only final models.
  • Token-aligned checkpoint comparison done right; the confident-pivot threshold guards against over-reading diffuse distributions.
  • Intellectual honesty: concedes there is no ground truth, only one representation estimator tested, and probe-specific choices may shift absolute values.
  • Actionable rule: never report LLID from a single probe family — the disagreement itself is the stable finding.

Limitations

  • One estimator carries the whole representation family: only the GMM (Shani & Basirat 2025) is tested — calibrated in the very languages being evaluated, so its task-language advantage may partly reflect in-domain calibration; cited subspace and neuron-level estimators are not compared.
  • Checkpoint study covers only balanced multilingual models (OLMo-2, Apertus) — the English-centric Llama-2 family where the original pivot claims originated is unexamined.
  • No causal interventions: documents disagreement but cannot determine which probe reflects the computation actually used.
  • Scale and scope: decoder-only 7–9B only; 27-language inventory excludes very low-resource languages, code, and mathematics; link to downstream cross-lingual transfer untested.
  • Numbers as trends: several headline comparisons are conveyed as figure trends without tabulated effect sizes — no single quotable number for the central claim.

The Instruments

  • GMM posterior (representation): fit per layer on ~100 prompts/language, PCA to 98% variance, diagonal covariance, uniform priors; calibration sets PUD9/PUD21 ± UD6, include_101lang.
  • Logit / tuned lens (decoding): hidden state after final norm projected through the unembedding; tuned lens fit on FineWeb2 over 27 languages, windows up to 2048 tokens.
  • Scoring: START(w) next-token probability mass (controlled) vs GlotLID-classified rollouts over a 5-position forward window (open-ended).
  • Metrics: entropy H, dominance D = max q(ℓ), pivot rate, agreement A — making probe choice part of any LLID claim.

Questions for Further Exploration

  1. Does representation–decoding disagreement causally predict downstream cross-lingual transfer failures — e.g., language mixing in long-form generation?
  2. Would language-subspace (Zhao et al. 2025) or neuron-level (Zeng et al. 2025) estimators agree with the GMM probe, or is the reported asymmetry GMM-specific?
  3. Can activation patching dissociate the two families: does editing the residual stream shift one probe’s reading while leaving the other — and the output behavior — unchanged?
  4. Is Apertus-8B’s sharp dominance transition at 20–40% of training attributable to an identifiable data-mixture or optimization event, and does an analog exist in English-centric models with private checkpoints?
  5. If calibration-set overlap drives part of the GMM’s task-language advantage, does fitting it on a disjoint corpus eliminate the asymmetry between probe families?
