> **一句话总结：** 清华大学与美团提出 CARE：在 rubric-based RL（GRPO）的每一步训练中，将当前最高分 rollout 与 GPT-4.1 离线生成的"锚点响应"对比，用 Adaptive 分支（检测被钻空子的 rubric 并改写/追加 [Resist] 硬约束，以否决式 veto reward 强制执行）和 Chase 分支（把 rollout 与锚点间最大的实质质量差距固化成更锐利的 [llm] rubric）在线维护"高奖励区"的判别精度；在自建 WildChecklist-9K 上，Qwen2.5-7B-Base 的 Arena-Hard-2.0 Vanilla 从静态 Rubric RL 的 2.0 提升到 10.7，InfoBench Overall 从 75.6 提升到 83.9，且是唯一在 300 步训练中对 GPT-4.1 锚点胜率持续上升的方法（最终 47%，三种子 +21.0 pp，配对 bootstrap 显著）。

> **TL;DR:** CARE grounds every rubric-evolution step of rubric-based GRPO in a per-instance anchor response generated offline by GPT-4.1 on WildChecklist-9K. An Adaptive branch detects when the top-scoring rollout hacks a satisfied rubric (contrastively, against the anchor) and repairs it by rewriting or appending a [Resist] constraint enforced with a veto reward (reward = 0 if any [Resist] rubric is violated); a Chase branch extracts the largest substantive quality gap between the top rollout and the anchor and turns it into a sharper [llm] rubric. On Qwen2.5-7B-Base, CARE lifts Arena-Hard-2.0 (Vanilla) from 2.0 (static Rubric RL) to 10.7 and InfoBench Overall from 75.6 to 83.9, nearly doubles FollowBench Style-HSR (48.0 → 94.7), and is the only method whose win-rate against GPT-4.1 anchors improves monotonically-ish over 300 steps (47% final single-run; +23.0 pp over Adaptive-only, 95% CI [15.0, 29.0]) — at a 41.1% per-step training-time overhead.

## 核心贡献

- **问题分解**：指出现有动态 rubric 方法的三种失败模式——(1) 无定向的 rubric 抽取（比较任意 rollout 对，捕捉的是偶然差异而非质量鸿沟）、(2) 盲目的漏洞检测（裁判只靠参数化知识，无具体未被打穿的参照物）、(3) 无界的 rubric 增殖（坏 rubric 只能追加不能改写，稀释验证精度、制造规则间冲突）。
- **锚点接地的在线 rubric 演化**：每步只用 GPT-4.1 离线生成、经 Gemini 3 Pro + GPT-5.4 双裁判一致通过 + 人工抽检（199/200 通过）的高质量锚点作为对比参照；把"盲比较"变成"有证据的精炼"。锚点是参照点而非质量上限。
- **两分支互补机制**：Adaptive 分支只在"已满足的 rubric"（未满足的 rubric 不可能被打穿）上检测漏洞，用 [Resist] 前缀约束 + 否决奖励（veto reward）使作弊收益归零；Chase 分支在无作弊时抽取 top rollout 与锚点间最大的实质差距（连贯性、信息深度、准确性、任务有效性），合并或新增原子化 [llm] rubric，且比较是双向的（差距可能偏向锚点也可能偏向 rollout）。
- **理论动机（附录 A）**：把 Chasing the Tail 的静态高奖励区论证推广到"rubric 本身在训练中演化"的动态设定：KL 正则化 Gibbs 更新展开后，前沿响应间的最终排序由"累计真实质量差"与"累计误设差"竞争决定——持续性高奖励区误设可逆转真实排序，因此判别精度必须在线维护，而非训练前一次性离线打磨。
- **WildChecklist-9K 数据集**：基于 WildChecklist（源自 WildChat 真人-GPT 对话）为 1 万候选实例生成 GPT-4.1 锚点，双前沿裁判一致接受率 93.8%，最终取一致通过池中的 9K 条；另留 300 条 hold-in 样本做 Win-rate 评测。
- **系统性验证**：3 个独立种子的重训、10,000 次配对 bootstrap 置信区间、独立 checkpoint 评测（对未参与训练的 Qwen3-8B-Thinking）、有无锚点的裁判-人工一致性审计，以及 +41.1% 每步开销的成本分析。

## Key Contributions

- **Diagnosis of three failure modes** in prior dynamic rubric methods: undirected extraction (arbitrary rollout-pair comparisons capture incidental differences), blind hack detection (the judge relies on parametric knowledge without a concrete, non-hacked reference), and unbounded rubric proliferation (flawed rubrics are only ever appended, degrading verifier accuracy and creating inter-rubric conflicts).
- **Anchor-grounded rubric evolution**: every evolution step contrasts the current top-scoring rollout against a per-instance GPT-4.1 anchor response (validated by dual frontier judges at 93.8% unanimous acceptance plus a 99.5%-pass human audit), turning blind heuristic comparison into evidence-based refinement.
- **Two complementary branches**: an Adaptive branch that scopes hack detection exclusively to satisfied rubrics (an unsatisfied rubric cannot be hacked) and repairs the exploited rubric via rewrite or a new [Resist] constraint backed by a veto reward; and a Chase branch that converts the single largest substantive gap between the top rollout and the anchor into a sharper atomic [llm] rubric, with bidirectional comparison (the gap may favor either side) so rubric discovery never becomes an imitation objective or a quality ceiling.
- **Dynamic-setting theory** (Appendix A): unrolling KL-regularized Gibbs updates shows the final frontier-response ranking is governed by cumulative true-quality signal vs. cumulative mis-specification; persistent high-reward-region distortion can overturn the true ordering, formally motivating *online* maintenance of high-reward-region discriminative accuracy.
- **WildChecklist-9K**: a 9K-instance training corpus of prompt + rubric set + validated anchor, built from WildChecklist (synthesized from real WildChat human-GPT interactions), with a 300-sample hold-in set for win-rate evaluation.

## Methodology

**Setup.** Rubric-based RL scores each rollout against a prompt-specific rubric set R via an LLM verifier: r(y, R) = (1/|R|) Σ 1[rubric r^k satisfied by y], trained with GRPO (G rollouts per prompt; here G = 16). Reward hacking occurs when r(y, R) ≈ r(y', R) but the true quality q(y) ≪ q(y') — the rubric set is the proxy reward model, and it is the rubric's *discriminative accuracy in the high-reward region* that determines whether over-optimization turns into hacking (Gao et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2026).

**Anchor generation (offline, one-time).** For each training instance (x_i, R_i), a frontier model (GPT-4.1) generates an anchor response y_i^a = M_anchor(x_i, R_i), explicitly conditioned on the prompt *and* its rubric set, with explicit automatic filtering and human validation. Anchors are cached; they serve as a reference point, not a quality upper bound — the Chase branch is explicitly allowed to find gaps where the rollout beats the anchor.

**Per-step CARE loop (Algorithm 1).** At each GRPO step: (1) generate G rollouts and compute rubric rewards; (2) select the top rollout y^top = argmax r(y_i^g, R_i) — the response currently receiving the strongest reward signal, hence both the most likely to be hacking and the most informative diagnostic; (3) partition rubrics into satisfied (R^sat) and unsatisfied (R^misat) by y^top — hack detection examines *only* R^sat, because a rubric the response fails cannot be hacked; (4) an LLM judge (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) receives (x_i, R_i^sat, R_i^misat, y^top, y_i^a) and decides, per satisfied rubric, whether y^top satisfies it in a substantively similar way as the anchor or exploits a loophole.
- **Adaptive branch (hack detected):** the judge either rewrites the exploited rubric r_i^c to close the loophole or appends a new atomic [Resist] constraint that specifically prohibits the observed hacking pattern. [Resist] rubrics are enforced by a veto reward r_vet(y, R) = r(y, R) · Π_{r∈R^Resist} s_r(y): since s_r(y) is binary, the product is zero if *any* [Resist] rubric is violated — making the hacking pattern categorically unprofitable, unlike weighted averaging which lets the policy compensate violations with high scores elsewhere.
- **Chase branch (no hack):** the judge identifies the single most significant substantive gap between y^top and y_i^a along dimensions such as logical coherence, information depth, accuracy, or task effectiveness, then merges it into an existing [llm] rubric or appends a new atomic [llm] rubric. The comparison is bidirectional, so the extracted dimension may capture an anchor advantage or a genuine rollout advantage.

**Training configuration.** Verl v0.4.0, GRPO on 16×A100; rollouts/prompt = 16, train batch = 96, KL coef 0.001, LR 1e-6 (constant + warmup), max seq 8192, 3 epochs over 9K examples (~94 steps/epoch, ~300 steps total). The same Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct serves as reward verifier and CARE judge. Both branches are implemented in a single shared rubric-evolution prompt (verbatim templates released in Appendix E); a step runs either Step 1 (patch) or Step 2 (gap extraction), never both.

**Data.** WildChecklist-9K: GPT-4.1 anchors generated for 10K candidates; Gemini 3 Pro and GPT-5.4 (high reasoning effort) independently judge rubric satisfaction, factual hallucination, and unsafe content — 93.8% both-accept / 6.0% disagree / 0.2% both-reject; the 9K set keeps only unanimously accepted instances; a stratified human audit of 200 anchors passes 199/200 (99.5%). A 300-sample hold-in set supports win-rate evaluation against GPT-4.1 anchor responses.

**Evaluation.** Arena-Hard-2.0 (Vanilla / Style-Controlled), InfoBench (Easy / Hard / Overall), FollowBench (SSR / HSR / CSL), plus Win-rate vs. GPT-4.1 anchors judged by Gemini 3 Pro with random-order sampling to mitigate position bias.

**Cost.** Per-step time grows from 318.2 s (Rubric RL) to 448.9 s (CARE) = 1.411× total / +41.1%; anchor generation is one-time and cached, and online comparison is only over the top rollout (adds B rather than B·G comparisons per step), so overhead ρ ≈ B·C_C / T_RL(B, G).

## Results & Highlights

- **Arena-Hard-2.0, Qwen2.5-7B-Base (Table 1):** CARE reaches 10.7 Vanilla / 11.8 Style-Controlled vs. 2.0 / 1.9 for static Rubric RL (+8.7 Vanilla), 3.2 for Online Rubrics, 1.1 for DPO (RLCF), and 1.3 for SFT-on-anchor. On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, CARE (10.8 / 12.2) more than doubles the next-best baseline (Online Rubrics 6.8 / 4.3; Rubric RL 5.3 / 3.9).
- **InfoBench:** Qwen2.5-7B-Base Overall: CARE 83.9 vs. Rubric RL 75.6 (+8.3) and SFT 83.1; Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct Overall: CARE 86.7 vs. Rubric RL 83.3 (+3.4) and Online Rubrics 80.4. FollowBench: Base CSL 3.15 vs. 2.65 (Rubric RL) and 1.20 (untuned); Instruct SSR 85.9 vs. 84.1, HSR 78.4 vs. 75.6.
- **FollowBench HSR by constraint type (Table 2, Base):** CARE's average HSR is 68.5 vs. 61.8 (Rubric RL), 46.9 (untuned Base), and 83.7 (GPT-4); the gain concentrates on implicit constraints — Style nearly doubles 48.0 → 94.7 and Situation rises 55.5 → 73.6 (+18.1), while Format goes 46.7 → 60.8 and Content only 37.0 → 44.8.
- **Sustained anti-hacking (Win-rate, Base, 300 steps):** CARE is the *only* method with sustained win-rate improvement against GPT-4.1 anchors, reaching 47% at the final checkpoint (approaching parity). Rubric RL peaks around step 100 then deteriorates (classic over-optimization); Rubric RL w/ Universal Criteria declines after ~step 220; Chase-only peaks ~step 200 then falls; Adaptive variants plateau at a lower ceiling. Three-seed retraining: 24.0% → 45.0% win-rate (+21.0 pp in every seed, cross-seed SD 0.0); paired bootstrap (10,000 replicates): CARE_300 − Adaptive_300 = +23.0 pp [15.0, 29.0], CARE_300 − CARE_100 = +21.0 pp [14.0, 28.0] — all CIs exclude zero.
- **Anchor ablation is the sharpest result:** Adaptive Rubric RL beats Adaptive w/o anchor everywhere (Arena-Hard Vanilla 5.3 vs. 4.4; InfoBench Overall 82.9 vs. 77.4; FollowBench CSL 2.91 vs. 2.57), and anchor-free hack detection is *worse than static rubrics* on two benchmarks (Rubric RL 81.2 InfoBench Overall and 2.65 CSL vs. 77.4 and 2.57). The audit explains why: frontier-judge agreement with Adaptive hack-detection decisions is 95.2% with anchors vs. 65.3% without; human agreement is 99.1% vs. 67.1%.
- **Veto reward matters:** Adaptive w/o veto (weighted averaging of [Resist] rubrics) underperforms full Adaptive across all benchmarks and has a lower win-rate trajectory, because weighted averaging lets the policy buy back [Resist] violations with high scores on other rubrics.
- **Generalization:** Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct: Arena-Hard Vanilla 8.9 vs. 2.6 (Rubric RL), InfoBench-Hard 86.7 — exceeding GPT-4.1's own 86.4; Qwen3-8B: 13.1 vs. 10.9, InfoBench Overall 84.0 vs. 80.0. Independent checkpoints of the Base run evaluated against Qwen3-8B-Thinking (not used in anchor construction or training) improve from 16.0% → 60.0% win-rate and 74.8 → 84.0 InfoBench Overall over steps 0→300, decoupling progress from evaluation against the training anchors.
- **Cost:** +41.1% per-step training time (318.2 s → 448.9 s) on Qwen2.5-7B-Base, with a one-time cached anchor-generation cost.

## Critical Assessment

- **Single-run headline benchmarks.** The three-benchmark Table 1 results are from one run each (the paper says so in §4.2); the 3-seed/bootstrap rigor applies only to the win-rate trajectory, not to Arena-Hard-2.0/InfoBench/FollowBench numbers. Given the win-rate curves show real inter-method variance and periodic fluctuations around data passes (94 steps), single-run benchmark deltas like +8.7 Arena-Hard Vanilla deserve the same seed treatment.
- **Partial circularity in the win-rate headline.** The 47% "approaching parity" figure is measured against the very GPT-4.1 anchor responses the policy was trained to chase, on the training rubric distribution (hold-in). The independent Qwen3-8B-Thinking checkpoint evaluation and the SFT-on-anchor comparison (SFT yields smaller, less consistent gains, even degrading on Arena-Hard-2.0) mitigate this well, but a held-out *prompt-distribution* shift test (e.g., anchors regenerated on a different corpus) is missing.
- **Judge–policy family coupling.** The reward verifier and CARE judge are both Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct while the trained policies are Qwen2.5-7B — a same-family judge whose biases toward Qwen-style outputs are never quantified. The win-rate judge (Gemini 3 Pro) is cross-family, but the *training-time* reward pipeline bias is not audited; swapping in a non-Qwen judge would be a cheap, decisive ablation.
- **Unmeasured rubric proliferation.** The paper criticizes append-only proliferation, and the evolution prompt has MECE/no-overlap rules, but no quantitative trajectory of rubric-set size over 300 steps is reported — the central claim of *bounded* evolution is enforced only by prompt instructions, and the Appendix B taxonomy organizes rubrics rather than counting them.
- **Bidirectional Chase is asserted, not measured.** All three case studies (word-sense ambiguity, language substitution, missing specific detail) show anchor-advantage gaps; there is no statistic on how often the Chase branch extracted a gap favoring the rollout, which is the load-bearing assumption for "not an imitation objective."
- **Frontier-teacher dependence and cost.** The method requires GPT-4.1 anchor generation plus dual frontier-judge validation plus a 72B judge at train time, and pays +41.1% per-step overhead with evolution firing at *every* step (the authors' own limitation #1). Reproduction without frontier API access is nontrivial, and the anchor-quality audit (99.5% pass) validates anchors against the given rubrics — it cannot catch rubrics themselves being poor, an issue the paper concedes is open (no principled rubric-quality metric; it leans on the high-reward-region discriminative-accuracy intuition from Chasing the Tail).
- **Benchmark scope.** All three evaluations are instruction-following/checklist-style suites, matching the WildChat-derived training distribution; nothing is shown for other open-ended regimes (multi-turn, coding, long-form creative writing beyond FollowBench, non-English), so "open-ended post-training" breadth is narrower than the framing suggests.

## Strengths

- The three failure modes of prior dynamic rubric methods are crisply articulated and each maps to a concrete design decision (anchor grounding → evidence-based comparison; satisfied-only detection scope → fewer false hack flags; rewrite-or-merge policy → bounded rubric growth).
- The satisfied-only detection scope ("a rubric that is not satisfied cannot be hacked") is a simple but genuinely clever constraint that shrinks the judge's job and is validated by the 95.2% vs. 65.3% agreement audit.
- The veto reward turns soft [Resist] preferences into hard constraints; the Adaptive w/o veto ablation shows weighted averaging measurably leaks (lower win-rate trajectory, worse benchmarks).
- Unusually strong experimental hygiene for this area: 3-seed retraining with near-zero variance, paired-bootstrap CIs that exclude zero, an independent-checkpoint evaluation against a model outside the training loop, human + dual-frontier-judge audits of both anchors and hack decisions, and an explicit overhead/cost analysis.
- Honest self-assessment: the limitations section names the every-step activation cost and the missing rubric-quality metric; the ethics section warns the mechanism could over-reward stylistic conformity and is not deployment-ready.
- Verbatim prompt templates and full hyperparameters are released, making the pipeline unusually reproducible for an LLM-judge paper.

## Limitations

- Rubric evolution runs at every training step (+41.1% overhead); selective/gated activation is proposed but not implemented or tested.
- No principled metric for generated-rubric quality — success is inferred indirectly from downstream benchmark and win-rate behavior (the authors flag this as an open problem, pointing to RIFT's rubric failure-mode taxonomy as a start).
- Depends on a frontier teacher (GPT-4.1) for anchors and a large 72B judge at training time; unclear how performance degrades with weaker anchors or judges.
- Main benchmark table is single-run per configuration; only the win-rate trajectory has multi-seed and CI support.
- Tested only at 7–8B scale on Qwen2.5/Llama-3.1/Qwen3 backbones with instruction-following benchmarks; scaling behavior and out-of-distribution open-ended tasks are unexplored.
- The Appendix A theory is idealized (per-step KL-regularized Gibbs updates) and explicitly mechanistic rather than an end-to-end guarantee that CARE's updates reduce frontier distortion.

## Relevance

与你最高优先级的"自演化智能体与自动化研究"方向直接相关：CARE 本质上是一套自动化奖励/rubric 设计机制——在长程 RL 自举过程中在线检测奖励漏洞、自动改写评分标准并用硬约束封死，属于"automated reward design + 长时间自优化系统的可靠性保障"的交集。其对奖励过优化的动态监测（Win-rate 曲线在数据回合附近的波动、各方法在 300 步内的平台/衰减形态）也与你关注的训练稳定性与可观测性议题呼应。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- How much anchor quality is actually needed? Would self-generated anchors (e.g., from a 72B teacher or the policy's own best-of-N) or a weaker open-weight teacher preserve the 95.2% hack-detection agreement, or is GPT-4.1-level quality load-bearing?
- Can rubric evolution be gated to high-risk steps (the authors' proposed selective activation)? What trigger signal works — rollout reward variance, judge confidence, or drift in satisfied-rubric rates?
- What is the actual rubric-set size trajectory over training? Does the MECE/no-overlap prompt rule measurably bound proliferation, and how does set growth interact with the 41.1% overhead?
- Would a cross-family judge (e.g., Gemini or GPT as the training-time verifier) eliminate the Qwen-family coupling, and how much of the gain survives?
- Does the [Resist]/veto mechanism transfer to verifiable-reward settings where hard constraints already exist programmatically, or is it only meaningful for LLM-judged rubrics?
- Beyond 3 epochs, does CARE's win-rate eventually plateau like Chase-only (step ~200), and can Adaptive-style repair sustain the Chase branch's frontier sharpening indefinitely?
