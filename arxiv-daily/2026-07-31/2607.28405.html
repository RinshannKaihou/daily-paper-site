Same 0.2pp-scale gap holds on LIBERO (97.4 vs 97.6%) and on the second WAM, LingBot-VA (0.5-0.7pp). Targeted video/action blocks also drop to ~29% of FP16 memory with a 1.4-1.6× block-level speedup on Blackwell NVFP4 kernels.

Why This Matters

Gives roboticists deploying world-action models at the edge a quantization method that keeps closed-loop task success within about 1 point of FP16 at 4-bit precision.

▲ Critical Edge

The real-robot FP16-vs-QuantWAMs gap (6.6pp: 63.3% vs 56.7%) is far larger than any simulation gap (0.2-0.7pp), suggesting the calibration's near-lossless behavior may not fully transfer to real hardware and sensor noise.

Methodology

1

Shared-basis outlier calibration — pool per-channel activation-outlier statistics only across "coordinate-admissible" modules: those sharing a literal quantizer-input coordinate system after a common Hadamard rotation (e.g. the shared value pathway in Fast-WAM, not query/key, which stay architecturally private per stream). A closed-form crossover rule (Proposition 1) decides when to pool: do it while calibration trajectory count N is below N*=σ²/τ² (cross-member heterogeneity small relative to sampling noise), else calibrate independently.

2

Co-training-objective saliency — score each Linear layer's precision-upgrade value from the empirical Fisher of the actual joint video+action loss (one backward pass, 32 calibration trajectories), retaining a cross-stream gradient term that separately-computed video/action Fisher scores would discard on "post-hoc fusion." Because scores are unstable at this sample size, mixed-precision is allocated at layer granularity (top 20% of candidate Linears), not per-element.

3

Fixed-intervention real-rollout auditing — re-audit which denoising steps deserve high-precision protection by replaying 32 real FP16 closed-loop rollout states through the same unprotected intervention used at profiling time, avoiding self-masking (already-protected steps look falsely safe) and synthetic/open-loop distribution mismatch. This repairs — rather than re-budgets — the protected-step schedule, and is validated once against a held-out split before being frozen.

Scope: all three sit atop a standard mixed-precision, channel-offloading PTQ backbone (à la Atom) targeting only the video-DiT and action-DiT blocks at W4A4; embeddings, projections, and the VAE stay untouched. Evaluated with a strict 4-way trajectory-disjoint split (calibration / profiling / validation / test) across 3 protocol seeds.

Each component adds a nontrivial, monotonic gain under a matched precision budget.

  • Real-robot (AgiBot G2, 3 tasks × 10 trials): FP16 63.3% (19/30) vs. QuantWAMs 56.7% (17/30) vs. Atom* 40.0% (12/30) — QuantWAMs still clearly beats the baseline, at a matched 1.4× block speedup.
  • Saliency-source ablation: Joint saliency beats post-hoc fusion by 3.6pp on Fast-WAM (95.0% vs 91.4%) and 5.2pp on LingBot-VA (98.0% vs 92.8%) at the same precision budget.
  • Allocation-granularity ablation: element-level precision allocation collapses to 72.1%/69.8%, far below the paper's layer-level allocation (95.0%/98.0%) — finite calibration data can't support element-level decisions.

Strengths

  • Clear, specific diagnosis of why generic PTQ heuristics fail for WAMs (structural/distributional/objective mismatch), not a vague "existing methods aren't good enough" motivation.
  • Theoretically grounded pooling-crossover criterion (Proposition 1), empirically cross-checked via bootstrap resampling before being tested for downstream task success — a rare two-step validation.
  • Thorough matched-budget cumulative ablation ladder plus independent axis ablations isolating grouping, allocation-granularity, saliency-source, and schedule-selection choices.
  • Evaluated across two architecturally distinct WAMs, two simulation benchmarks, and a real dual-arm robot, with disjoint calibration/profiling/validation/test splits and multi-seed protocol.
  • Unusually transparent about what its comparisons do and do not establish (non-equivalence claims, non-causal ablations, block-level-only efficiency metrics).

Limitations

  • Not training-free: requires labels, one backward pass, FP16 rollouts, and schedule validation — the authors themselves say it "optimizes local surrogates rather than closed-loop performance."
  • Limited to two WAM implementations, in-distribution calibration, and a single Blackwell hardware backend; transfer to unseen tasks or other accelerators is untested.
  • Reported speedup/memory gains are block-level (video+action DiT only), explicitly not end-to-end control-loop metrics.
  • Real-robot study (30 trials total) is explicitly underpowered for a rigorous FP16 comparison, and shows a larger relative degradation (6.6pp) than any simulation benchmark.
  • Causal attribution for the Joint saliency objective's gain over post-hoc fusion — its central weight-precision claim — is explicitly not established by the ablation alone.

Questions for Further Exploration

  1. How much of the gain over Atom*/SVDQuant* would remain if those baselines also got the co-training gradient and FP16 rollout replay signals, not just matched nominal precision budgets?
  2. Would the coordinate-admissibility grouping rule generalize to WAMs with additional modalities (language, depth, tactile) beyond video-action coupling?
  3. What is the method's sensitivity to calibration budget (fixed at 32 trajectories throughout), and how does performance degrade as it shrinks?
  4. Can the framework be validated on non-Blackwell/non-NVFP4 hardware to separate algorithm-level gains from kernel-specific speedups?
  5. What drives the larger real-world (6.6pp) vs. simulation (0.2-0.7pp) gap — sensor/actuation noise, sim-to-real shift in calibration rollouts, or something AgiBot G2-specific?
  6. How does the fixed per-benchmark denoising-step protection schedule generalize to genuinely out-of-distribution tasks or objects not seen during calibration and profiling?
