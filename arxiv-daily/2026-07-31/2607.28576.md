> **一句话总结：** 在 GSM8K 和 MATH-500 上，对 Qwen2.5 的 1.5B/3B/7B 模型做严格的等token成本配对统计检验后发现：Self-Refine、Reflexion、Best-of-N自我验证、多智能体辩论这些"反思型"方法没有一个能在相同token开销下显著超过最简单的重复采样+多数投票（self-consistency）；凡是让模型评判/改写自己输出的方法在全部18次比较中都显著更差，其中Best-of-N在Qwen2.5-3B/MATH-500上比直接对同一批样本计数还低17.3个百分点(p<0.001)。

> **TL;DR:** A rigorous, statistically-powered replication of Wang et al.'s "budget-aware" claim finds that across 36 paired, cost-matched comparisons (7 methods × 2 models × 2 math benchmarks, extended down to 1.5B and 3B), zero methods significantly beat self-consistency at equal token cost, while 10 are significantly worse — all of them methods where the model judges or rewrites its own output (Self-Refine, forced Reflexion, Best-of-N self-verify). The cleanest result: holding the exact same 8 sampled solutions fixed and only changing whether the model *picks* the best one or the pipeline simply *counts* the majority answer, counting wins by 5–17.3 percentage points below 7B (e.g., 52.0% vs 69.3% on Qwen2.5-3B/MATH-500, Δ=-17.3pp, p<0.001), and the gap only closes to statistical noise at 7B.

## 核心贡献

- 用"设计实验"的方式重新检验 Wang et al. (2024) 提出的"等预算下简单采样常胜"这一未经统计检验的论断：7种方法 × 2个数学基准（GSM8K、MATH-500，各150题）× Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B（Best-of-N额外测7B），全部按方法自身实测的token成本与self-consistency曲线插值比较，配对bootstrap置信区间 + Holm–Bonferroni多重检验校正。
- 提出并验证一个"无混淆"的关键实验：Best-of-N先采样8个解，让模型自己选一个最好的；把同样这8个样本简单做多数投票计数。样本、token、模型完全相同，唯一变量是"选择规则"，结果是"计数"稳定优于"模型自选"，且差距随模型增大而收窄，在7B时不再可与零区分。
- 将36次比较按方法对额外token"做了什么"分组：凡是让模型自我评估/改写输出的方法（Self-Refine、强制版Reflexion、Best-of-N自选）在全部18次比较中都劣于等成本基线；不含自我评估的方法（CoT、Plan-and-Solve、多智能体辩论等）则与基线在统计上无法区分。
- 发现并纠正了自己实验中的两个会系统性偏袒方法的bug：(1) 服务器上下文窗口限制导致Best-of-N在MATH-500上因验证prompt过长而截断失败，恰好淘汰更难的题目，造成~20个百分点的偏差；(2) 分析代码曾把Best-of-N错误按"多数投票"打分而非按"验证者实际选择"打分，导致Best-of-N看起来持平于基线，修正后变为在全部设置下显著劣于基线。
- 记录 Reflexion 原版实现的一个"静默失效"现象：在1.5B模型上，Reflexion自我判断"正确"的比例是100%，导致反思-重试机制从未触发一次，实际测到的只是一次链式思维（但仍冠以Reflexion之名并表现"良好"），说明自触发控制流可以在不发出任何信号的情况下停止工作。

## Key Contributions

- Re-runs the budget-matched reasoning-strategy comparison as a properly powered, paired, statistically-corrected experiment (paired bootstrap CIs with 10,000 resamples, Holm–Bonferroni correction across 7 methods per setting), extending coverage down to 1.5B and 3B parameters, a regime the original study (Wang et al., 7B+ only) never tested.
- Introduces an efficient way to get the *entire* self-consistency accuracy-vs-cost curve from a single pool of K=16 sampled chains per question (subsampling N≤16 without replacement, 200 draws per N), making per-method cost-matched comparison affordable and giving each method its own precisely interpolated baseline point rather than a shared, arbitrary budget cap.
- Isolates the "does self-evaluation help" question from confounds entirely via a same-samples Best-of-N vs. majority-counting comparison: identical 8 candidate solutions, only the aggregation rule (model picks vs. tally) differs.
- Documents a diagnostic for verifier quality as models scale: the model's self-chosen answer disagrees with the majority answer on a shrinking fraction of questions as scale increases (30→17→8 of 150 on GSM8K at 1.5B/3B/7B), and even where it disagrees it is usually wrong, so the observed "closing gap" is convergence toward voting, not the verifier becoming a better judge.
- Full open release of harness, prompts, and all 19,200 raw generations, explicitly so grading and answer extraction can be independently re-audited rather than trusted — and the paper itself demonstrates why this matters by reporting two scoring bugs it caught in its own pipeline via cross-checking.

## Methodology

The paper studies mathematical reasoning with automatically-checkable answers (GSM8K, 150 sampled questions; MATH-500, 150 sampled questions) using two open, instruction-tuned models from the Qwen2.5 family (1.5B and 3B, plus 7B for one comparison), run at Q8_0 (8-bit) quantization via llama.cpp on CPU (single AMD EPYC 7452, no GPU) — chosen specifically to reach a smaller-model regime the predecessor study never tested.

Seven methods are implemented faithfully from their original papers, with one shared restriction: no method is ever shown the ground-truth answer (avoiding the leak Huang et al. identified, where self-correction "gains" come from using the correct answer to decide when to stop). The methods: Chain-of-Thought (1 call), Plan-and-Solve (1 call), Self-Refine (R=3 critique/revise rounds, 7 calls), Reflexion as published (self-judges correctness and only retries on "incorrect," 1–10 calls), a **forced** Reflexion variant that always runs all 3 reflect-and-retry rounds regardless of self-judgment (7 calls, explicitly labeled as the authors' own addition, not Shinn et al.'s), Best-of-N + self-verify (N=8 samples, model picks the best, 9 calls), and Multi-Agent Debate (3 agents, 2 rounds, 9 calls). All are compared against a **sampling baseline** (self-consistency / majority vote), the plainest possible way to spend extra tokens: draw N independent chains at non-zero temperature, take the modal answer.

Cost is measured strictly as generated (completion) tokens, summed across every model call a method makes per question, including critique/reflection/debate/verification tokens that never appear in the final answer — deliberately the measure on which the sequential, chatty methods look *best*, making the comparison conservative against the paper's own conclusion. Self-consistency's full accuracy-vs-cost curve is built cheaply by drawing a pool of K=16 independent chains once per question, then repeatedly subsampling N∈{1,2,3,4,6,8,12,16} chains without replacement (200 draws per N) — this is not an approximation, since drawing N from the K-pool is distributed exactly as running self-consistency at N would be. Each method's measured cost is then located on this curve by linear interpolation, and the paired difference in accuracy (method vs. interpolated baseline, matched on the same 150 questions) is reported with a 95% paired bootstrap CI (10,000 resamples) and a two-sided p-value, Holm–Bonferroni corrected across the 7 methods tested within each of the 4 (model, dataset) settings. A deterministic, brace-matching parser extracts \boxed{...} answers (validated against 1,819 synthetic gold-answer responses under 6 cosmetic LaTeX variations, 100% correct extraction).

Two important experimental-design notes the paper is explicit about: (1) the confound-free Best-of-N-vs-counting comparison reuses the *same* 8 sampled solutions and changes only the selection rule (model-pick vs. tally), removing any possible objection about token or sample differences; (2) Reflexion's cost is not fixed in advance — it is an outcome of how often the model's self-judgment fires — so the paper also runs a "forced" variant with a fixed budget to test the reflection mechanism itself independent of the judging prompt.

## Results & Highlights

- **Headline tally**: Across all 36 method-vs-baseline comparisons (7 methods × up to 2 models × 2 datasets, minus a few not run at every scale), after Holm correction **0 are significantly better** than self-consistency at matched token cost, **10 are significantly worse**, and 26 are statistically indistinguishable. All 10 significant losses come from methods that ask the model to inspect or rewrite its own output (Self-Refine, forced Reflexion, Best-of-N self-verify) — these three methods lose in **18 of 18** of their comparisons.
- **Best-of-N vs. counting on identical samples** (the paper's sharpest, confound-free result): on Qwen2.5-3B/MATH-500, model-chosen accuracy is **52.0%** vs. **69.3%** for simply counting the same 8 samples, Δ = **-17.3 pp**, 95% CI [-24.0, -11.3], p<0.001 — the largest and most significant gap the paper measures. Across all 6 model/dataset settings this gap ranges from -1.3pp to -17.3pp, with the verifier agreeing with the majority answer on only 63–95% of questions.
- **Best-of-N vs. self-consistency at equal cost**, Qwen2.5-1.5B/MATH-500: Best-of-N accuracy **46.7%** vs. self-consistency (interpolated at matched cost) **54.8%**, Δ = **-8.1 pp**, 95% CI [-14.2, -2.1], p=0.041.
- **Reflexion (forced) vs. self-consistency**, Qwen2.5-7B/MATH-500 — i.e., the reflect-and-retry mechanism remains harmful even at the largest scale tested: Reflexion(forced) **63.3%** vs. self-consistency **73.5%**, Δ = **-10.1 pp**, 95% CI [-15.0, -5.7], p<0.001.
- **Self-Refine vs. self-consistency**, Qwen2.5-7B/MATH-500: Self-Refine **66.7%** vs. self-consistency **73.0%**, Δ = **-6.3 pp**, 95% CI [-11.1, -2.0], p=0.018 — showing Self-Refine's penalty does not shrink to zero with scale the way Best-of-N's does.
- **Scale closes the Best-of-N gap but doesn't reverse it**: the judge-vs-count penalty is significant in 4/4 settings below 7B but 0/2 at 7B (Qwen2.5-7B/GSM8K: -2.0pp, CI [-5.3,+0.7]; Qwen2.5-7B/MATH-500: -1.3pp, CI [-4.7,+2.0]), and disagreement-rate/override-accuracy diagnostics show this is convergence toward the tally (the model increasingly just agrees with the majority), not the verifier becoming a better judge — consistent with prior work (Zhang et al.) that a *trained* verifier does beat majority voting, implying training rather than judging is the useful ingredient.
- **Self-consistency baseline scaling curves** (illustrating how much of a "gain" is just more tokens): Qwen2.5-1.5B/GSM8K rises from 70.1% at N=1 (297 tokens) to 80.9% at N=16 (4,749 tokens); Qwen2.5-3B/MATH-500 rises from 55.7% (N=1) to 69.3% (N=16, 8,803 tokens).
- **Reflexion silently collapses on the small model**: on both Qwen2.5-1.5B benchmarks, the self-judgment step returned "correct" on 100% of questions, so the reflect-and-retry loop fired zero times and Reflexion-as-published measured nothing but a single chain of thought wearing the method's name — it scored competitively only because it had effectively become the cheapest method.
- Robustness checks (Monte Carlo re-seeding, subsetting to fully-answered questions) generally confirm the direction of the finding but flag that a few individual per-method verdicts (e.g., Best-of-N at 1.5B/MATH-500, Plan-and-Solve at 3B/MATH-500) flip significance under the parseable-subset analysis — the paper reports this candidly rather than picking the favorable version.

## Authors & Credibility

Single-author work by Iliya Mirzaei, Department of Computer Science, Stony Brook University. This is explicitly framed as a "replication with extensions, not a new phenomenon" of Wang et al. (arXiv:2406.06461, "Reasoning in Token Economies"). The paper demonstrates unusually strong methodological self-scrutiny: it validates its own answer-extraction parser against synthetic gold answers under six cosmetic variations, includes a full appendix (A.4–A.6) documenting two bugs the author found in their *own* pipeline (a context-window truncation that biased an early Best-of-N run toward easier questions, and a scoring bug that silently graded Best-of-N as majority voting instead of the verifier's actual choice) and how each was caught and corrected, and includes an independent re-derivation script that recomputes every reported number from raw records using separate aggregation code (96/96 checks agree). No institutional funding or external verifier training is claimed; the work runs entirely on a single CPU machine (no GPU), which is also disclosed as a scale limitation. There is no venue/peer-review status indicated (posted July 31, 2026, arXiv:2607.28576).

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty**: Modest and self-acknowledged — the paper does not claim a new phenomenon, only that it tests an existing, widely-cited but statistically unsupported claim (Wang et al.'s "self-consistency frequently wins" based on point estimates from 100 questions/dataset with no CIs, no significance tests). The genuinely novel piece is the confound-free Best-of-N-vs-counting design (same samples, only the aggregation rule varies) and the small-model extension (1.5B, 3B) that the predecessor study never covered.

**Rigor**: This is the paper's strongest dimension. Every headline number carries a paired bootstrap 95% CI and a Holm-corrected p-value; the paper explicitly computes what its own sample size (150 questions, 28 comparisons) can and cannot detect (~5-6pp minimum detectable effect) rather than letting readers assume otherwise; it separately quantifies Monte Carlo subsampling noise (≤0.36pp) and question-vs-run-to-run variance as distinct, labeled sources of uncertainty; and it reports two internal bugs it found and fixed, with before/after numbers, rather than silently correcting and moving on. A genuine weak point flagged by the author, not hidden: the robustness check restricting to questions every method could parse (excluding token-capped-out failures) reverses significance for at least one method in three of four re-checked settings (e.g., Plan-and-Solve at 3B/MATH-500 flips from p=0.279 non-significant to p=0.018 significant), meaning some individual per-cell verdicts are more fragile than the headline pattern.

**Reproducibility**: Very high — full harness, exact prompts (Appendix A.2), deterministic SHA-256-derived seeding scheme, and all 19,200 raw generations are released, explicitly so a skeptical reader can regrade the answers rather than trust the paper's parser.

**Significance**: Bounded and honestly scoped. The authors explicitly state the claim does not extend to frontier-scale models (max tested is 7B, and only for the cheapest comparison), does not extend to open-ended tasks (majority voting requires comparable answers, which math provides and prose does not — the authors note this actually biases the domain *toward* self-consistency, since it's the one setting where the competing baseline can even be computed), and does not rule out modest real per-method benefits that 150 questions/comparison cannot statistically resolve. This is a much narrower and more defensible claim than "reflection doesn't work," which the paper is careful never to assert.

**Red flags / weak points specific to this paper**: (1) The comparison is entirely math-only with exact-match grading, which the authors themselves note is the domain where the sampling baseline (majority voting) has its strongest possible competitor available — the result may simply not transfer to the open-ended generation tasks Self-Refine was originally designed for, and the paper says so directly rather than overclaiming. (2) Only one prompt/implementation per method was tested (Appendix A.2), and Reflexion's near-total self-judgment failure at 1.5B is shown to be a property of the specific judging prompt used, not a theorem about the method — a differently worded judge prompt could change this specific finding. (3) The three methods that involve randomness (baseline, Best-of-N, debate) were run once, so seed-level run-to-run variance is not captured by the question-level bootstrap, which the paper flags as biasing its significant findings toward *overstating* significance (a rare case of a paper naming a way its own reported effects could be too strong, not too weak).

## Strengths

- Exceptionally transparent and self-auditing: documents two of its own scoring bugs in detail, including exact before/after numbers, rather than quietly fixing and re-reporting.
- Statistical treatment (paired bootstrap, Holm correction, explicit minimum-detectable-effect calculation) is well above the norm for LLM reasoning-strategy papers and directly addresses a real, named gap in prior work (Wang et al.'s point estimates with no CIs).
- The Best-of-N-vs-majority-counting design is genuinely clever: it isolates the causal claim (is self-evaluation harmful?) from every other confound by holding the samples themselves fixed.
- Full data/code/generation release enables independent verification, which the paper itself demonstrates the value of by finding its own bugs this way.
- Candid about limitations throughout (Section 6.5 is unusually thorough), including several places where the paper's own robustness checks partially undercut its point estimates.

## Limitations

- Model scale capped at 7B (and only for the single cheapest comparison, Best-of-N); the paper explicitly cannot say what happens at frontier scale, where larger/better-trained models might make self-evaluation genuinely competitive or superior.
- 8-bit quantization throughout; the paper cannot rule out quantization interacting differently across methods.
- Task domain restricted to math with automatically checkable, single-value answers — a domain the authors argue is structurally favorable to majority-vote counting and may not transfer to open-ended generation, summarization, or dialogue tasks where Self-Refine-style critique was originally proposed and evaluated.
- One fixed implementation and one fixed hyperparameter configuration per method (not tuned per dataset), and prompts are sensitive to wording — a different but equally "faithful" implementation could behave differently.
- Uses generated (output) tokens only as the cost measure; the paper acknowledges that counting input tokens would make Best-of-N and debate look considerably worse still (since verification/debate re-reads long contexts), and that a latency measure under parallel hardware would penalize the strictly sequential methods (Self-Refine, Reflexion) even more — so the paper's chosen cost measure is, by its own admission, the one under which the tested methods look best.
- Confidence intervals from only 150 paired questions can reliably detect differences of roughly 5–6 percentage points or larger; smaller true effects for any individual method cannot be distinguished from zero with this sample size.

## Relevance

这篇论文对做LLM推理策略/test-time compute评测方法论的研究非常相关：它提供了一套可直接复用的"等成本统计检验"框架（配对bootstrap + Holm校正 + 池化子采样构造基线曲线），并给出了在小模型（1.5B-7B）上Self-Refine/Reflexion/Best-of-N等自我修正类方法系统性跑输简单重复采样的具体证据，可作为设计新推理策略实验或审视现有方法评测有效性时的参考基准和统计门槛。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- Does the Best-of-N judge-vs-count gap actually reverse at frontier scale (e.g., 30B+ or GPT/Claude-class models), as the paper's own extrapolation from the closing 1.5B→3B→7B trend might predict, or does it plateau at parity as observed here?
- Would a differently structured verifier prompt (e.g., scoring each candidate independently rather than choosing among all eight at once, as the paper itself suggests) close the Best-of-N gap at smaller scales, and if so, how much of the measured penalty is about the verification *format* rather than the verification *act*?
- How much of the result changes on open-ended, non-exact-match tasks where majority voting cannot serve as the baseline at all — what would the "next-cheapest" honest baseline (e.g., single arbitrary sample) look like there, and would self-refinement beat it?
- Would training a lightweight verifier (as in Zhang et al., cited as the one result pointing the other way) on the same small Qwen2.5 models close the gap this paper measures for the untrained, single-prompt verifier?
- Does the Reflexion self-judgment failure rate (100% "correct" at 1.5B, dropping with scale) reflect a general property of small models being poorly calibrated about their own correctness, and if so, does that alone explain why self-triggered reasoning methods are especially risky to deploy without instrumenting how often they actually fire?
