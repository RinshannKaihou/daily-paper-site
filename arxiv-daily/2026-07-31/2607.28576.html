Key Metric — Same 8 Samples, Different Rule

−17.3pp
Qwen2.5-3B / MATH-500: letting the model pick its best-of-8 answer scores 52.0% — simply counting the majority vote among the identical 8 samples scores 69.3% (95% CI [−24.0, −11.3], p<0.001).
No token or sample difference — only the aggregation rule changed. see Results ↓

Why This Matters

Gives LLM reasoning-strategy researchers a reusable equal-cost statistical testing framework, plus concrete evidence that self-correction methods need real scrutiny before adoption.

Critical Edge

The whole result is math-only with exact-match grading — the one domain where majority-vote counting has its easiest possible competitor — so it may not transfer to the open-ended tasks Self-Refine was originally designed for, and testing stops at 7B.

Methodology

Setup — small-model regime the predecessor study skipped
7 methods faithfully re-implemented→ Qwen2.5-1.5B / 3B (+7B for one comparison)→ GSM8K + MATH-500, 150 Q each→ Q8_0 quant, llama.cpp, single CPU, no GPU
Cost accounting — built to favor the chatty methods
cost = all generated tokens, every call→ self-consistency curve from one K=16 pool→ subsample N≤16 w/o replacement, 200 draws→ method's cost located by interpolation
Statistics — the paper's strongest dimension
paired bootstrap 95% CI, 10,000 resamples→ Holm–Bonferroni across 7 methods/setting→ confound-free: same 8 samples, only rule varies

No method ever sees the ground-truth answer (closing the leak Huang et al. flagged). Methods tested: Chain-of-Thought, Plan-and-Solve, Self-Refine (3 rounds), Reflexion as published, a forced-Reflexion variant (always retries), Best-of-N self-verify (N=8), and 3-agent Multi-Agent Debate — all compared against the plainest baseline, self-consistency majority vote, at matched token cost.

Strengths

  • Exceptionally self-auditing: documents two of its own scoring bugs in detail, with exact before/after numbers, instead of quietly re-reporting.
  • Statistical treatment (paired bootstrap, Holm correction, explicit minimum-detectable-effect calculation) is well above the norm for reasoning-strategy papers.
  • The Best-of-N-vs-counting design is genuinely clever: it isolates "is self-evaluation harmful?" by holding the samples themselves fixed.
  • Full harness, prompts, and all 19,200 raw generations released, so grading can be independently re-audited rather than trusted.
  • Candid about limitations throughout — several places where its own robustness checks partially undercut its point estimates.

Limitations

  • Model scale capped at 7B, and only for the single cheapest comparison — frontier-scale behavior is untested and explicitly unclaimed.
  • Math-only, exact-match domain — structurally favorable to majority-vote counting; may not transfer to open-ended generation or dialogue tasks.
  • One fixed implementation and prompt per method; a differently worded judge prompt could change Reflexion's near-total self-judgment failure at 1.5B.
  • Cost measured in output tokens only — the paper admits this is the measure under which the tested methods look best.
  • 150 paired questions reliably detect only ~5–6pp+ differences; smaller true per-method effects can't be distinguished from zero.
  • Randomized methods (baseline, Best-of-N, debate) run once — seed-level variance isn't captured, biasing toward overstating significance.

Questions for Further Exploration

  • Does the Best-of-N judge-vs-count gap reverse at frontier scale (30B+, GPT/Claude-class), or does it plateau at parity as observed here?
  • Would scoring each candidate independently, rather than choosing among all eight at once, close the gap at smaller scales?
  • What happens on open-ended, non-exact-match tasks where majority voting can't serve as a baseline at all?
  • Would a lightweight trained verifier (as in Zhang et al., the one result pointing the other way) close the gap for these small models?
  • Does small-model overconfidence (100% self-judged "correct" at 1.5B) generalize as a reason self-triggered reasoning methods are risky to deploy unmonitored?
