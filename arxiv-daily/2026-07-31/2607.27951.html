Corollary 1 · Eq. 6

Under a dual-use condition (Eq. 5: every valuable release has a malicious use) and copyable evidence, Theorem 1 proves any interactive safeguard — however many clarifying questions or randomized checks — collapses exactly to this static floor Γ(q). More questioning never helps once the attacker can copy the answer.

Why This Matters

Sets a hard boundary for LLM safety design: no amount of prompting, filtering, or questioning escapes the floor without a non-copyable, downstream-predictive credential.

中文原文（Relevance）：论文提出的"证据可复制导致安全性存在下界"的理论框架，对研究 LLM 安全防护（越狱防御、意图识别、访问控制）设计边界和不可能性结果的读者有直接参考价值，尤其适合与 Bhatt et al. 的 Defense Trilemma、Ball et al. 的过滤计算难度结果对照阅读。

▲ Critical Edge

The paper never estimates ρ, d, or ηκ for any real deployed safeguard — its "exact" floor is proven mathematically, not measured on any production system.

Methodology

1

Setup — for the same byte-identical release, compare two downstream-use worlds: legitimate (utility uB) and malicious (an attacker picks the best of possibly several allowed misuses). The release menu includes a zero-utility refusal.

2

Access-verification exchange — before the terminal release, a transcript of questions and answers is generated by either a legitimate or malicious user strategy interacting with a committed safeguard policy κ.

3

Capability floor Γ(q) — the minimum worst-case malicious utility achievable while guaranteeing legitimate utility ≥ q, defined as a linear program over the release-outcome polytope (Eq. 4).

4

Copyability → reduction — if the attacker can reproduce the legitimate strategy's transcript law (Proposition 1), Theorem 1 proves any interactive safeguard collapses exactly to the static floor Γ(q).

5

Trusted credentials — a non-copyable signal S (verified role, continuity, execution state) with separation d between legitimate/malicious distributions can push below the floor, but only if β + d + ηκ ≥ Γ(q) (Theorem 3) and some legitimate value sits on signal values malicious processes literally cannot reach (Corollary 3).

Scope: results assume a fixed utility calibration and finite attacker class, and explicitly exclude open-weight model release — "open access" here means credential-free access to a committed inference API (Section 5).

No original experiments, datasets, or baselines. The paper never instantiates its own operational quantities — ρ (dual-use ratio), δκ (copying error), d (credential separation) — for any concrete deployed system; Section 4 only synthesizes numbers from other papers to argue the premises are realistic.

Strengths

  • Proves an exact equality (Theorem 1), not just an inequality or hardness result — sharper than several contemporaneous impossibility papers it compares against.
  • Decomposes any deployment's safety claim into three separately measurable, falsifiable quantities (β, d, ηκ in Eq. 22).
  • Unusually careful about scope: explicitly excludes open-weight release and states which restricted-implementation classes still inherit the floor (Corollary 2).
  • Extends the floor across task families, imperfect per-turn copying, and repeated/correlated multi-session attacks (Proposition 2) with no independence assumption.
  • Ties the abstract framework to real deployed programs — OpenAI's "Trusted Access for Cyber," Anthropic's security-researcher verification — as concrete credential instances.

Limitations

  • No original experiments or measurements instantiating Γ(q), ρ, δκ, or d for any real safeguard — all Section 4 "evidence" is secondhand synthesis.
  • The dual-use condition (Eq. 5) and utility functions uB, uM are exogenous assumptions; no procedure is given for estimating them in production.
  • Explicitly excludes open-weight model release and user-controlled model settings — a significant share of real dual-use risk falls outside scope.
  • "Reliable safety" is defined purely worst-case against a specified, finite attacker class; average-case or probabilistic attacker models are not addressed.
  • The trusted-credential fix is not evaluated for feasibility, cost, or privacy impact of building a signal S with the needed separation and support-disjointness.

Questions for Further Exploration

  1. Can Γ(q), ρ, or δκ be estimated empirically for a specific deployed safeguard, to test whether the "practically relevant" claim holds with a meaningful gap?
  2. How does the exact worst-case floor relate to average-case or probabilistic-attacker robustness, likely the more operative threat model for real deployments?
  3. What is the actual cost/privacy tradeoff of building a credential with sufficient separation d and support-disjointness for zero assistance in a concrete domain?
  4. Does the additive composition result (Proposition 2) change if the attacker can also choose which task context to pursue across sessions?
  5. How robust is the dual-use ratio ρ to menu redesign — can real deployments find intermediate releases that meaningfully shrink Γ(q) without a credential system?
