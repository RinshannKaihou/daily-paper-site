Perfect world-model accuracy doesn't win ARC-AGI-3 games — knowing when to build, fix, or bypass the model matters more.

一句话总结：论文把ARC-AGI-3形式化为"渲染的确定性Moore机"识别问题，提出Tycho系统让编码代理在交互中构建、验证、修复或绕过可执行的Python世界模型；在25个公开游戏上用Claude Opus 4.8做四种模型维护策略的匹配对比，"actor请求委托给builder"（orchestrator）策略RHAE最高达88.49，随后用该策略跑GPT-5.6 Sol和Opus 5均达到100.00 RHAE（183关全通），Opus 5比官方人类基线少用61%的计分动作；但"自动触发修复"（trigger）策略虽然让模型转移预测准确率最高（88.1% vs. single的13.9%、orchestrator的16.2%），RHAE却只有83.07，低于single和orchestrator——"模型准不准"和"游戏玩得好不好"是两回事。

Why This Matters

Shows LLM agents can master interactive, rule-free games by managing an explicit world model — but only if they also manage when to trust it.

中文原文（Relevance）：该工作提供了一个清晰、可复用的"程序化世界模型 + 元推理策略"框架，用于分析交互式ARC类基准中"何时该建模、何时该直接推理"的核心权衡，对研究LLM agent世界模型构建、验证与主动抽象策略有较高参考价值，其四策略匹配对比方法论也可迁移到其他需要在推理成本与模型精度间做元决策的agentic任务评测中。

▲ Critical Edge

The entire matched-policy comparison is a single stochastic run per policy on 25 games — with no repeated-run variance estimate, the headline gap that selected orchestrator (88.49) over single (85.36) could partly reflect run-to-run noise rather than a stable architectural effect, a limitation the paper itself admits rather than hides.

Methodology

1

Formal substrate. Each ARC-AGI-3 level is defined as a parameterized rendered deterministic Moore machine, with a typed interaction history tagging every recorded grid as decision, transient, or terminal so builders can't fabricate transitions between non-consecutive frames. Proposition 1 proves the current rendered grid and action mask alone are insufficient state; a renderer may abstain (⊥) on undetermined pixels rather than guess.

2

Executable hypothesis. The agent authors a Python program with four required functions — init_state, transition, render, outcome — plus optional planner hooks. A verifier replays each level's recent attempts against the model to score transition match, coverage, and terminal recall; a planner threads accepted history into a "guarded," hash-validated route to level_complete.

3

Four metareasoning policies, one interface. Under identical Claude Opus 4.8 budgets (xhigh reasoning, 3,500 LM calls, $750/game), the harness compares no-world-model (direct reasoning), single (actor edits its own model), orchestrator (actor delegates to a specialist builder subagent on request), and trigger (harness auto-invokes the builder after verification failure) — they differ only in who edits the model and when, never who acts.

Two-stage evaluation: all 25 public ARC-AGI-3 games (183 levels) settle the best Opus 4.8 policy by mean RHAE; that policy (orchestrator) is then run as a full system with GPT-5.6 Sol and Opus 5, replayed through the official ARC-AGI API and cross-checked against 340 released human replays.

Trigger's model is by far the most faithful simulator, yet it delivers the worst RHAE of the three model-using policies — accurate dynamics did not translate into better decisions.

  • Human-replay standing: against 340 released first-run replays, orchestrator/Opus 4.8 reaches a game-balanced midrank of 86.14 (17/25 top-decile); GPT-5.6 Sol reaches 98.47 (24/25) and Opus 5 reaches 100.00 (25/25 top-decile).
  • Cost is roughly flat for the winning policy: ~$5.66k (no-model), $7.27k (single), $5.78k (orchestrator), $8.03k (trigger) for the 25-game study — orchestrator gets the highest RHAE at essentially no-model cost. The GPT-5.6 Sol transfer run costs ~$4.47k; Opus 5 costs ~$2.99k.
  • Cross-system leaderboard (Table 5): Tycho/Opus 5 and Tycho/GPT-5.6 Sol (both 100.00 RHAE) lead Rodionov's variant (98.97), the Schema harness (98.98, allows reruns), and PRO-LONG's first pass (94.71) — the paper is explicit that scoring protocols differ across rows and this is not a single controlled leaderboard.
  • sk48 case study: an accurate transition model paired with an unresolved objective completed 0/8 levels under orchestrator (13 builder calls, 314 actions), while trigger completed all 8 on the same game — a striking anecdote, but not systematically audited across the other 24 games.

Critical Assessment

NoveltyFormalizing levels as rendered deterministic Moore machines and cleanly separating "model accuracy" from "model allocation" is genuinely useful, but the paper itself notes several contemporaneous efforts (Rodionov, OPINE-World, Schema, PRO-LONG) converging on similar programmatic-world-model ideas the same month.
RigorUnusually transparent about protocol differences across competing systems, reports paired game-resampling intervals (Table 3), and admits RHAE saturates — 114–171 of completed levels already hit the per-level efficiency ceiling of 115, so the metric "compresses substantial differences in action use."
ReproducibilityCode is open-sourced (Apache-2.0, GitHub), with token/cost breakdowns (Table 6) and a pre-registered 5-dev/20-eval game split. Full cost reproduction stays sensitive to caching — the authors' own cross-system normalization shows a 3–5× swing on a competitor's numbers.
SignificanceSingle stochastic run per policy with no repeated-run variance estimate — the headline policy-selection gap could partly be noise. RHAE also ignores inference cost entirely: Opus 5 still uses 15.1k model calls and ~$2.99k compute despite 61% fewer scored actions.

Strengths

  • Clean, well-motivated formalization (rendered deterministic Moore machines, typed interaction history, partial-output prediction with abstention) that generalizes beyond this one system.
  • Genuinely matched four-way ablation of model-maintenance policies under identical LM/budget conditions, isolating the effect of when/who builds the model rather than conflating it with model choice.
  • Transparent cross-system benchmarking against at least 8 contemporaneous ARC-AGI-3 approaches, with explicit protocol caveats rather than a single flattering leaderboard number.
  • Unusually self-critical limitations section covering contamination risk, single-run variance, human-mediated adaptation, and action-efficiency-excludes-inference-cost.
  • Open-source release with cost/token accounting (Table 6) enabling independent cost-based comparison, which the paper itself uses to normalize against competitors.

Limitations

  • Single stochastic run per policy/model on a fixed 25-game set; no repeated-run variance estimates, so reported RHAE gaps between policies could partially reflect noise (the paper's own caveat).
  • Three of the 25 public games (ft09, ls20, vc33) were already public since the July 2025 developer preview, so contamination cannot be fully excluded.
  • Task contract, memory schema, and modeling tools encode ARC-AGI-3-specific priors (64×64 grids, 16 colors, RESET semantics) — explicitly not benchmark-agnostic.
  • The harness-adaptation loop (Section 6) is entirely human-mediated: authors select, refine, and validate harness changes from LLM-generated reflections — not autonomous self-improvement.
  • RHAE ignores inference cost/compute entirely, so "efficiency" gains may substitute for large amounts of unscored reasoning (Opus 5's 15.1k model calls for one run).
  • Human-replay comparisons use independent first-run replays (10–54 per game) as a reference distribution, not a matched 25-game head-to-head participant.

Questions for Further Exploration

  1. Would repeated runs (multiple seeds) of each policy substantially shrink or widen the observed RHAE gaps between single, orchestrator, and trigger?
  2. How does the orchestrator policy perform on genuinely held-out ARC-AGI-3 games unseen during any prior development or model training — the paper's own "decisive next test," not attempted here?
  3. Can the active-abstraction decision (construct/repair/use/bypass) itself be learned or automated rather than left to the frontier LLM's in-context judgment?
  4. How sensitive are the reported cost estimates (Table 6) to actual prompt-caching behavior in production API usage?
  5. Could a hybrid policy combine trigger's automatic repair with orchestrator's actor-initiated delegation to capture superior transition accuracy without trigger's lower RHAE?
