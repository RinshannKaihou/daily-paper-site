# Are Causal Neuron Circuits Unique and Transferable?

> **一句话总结：** 作者对 12 神经元因果分组穷举测量全部 4,096 个子集，发现"整组很少是必要的"（狮子王链上最少 4/8/7 个神经元即可翻转答案），不同模型中 3/10、7/10、7/8 条链存在互不相交的替代子集，且一个能通过随机对照、可加性、迁移性全部检验的分组（Qwen3-0.6B 首都题，零消融使答案裕度降 27% 而随机神经元≈0%）却对题目内容毫无特异性——提醒我们组级干预演示只证明"充分"，不证明"必要"或"唯一"。

> **TL;DR:** Prompted by Goodfire's 18-neuron "Lion King" demo in Qwen3-32B, this single-author preprint exhaustively measures all 4,096 subsets of 12-neuron groups in Qwen3-0.6B/1.7B, Llama-3.2-1B and Gemma-3-1B. On 20 self-written two-hop chains, patching 12 neurons with first-hop activations flips the answer to the bridge entity (greedy generation starts with the bridge in 9/11 chains for Qwen3-1.7B, 9/11 for Llama-3.2-1B, 8/10 for Gemma-3-1B) where a 12-random-neuron control flips 0/11, 0/11, 0/10 — yet the full group is rarely necessary (smallest flipping subset on the Lion King chain: 4, 8, 7 neurons), disjoint alternatives are common in low-margin chains, and Study 1's capital-question group passes every standard check (−27% margin vs ~0% for random, R²=0.99 additivity, Spearman 0.96–1.00 transfer) while a content-matched control shows it carries no question-specific content. The actionable recommendation: group demonstrations should add content-matched controls (resampling from other inputs), report the sufficient-subset size distribution, and report the clean margin.

## 核心贡献

- **把"可解释性演示证明了什么"变成可测量的问题。** 以 Goodfire 在 Qwen3-32B 上移除 18 个"狮子王"神经元（P(Scar) 从 0.91 降到 0.24）为引子，作者将该类组级干预拆解为三个可穷举检验的问题：必要性（组里多少神经元真正必需）、唯一性（不相交的子集能否产生同样效果）、迁移性（冻结的组对改写问题和其他问题是否有效）。
- **穷举式子集分析（每 12 神经元组测全部 4,096 个子集）。** 由此派生出可加性拟合、两两交互项、最小充分子集、不相交替代对、迁移相关等指标，把 Méloux 等人在玩具网络上提出的"解释不可辨识性"担忧首次落到预训练大模型的实际分组演示层面。
- **研究 1 的"看似因果却无内容"警示。** 在 Qwen3-0.6B 首都二选一任务上，12 个神经元通过全部常规检验（零消融降裕度 27% vs 随机组 0%、近乎完美可加、跨改写迁移 Spearman 0.96–1.00），但把它们的激活换成来自其他题目的值几乎无影响（0.00 [-0.01, 0.01]）——激活在不同题目间近乎常量（MAD 0.12 vs 中位幅值 2.53）。标准证据组合与"分组不携带题目内容"完全兼容。
- **研究 2 在三个模型家族复现 Scar→Simba 式翻转并量化其结构。** 用首跳问题激活对 12 神经元做 patching，在 Qwen3-1.7B / Llama-3.2-1B / Gemma-3-1B 上分别翻转 10/11、10/11、8/10 条链（随机神经元 0 翻转）；整组很少必要，且置信度低（裕度 m0 < 4）的链中 13/15 存在不相交替代子集，置信度高（m0 ≥ 4）时仅 4/13。
- **可操作的方法学建议：** 组级演示除随机对照外应补充"内容匹配对照"（从其他输入重采样/patching）、报告充分子集的大小分布、并报告清洁裕度，因为裕度塑造一切必要性与唯一性结论。
- **全程可复现：** 代码、题目集、每次运行的保存结果及计算论文中每个数字的脚本全部开源（MIT 许可，github.com/omtekyav/causal-neuron-circuits），且附录 B 逐条记录了预注册规则与事后改动。

## Key Contributions

- **Reframes what group-intervention demos prove.** Using Goodfire's 18-neuron "Lion King" demo in Qwen3-32B (removal changes the answer from "Scar" to "Simba", P(Scar) 0.91 → 0.24) as motivation, the paper decomposes the open questions into necessity (how much of the group is needed), uniqueness (can disjoint subsets do the same job), and transfer (rephrasings vs. other questions) — all answerable exhaustively at n = 12.
- **Exhaustive subset analysis as a cheap, complete audit.** For every frozen 12-neuron group the authors measure all 2^12 = 4,096 subsets, deriving additivity fits, pairwise interactions, minimal effective sets, disjoint alternative pairs, and transfer correlations. This turns the identifiability concern of Méloux et al. (toy networks) into a measurement on pre-trained LLMs at the level where group demonstrations are actually made.
- **Study 1: a cautionary null result on Qwen3-0.6B capital questions.** A 12-neuron group passes the usual evidence — zero-ablation drops the answer margin by 27% (random neurons ~0%), near-perfect additivity (R² = 0.99, slope 1.00, zero interacting pairs), transfer to rephrasings (Spearman 0.96–1.00) — yet resampling the same neurons with values from other capital questions does nothing (0.00 [-0.01, 0.01]): activations are nearly constant across questions (MAD 0.12 vs. median magnitude 2.53). A group can look causal, additive, and transferable without carrying question-specific content.
- **Study 2: the Scar → Simba shift replicated across three model families, then dissected.** Patching 12 neurons with first-hop activations flips the composed answer to the bridge entity in 10/11 (Qwen3-1.7B), 10/11 (Llama-3.2-1B), 8/10 (Gemma-3-1B) chains with zero flips from random neurons; the full group is rarely necessary (smallest flipping subsets range 1–8 neurons), and disjoint flipping subsets coexist in 3/10, 7/10, 7/8 chains — mostly when the model's clean margin is low (13/15 chains with m0 < 4 vs. 4/13 with m0 ≥ 4).
- **Methodological recommendations for future group demonstrations:** report (i) a content-matched control (resampling or patching from other inputs) in addition to random neurons, (ii) the size distribution of sufficient subsets, and (iii) the clean margin, which shapes every necessity/uniqueness conclusion.
- **Reproducibility-first practice:** all code, question sets, saved run results, and scripts reproducing every number and figure are released under MIT; decision rules were pre-specified with every post-hoc deviation documented in Appendix B.

## Methodology

The paper works with base checkpoints of Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen3-1.7B, Llama-3.2-1B, and Gemma-3-1B (Llama/Gemma via public unsloth mirrors). A "neuron" is one coordinate of the input to an MLP's down-projection (the gated MLP activation) at a given layer; all four interventions — zero ablation, mean ablation (average over a separate prompt set), resampling (activation from a different prompt), and patching (activation from a designated source prompt) — act only at the last prompt token.

The metric is the margin m = logit(correct) − logit(alternative); in Study 2 the alternative is the first token of the bridge entity. Intervention effect is ∆(S) = m(clean) − m(S), normalized by the clean margin m0 (∆/m0 = 1 means the margin hits zero; > 1 means the answer flips; exact bfloat16 ties are not counted as flips). Candidate neurons are ranked by first-order estimates (a·∂m/∂a for ablation, (a_target − a_source)·∂m/∂a for patching, in the spirit of AtP*), then individually measured; the 12 candidates are the 6 strongest by measured effect plus the next 6 by estimate, so weak-alone neurons are not excluded a priori. Selection uses one prompt per question and the group is then frozen.

For each frozen group, ∆(S) is measured for all 4,096 subsets, yielding: additivity (fit of ∆(S) by the sum of single-neuron effects), pairwise interactions I(i,j) = ∆({i,j}) − ∆({i}) − ∆({j}), minimal effective sets (thresholds at 30/50/70% of the full effect, plus the flip threshold ∆(S) > m0 in Study 2), disjoint pairs of minimal sets, and transfer of the frozen group to other prompts. Controls are 12 random neurons matched in layer counts under the identical intervention (fixed seed, one draw per question). Study 1 runs in float32; Study 2 in bfloat16 with an empirically measured noise floor (0.125–0.50 logits, from re-running 30 subsets per chain with different batch sizes and uncached forward passes); interaction thresholds are max(5% of full effect, 3× noise floor). Confidence intervals are 95% bootstrap over questions/chains.

Study 1 uses 10 two-option capital questions ("A. Paris / B. Stockholm", three-shot) on Qwen3-0.6B with candidates from layers 14 and 21, plus 10 held-out questions. Study 2 uses 20 author-written two-hop chains (subject → bridge → answer, e.g. The Lion King → Simba → Scar; Eiffel Tower → France → Paris), each with a first-hop question, a second-hop question, and three phrasings of the composed question; a chain enters the analysis only if the model answers both hops and the first composed phrasing correctly with m0 ≥ 1.

## Results & Highlights

- **Study 1 (Qwen3-0.6B, 10 capital questions):** zero-ablating the 12 selected neurons lowers the answer margin by ∆/m0 = 0.27 [0.26, 0.30] while 12 random neurons give −0.00 [−0.00, 0.00]; effects are almost perfectly additive (R² = 0.99 [0.99, 1.00], slope 1.00 [0.97, 1.02]) with 0 interacting pairs — yet replacing the same neurons with their activations from other capital questions changes nothing (0.00 [−0.01, 0.01] for same-correct-letter resampling; −0.00 [−0.01, 0.00] for the mean of other questions), i.e. the 27% margin drop coexists with a 0% content-matched effect.
- **Study 1 activation statistics:** median absolute deviation of a neuron's activation from its mean across other discovery questions is 0.12 against a median activation magnitude of 2.53; the frozen group still drops the margin by 0.24 [0.22, 0.26] on 10 held-out questions (random group: −0.01).
- **Study 2 flips vs. random control:** patching the 12-neuron group with first-hop activations flips the answer in 10/11 chains (Qwen3-1.7B), 10/11 (Llama-3.2-1B), 8/10 (Gemma-3-1B); greedy generation then starts with the bridge in 9/11, 9/11, 8/10 chains; 12 random neurons patched identically flip 0/11, 0/11, 0/10 — never.
- **On the 7 chains shared by all three models (Table 3):** the full-group patch flips 6/7 chains in each of the three families with ∆/m0 medians of 1.52 [1.23, 1.62] (Qwen3-1.7B), 1.83 [1.17, 2.21] (Llama-3.2-1B), 1.80 [1.23, 3.17] (Gemma-3-1B), vs. 0/7 flips for random neurons.
- **Necessity:** the smallest answer-flipping subset of the Lion King chain has 4 neurons (Qwen3-1.7B), 8 (Llama-3.2-1B), 7 (Gemma-3-1B) out of 12; across chains the minima are as small as 1 neuron (e.g. Llama-3.2-1B: 1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 4, 5, 8), and subset-effect curves rise smoothly rather than step-like.
- **Uniqueness and confidence:** among chains the full group flips, disjoint pairs of minimal flipping subsets exist in 3/10 (Qwen3-1.7B), 7/10 (Llama-3.2-1B), 7/8 (Gemma-3-1B); pooling models, 13/15 chains with clean margin m0 < 4 have disjoint alternatives vs. 4/13 chains with m0 ≥ 4, and the 4 chains no subset at all can flip all have m0 ≥ 4.6 (exploratory, post-hoc cut-off).
- **Transfer:** frozen-group effects on rephrasings of the same chain correlate at Spearman 0.98 [0.97, 0.99] / 0.98 [0.91, 0.98] / 0.97 [0.90, 0.98] with the original, but the same neurons on other chains yield ∆/m0 of only 0.18 [0.07, 0.34] / 0.09 [0.03, 0.26] / 0.11 [0.04, 0.26] versus 1.52 / 1.89 / 1.82 on their own chain; cross-chain patching of the P-group still reduces the margin substantially (∆/m0 ≈ 0.85–0.92), showing part of the effect is generic disruption rather than bridge-specific.
- **Interaction structure in Study 2:** additive fits have R² = 0.90 [0.83, 0.92] / 0.89 [0.70, 0.93] / 0.84 [0.58, 0.92] with sub-unit slopes (0.78 / 0.80 / 0.83), and pairs exceeding the interaction threshold are rare: 4/726 (Qwen3-1.7B), 7/726 (Llama-3.2-1B), 11/660 (Gemma-3-1B).

## Authors & Credibility

Single-author work by Ömer Tekin Yavuz, an R&D and AI Engineer at İletişim Yazılım (Bursa, Turkey) — an independent, non-academic affiliation; the paper is an arXiv preprint (v2, 11 Nov 2026), not peer-reviewed. The acknowledgment states generative AI tools were used for analysis code and language editing, with all experiments run and all numbers computed by the author from saved result files. Credibility is bolstered by unusually transparent methodology: decision rules written down before data collection, Appendix B documenting every post-hoc change (e.g. the flip threshold tightened from ∆(S) ≥ m0 to ∆(S) > m0, changing one headline count from 4/10 to 3/10), a measured bfloat16 noise floor, layer-matched random controls, and 95% bootstrap intervals throughout. All code, question sets, and saved results are on GitHub under an MIT license with scripts that recompute every number and figure. The author is careful not to overclaim: the paper explicitly does not test Goodfire's 18 neurons and states it does not refute their result.

## Critical Assessment

- **Novelty:** The core move — exhaustively enumerating all 4,096 subsets of a neuron group to audit necessity, uniqueness, and interaction — is simple but genuinely novel at the level of real pre-trained LLMs, and Study 1's dissociation (a group passing random-control + additivity + transfer while failing a content-matched control) is a sharper cautionary result than the typical "circuits are redundant" observation. It productively operationalizes Méloux et al.'s identifiability worry on natural-language tasks.
- **Rigor:** Above average for a preprint: exhaustive measurement rather than sampling, explicit noise floors (0.125–0.50 logits), thresholds at max(5% of effect, 3× noise floor), pre-specified rules with a deviation log, and honest labelling of the margin cut-off m0 = 4 as post hoc and confounded with chain identity.
- **Reproducibility:** Strong — MIT-licensed code, question sets, saved run outputs, and per-figure scripts; fixed seeds; unsloth mirrors named. The main reproducibility soft spot is the one-draw-per-question random control (a distribution of random groups would be better, as the authors admit).
- **Significance:** Real but bounded. The recommendations (content-matched controls, subset-size distributions, report the clean margin) are cheap and could meaningfully raise the evidential bar for interpretability demos; the findings themselves are pilot-scale. The additivity result is partially baked in by construction: candidates are chosen as the 6 strongest individually-measured neurons plus 6 best first-order estimates, a selection that structurally favors neurons that matter alone — purely synergistic minimal circuits would be missed by design, and the paper concedes this.
- **Red flags / weaknesses to weigh:** (1) Scale mismatch with the motivating example — 0.6–1.7B models on ≤ 11 chains each, versus Goodfire's 32B model; the 32B "did not fit in the GPU memory available", so the title's question is answered only for small models. (2) The 20 two-hop chains are written by the author himself, a small and possibly idiosyncratic task set with heavy pop-culture/geography skew. (3) The paper's own four-neuron Lion King subsets that were generation-checked all share the same two neurons, so the disjoint-alternative claim rests on the margin criterion, not on generation. (4) Interventions touch only the last prompt token, so earlier-position computation is untested. (5) Single-author, industry, non-academic provenance with AI-assisted analysis code means external replication is worth waiting for, even though internal transparency is high.

## Strengths

- Exhaustive (not sampled) subset measurement makes the necessity/uniqueness claims complete relative to the candidate group, with no statistical extrapolation needed.
- Study 1 is a genuinely useful negative control for the field: it demonstrates concretely that the standard evidence bundle (effect size + null random control + additivity + transfer) can be produced by a content-agnostic group, and it identifies the control that catches this (resampling from other inputs).
- Cross-family replication (Qwen3-1.7B, Llama-3.2-1B, Gemma-3-1B) on an identical shared set of 7 chains, with a pre-frozen protocol applied unchanged, makes the qualitative pattern — flips without random flips, small sufficient subsets, strong same-chain / weak cross-chain transfer — much more convincing than a single-model result.
- Exceptional transparency: pre-specified decision rules, a public log of every post-hoc change, measured numerical noise floors, and a repository that recomputes every number in the paper.
- The confidence-dependent uniqueness observation (disjoint alternatives concentrate in low-margin chains) is an interesting, hypothesis-generating bridge between circuit structure and model uncertainty.

## Limitations

- Pilot scale in both directions that matter: models of 0.6–1.7B parameters (the 32B motivating model did not fit in GPU memory) and at most 11 analyzed chains per model, all author-written.
- All necessity/uniqueness statements are relative to 12 neurons selected by single-neuron and first-order criteria, which favor individually-important neurons and may miss purely synergistic ones; "minimal sets" are minimal only within the candidate group, not the model's circuit.
- Interventions act only at the last prompt token; the metric is a first-token margin between answer and bridge (multi-token answers judged by first token, generation by first line).
- Study 2 runs in bfloat16 with a noise floor up to 0.50 logits, so small pairwise interactions may be invisible; the margin cut-off m0 = 4 was chosen after seeing Qwen3-1.7B data, and margins and chains are confounded.
- One random group per question (fixed seed) rather than a distribution of random groups; cross-chain transfer was tested on other included chains only, not on a broader input distribution.
- The paper studies neuron-level causal interventions and alternative subsets; it explicitly does not recover full circuit connectivity, and does not test Goodfire's actual 18-neuron group.

## Relevance

直接命中"LLM Hidden States & Interpretability"方向：这是对神经元级因果电路（circuits）证据标准的实证检验，与 probing、activation patching、SAE/电路发现等核心议题同域。其"内容匹配对照 + 充分子集分布 + 清洁裕度"的汇报建议，可立即用于评估其他 circuit 论文证据的可靠程度。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- Do the findings hold at 8B–32B scale and on the actual Goodfire 18-neuron group, where the clean margins and redundancy structure may differ substantially from 1–2B models?
- Would a candidate-selection procedure that admits synergistic neurons (e.g. greedy pair search or higher-order effect estimates) change the additivity and minimal-set-size conclusions, given the current top-single-neuron selection may bake in additivity?
- What explains the Study 1 group mechanistically — are the near-constant activations (MAD 0.12 vs magnitude 2.53) a positional/format signal, a general "question-answering mode" feature, or an artifact of two-option prompting with a three-shot prefix?
- Is the association between low clean margin and disjoint alternatives a general property (testable with more chains and margin-matched designs), and does it survive interventions at earlier token positions or over multiple positions?
- How do the subset statistics interact with known redundancy phenomena like backup heads and the Hydra effect — does ablating a minimal flipping subset trigger downstream self-repair that the single-position margin metric misses?
- Can a content-matched control (resampling from other inputs) be cheaply standardized into circuit-discovery pipelines (ACDC, attribution graphs, transcoders) so that "generic vs. content-specific" is reported alongside effect size by default?
