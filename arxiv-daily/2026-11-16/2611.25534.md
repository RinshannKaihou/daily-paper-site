> **一句话总结：** 本文首次系统研究"无反馈自我进化"（模型仅凭技能文本+一句主题、反复重写专家技能、不看任务/运行/评分）对 Agent 技能的影响：6 个模型 × 3 个基准、连续 30 轮重写后全部退化——SkillsBench 通过率平均掉 14.6 分（首轮即掉 7.1 分），LogicBench 从 86.2% 降到 71.4%（等于不用技能），MedCalc-Bench 从 89.7% 降到 78.7%（低于无技能的 81.1%）；作者用"模型只看主题自写的 10 份技能"定义模型先验，证明重写把技能拉向先验，并优先丢弃先验之外的内容（30 轮后仅存 23.1%，而先验内条目存留 84.2%），把这些条目放回可恢复 8.8/14.6 分；据此提出的 SkillDiff 只在原文原地标注先验外条目并加一句提示，不改一个字、不需反馈，就在 SkillsBench 上平均再涨 4.8 分。

> **TL;DR:** Letting six models (GPT-5.6-Luna, GPT-6.1-Sol, MiMo-V2.5-Pro, Claude Haiku 5.5, Qwen3.7-Plus, Qwen3.8-Flash) rewrite expert agent skills for 30 rounds with no task, run, or verifier makes every skill worse, not better: on SkillsBench the pass rate falls 7.1 points after one rewrite and 14.6 after thirty (best-to-worst per-agent loss 10.3–23.0), and on the SRA-Bench subsets LogicBench/MedCalc-Bench rewriting erases the skill's entire value (86.2%→71.4% and 89.7%→78.7%, the latter *below* the 81.1% no-skill baseline). Item-level tracking against ten self-written "prior" skills shows why: rewriting is not random corruption but a selective pull toward what the model would write from the topic alone — after 30 rounds only 23.1% of items absent from all ten self-written skills survive versus 84.2% of items all ten state (61.1-point gap), and restoring just the out-of-prior items to the round-30 skill recovers 8.8 of the 14.6 lost points versus 4.4 for restoring the same volume of other lost items. The practical payoff, SkillDiff, adds `[FOCUS]` tags and a one-line instruction to the untouched original skill and gains 4.8 points on SkillsBench (GPT-5.6-Luna +12.6, Qwen3.8-Flash +1.1) — but it is a placebo-free, single-run measurement, so the effect size deserves replication.

## 核心贡献

- **现象（首次系统刻画无反馈自我进化对技能的伤害）**：让 6 个模型在无任务、无执行、无判别信号的情况下，仅凭技能文本与一句主题连续重写 30 轮，然后用同一模型作为 Agent 使用重写后的技能。三个基准上、每个模型上都退化：SkillsBench 通过率第 1 轮就降 7.1 分、第 5 轮 10.9 分、第 30 轮 14.6 分（单模型 10.3–23.0 分）；LogicBench 从 86.2% 降到 71.4%（与完全不用技能持平）；MedCalc-Bench 从 89.7% 降到 78.7%，反而低于无技能的 81.1%。伤害来源于重写本身，而非"跨模型不匹配"：同一模型重写、同一模型使用，照样退化；两模型互换重写产物后，四种组合也全部低于原始技能（平均 −6.0 分）。
- **诊断（用"自写技能"定义模型先验，证明重写把技能拉向先验）**：让模型在不看专家文档的情况下、按同一主题与长度目标自写 10 份技能，作为"模型先验"的样本；用 TF-IDF 余弦距离度量（以自写技能两两平均距离归一化）。原始技能距先验 1.2–1.7 单位，重写使其单调靠近：平均 1.37 → 第 5 轮 1.19 → 第 30 轮 1.22，即先快速靠拢再稳定在"原始技能与自写技能之间"。用 Qwen3-Embedding 与裸词频两种替代度量，18 个（模型×基准）配对中分别有 15/18 与 16/18 在第 30 轮更靠近先验，结论不依赖度量。
- **机制（丢失是选择性的：先丢先验之外的内容）**：把技能机械切成 6,605 个条目（SkillsBench）、2,376 个（LogicBench+MedCalc-Bench），用 LLM 判别器逐条判定是否被保留，再按"10 份自写技能中有几份提到该条目"（0/10 到 10/10）分组。30 轮后平均仅 23.1% 的 0/10 条目存活，10/10 条目存活 84.2%；表 1 的分组均值 19% vs 83%（+64 分）。该分裂在列表项、段落、代码块、标题四类内部同样成立（各差 +31.6 到 +39.4 分），因此不是"重写偏爱压缩某类内容"的产物。
- **因果（放回先验外条目即可恢复大部分损失）**：从第 30 轮技能出发建立共同底座，分别放回 0/10 条目、≥1/10 条目、全部丢失条目。SkillsBench 上 30 轮共损失 14.6 分，只放回 0/10 条目恢复 8.8 分，放回其余条目仅恢复 4.4 分，放回全部（文本更多）也只有 10.2 分；LogicBench 71.4% → 80.4%（0/10）vs 75.0%（≥1/10），MedCalc-Bench 78.7% → 83.1% vs 80.0%。反向的删除实验也一致：从原始技能删掉同样字数，删 0/10 条目使通过率降 4.6 分，删 ≥1/10 条目反而 +0.6 分（差 5.2 分）。
- **方法（SkillDiff：不重写、不加内容、只改注意力）**：既然技能是"先验外知识"的唯一来源，重写至多只能保住它，那么剩下的问题是 Agent 会不会真的照着用。SkillDiff 用同样的"10 份自写技能 + 逐条判别"找出 0/10 条目，原地加粗标记 `[FOCUS]`，并在标题下加一句提示，要求把被标记条目里的具体步骤、工具、参数、条件与例外"照原样写进计划"而不是简化成通用做法。不改一个词、不需反馈、不需运行；去掉标签与提示即可逐字节还原原文。SkillsBench 上平均 +4.8 分（6 个 Agent 全部为正），成本为每技能每模型 1 次主题调用 + 10 次生成 + 10 次判别，标注平均增加 31 个标签、129 个词。

## Key Contributions

- **First systematic characterization of feedback-free skill evolution.** Prior work keeps a rewrite only when a verifier, validation task, or execution result supports it; this paper isolates the feedback-free regime that practice actually relies on and shows it is monotonically harmful over 30 recursive rounds on all six models and all three benchmarks. Skill value at round 30 is approximately that of having no skill at all on LogicBench/MedCalc-Bench, and on SkillsBench the per-agent loss ranges 10.3–23.0 points.
- **An operational definition of "the model's prior for a skill."** Ten skills written from a one-sentence topic (same length target, no access to the expert document) serve as samples of what the model supplies on its own; a normalized TF-IDF distance to these samples places a held-out self-written skill at 1.00 and the original expert skill at 1.37, giving a calibrated scale rather than an arbitrary similarity score.
- **Item-level, judge-validated evidence that loss is selective rather than uniform degradation.** 6,605 SkillsBench items (83,950 words: 3,085 list entries, 1,389 headings, 1,292 paragraphs, 684 code blocks, 68 tables, 87 front-matter blocks) are tracked individually; the 0/10-vs-10/10 retention gap is +64, +59, +60 points across the three benchmarks, and survives a second judge (+52.5), a second run of the same judge (+51.4), and a cross-family judge (+40.7).
- **A causal claim supported from both directions.** Restoration (put out-of-prior items back: +8.8 of 14.6 lost points, ~2× the +4.4 from restoring other items) and deletion (removing an equal number of words costs 4.6 points if out-of-prior, +0.6 if not) bracket the same conclusion, which is stronger than the usual "the skill got shorter" story.
- **SkillDiff: a zero-write, zero-feedback intervention with a measured gain.** +4.8 points mean on SkillsBench (range +1.1 to +12.6), no change to the skill's text, no agent run needed at marking time, and byte-exact reversibility (removing tags and note restores the original file).
- **Honest robustness appendix.** τ²-bench (164 airline/retail tasks) reproduces all four analyses; pass@16 (75.3% vs 63.2%) shows the loss is not a single-run artifact; 14 tasks pass at least once with the original skill but never in 16 runs with the round-30 skill (only 2 in reverse); three independent rewriting chains per skill and a web-search-off ablation are reported.

## Methodology

**Feedback-free rewriting loop.** Each model receives the current skill text plus a one-sentence topic and an instruction to identify problems and improve the skill. No task, no execution trace, no judgment, no verifier. Each output replaces its predecessor, recursively, for 30 rounds; only the skill *document* is rewritten — bundled scripts and reference files stay byte-identical. The same model that rewrote a skill is then the agent that uses it, so any benefit from self-adaptation is available to the method.

**Benchmarks.** (1) *SkillsBench* — 87 containerized tasks across eight domains (science, finance, office work, software engineering, …), each with a deterministic verifier; 63 of the 87 ship several curated skills, so GPT-5.6-Sol pre-selects the most relevant skill from the task instruction alone before any run, removing skill-retrieval as a confound and ensuring one expert skill per task. (2) *LogicBench* — 760 logical-reasoning questions with 19 gold skills. (3) *MedCalc-Bench* — 1,100 medical-calculation questions with 55 gold skills. The latter two are SRA-Bench subsets where the whole skill is pasted into a single prompt, so the skill participates in one answer rather than in a multi-step plan — a deliberate contrast between "skill as plan ingredient" and "skill as prompt context."

**Models and harnesses.** Six models across families and capability tiers: GPT-5.6-Luna and GPT-6.1-Sol (Codex), Claude Haiku 5.5 (Claude Code), MiMo-V2.5-Pro, Qwen3.7-Plus, Qwen3.8-Flash (OpenHands). Original-skill pass rates on SkillsBench span roughly a quarter to more than half of tasks, so the study is not run at a ceiling or a floor. Evaluation uses the original skill and the skills of rounds 1, 5, 10, 30 on identical tasks; LogicBench/MedCalc-Bench additionally include a no-skill condition.

**Prior measurement.** For every skill, the model writes ten documents from the same one-sentence topic and length target without seeing the expert text. Distance is mean cosine distance over TF-IDF word vectors, normalized by the mean pairwise distance among the ten self-written skills (so 1.0 = "as close to them as they are to each other"). TF-IDF is chosen deliberately because the expert's contribution is often a command, path, or parameter name, which embeddings underweight; two alternative representations (Qwen3-Embedding, raw word frequencies) are reported as robustness checks.

**Item segmentation and judging.** Every non-blank line of a skill belongs to exactly one item (heading, list entry, table run, fenced code block, paragraph, or the front-matter block), giving 6,605 SkillsBench items. A single LLM judge (GPT-5.6-Luna) reads all items of a skill together with one candidate document and labels each item `SAME` / `KEPT` / `REWORDED` / `WEAKENED` / `DROPPED` / `CHANGED`; shared words without shared meaning do not count as retention, and the judge is instructed not to presume that an edit is damage. `UNCLEAR` items are excluded. Reliability is checked with a second judge (GPT-6.1-Sol), a cross-family judge (Claude Haiku 5.5), a rerun of the main judge, 30 additional self-written documents (three more samples of ten), and two controls with known answers (identical skill → nothing lost; another task's skill → ≈99% lost).

**Restoration and deletion.** For each round-30 skill, a locator removes passages carrying weakened/changed versions of lost items to build a common base, then three versions reinsert only 0/10 items, only the other lost items, or all lost items. The symmetric deletion experiment removes ~23% of the body from the *original* skill, matched on word count, either only out-of-prior items or only in-prior items.

**SkillDiff.** Reuses the same pipeline: ten self-written skills → per-item judging → select items no self-written skill states → bold `[FOCUS]` tag in place + a note under the title asking the agent to carry those items' specific procedures, tools, parameters, conditions, and exceptions into its plan verbatim. Evaluation runs each marked skill in the same batch as its unmarked original to remove batch-to-batch drift.

## Results & Highlights

- **Every model degrades on every benchmark, fastest in the first rewrite.** SkillsBench mean pass rate falls 7.1 points after round 1, 10.9 after round 5, and 14.6 after round 30; per-agent round-30 losses run from 10.3 (Qwen3.8-Flash) to 23.0 (GPT-5.6-Luna). LogicBench drops 86.2% → 71.4% (−14.7) and MedCalc-Bench 89.7% → 78.7% (−11.1), where the no-skill baseline is 81.1% — i.e. thirty rewrites leave a *worse-than-nothing* skill on MedCalc-Bench, and exactly a nothing-level skill on LogicBench.
- **The item-level gap is the paper's cleanest number.** After 30 rounds, 23.1% of items that none of the ten self-written skills state survive, versus 84.2% of items all ten state — a 61.1-point retention gap averaged over 6 models × 3 benchmarks. Table 1's per-benchmark averages are 19% vs 83% (SkillsBench, Δ+64), 25% vs 84% (LogicBench, Δ+59), 25% vs 85% (MedCalc-Bench, Δ+60), and the split is already present after round 1 (49.5% vs 23.0% lost on SkillsBench).
- **Restoring out-of-prior items recovers about twice as much as restoring the rest.** On SkillsBench the 30-round loss is 14.6 points; restoring 0/10 items recovers 8.8 (pass rate 25.7 → 34.5), restoring ≥1/10 items recovers 4.4 (→30.1), restoring *all* lost items — more text — recovers only 10.2 (→35.8) against the 40.2 original. LogicBench: 71.4 → 80.4 (0/10) vs 75.0 (≥1/10) vs 80.6 (all); MedCalc-Bench: 78.7 → 83.1 vs 80.0 vs 84.4. Recovery tracks *what* returns, not how much.
- **Deletion reproduces the asymmetry from the other direction.** Removing ~23% of the original skill's words costs 4.6 points when the removed material is out-of-prior, versus +0.6 when the same volume of in-prior material is removed — a 5.2-point gap (GPT-5.6-Luna: −5.7 vs +2.3, i.e. −8.0 between conditions; GPT-6.1-Sol: −3.4 vs −1.1, −2.3).
- **SkillDiff adds 4.8 points without touching the text.** Per-agent gains on SkillsBench: GPT-5.6-Luna +12.6, GPT-6.1-Sol +5.7, MiMo-V2.5-Pro +3.4, Qwen3.7-Plus +2.3, Claude Haiku 5.5 +3.4, Qwen3.8-Flash +1.1; all six positive, mean +4.8, marked skills compared against originals run in the same batch. Cost is one topic call + ten generations + ten judge calls per (skill, model), and the marking adds ~31 tags / 129 words on average to a 965-word skill.
- **Generalization beyond skills.** On τ²-bench's 164 airline/retail tasks, thirty rounds of rewriting a written *policy* drops GPT-5.6-Luna from 64.0% to 25.6% and GPT-6.1-Sol from 72.6% to 18.9%; the 0/10 items are kept 45% vs 73% (Luna) and 66% vs 87% (Sol), and restoring them lifts the round-30 policy to 48.2% / 37.8% versus 23.2% / 19.5% for restoring the other items.
- **Not a single-run artifact.** With GPT-5.6-Luna at ≥16 runs per SkillsBench task, pass@16 is 75.3% with the original skill but 63.2% with the round-30 skill; the round-30 skill is below the original at every k from 1 to 16 with the 95% interval excluding zero, and 14 tasks pass at least once with the original but never in 16 attempts with the rewrite (2 in reverse). Averaged over all runs, the loss is 8.4 points after round 1 and 13.0 after round 30 for Luna, 7.3 and 11.5 for Sol.
- **The out-of-prior set is mostly shared across models.** An item outside model A's prior is outside model B's prior 82% of the time on average versus 48% expected by chance; no model pair falls below 70%, and 1,866 of 6,605 items lie outside every model's prior — so this is largely "knowledge models in general don't write," not a per-model quirk.

## Critical Assessment

**What is genuinely strong.** The causal argument is the best part and is built the way a good ablation should be: the paper does not stop at "rewriting hurts" and does not stop at "rewriting moves text toward the model's own style" (a correlation the whole-text distance metric could easily have been over-read as — the authors explicitly note that distance flattens after round 5 while pass rate keeps falling, and correctly abandon the document-level metric in favor of item-level tracking). Restoration and deletion then bracket the same conclusion from opposite directions, and the deletion experiment is the one most papers would skip: it rules out "any 23% of the text is equally important" and shows the asymmetry is in content, not volume. The known-answer judge controls (identical skill → 0 lost; another task's skill → 1.3%/1.5% labeled kept) preempt the obvious "your judge is just noisy" objection, and κ 0.72–0.84 across a cross-family judge is better than most LLM-annotation papers report.

**The weakest link is SkillDiff's evaluation.** Table 3 reports point deltas only — no confidence intervals, no repeated runs, no per-task breakdown, and no placebo control. With 87 SkillsBench tasks, one task is worth ~1.15 points, so +4.8 is roughly four tasks flipping, and +1.1 (Qwen3.8-Flash) or +2.3 (Qwen3.7-Plus) is one or two tasks. The paper is scrupulous about repeated runs for the *degradation* result (pass@16, 16 full evaluations, 95% intervals) and then reports the *intervention* result from a single batch per condition. Two controls that would cost almost nothing are missing: (a) marking an equal number of randomly chosen items instead of out-of-prior ones, and (b) adding only the "follow the skill literally, don't simplify to a generic approach" note *without* any tags. Without (a) and (b) one cannot separate "the model was told which content is scarce knowledge" from "the model was told to try harder / had its attention drawn to bold text," and the paper's own theory predicts (a) should not help while a generic-effort account predicts it might. Given that the entire practical contribution rests on this table, that is a real gap, not a nitpick.

**Rewriting-prompt sensitivity is unexamined.** Every round uses one instruction ("identify problems and improve the skill") held constant across six models, four harnesses, and three benchmarks. That is good for internal validity and bad for external validity: practitioners who write "improve this skill but preserve every command, path, and parameter" may see far less loss, and the paper's framing ("rewriting without feedback degrades skills") is really "rewriting *under this prompt* degrades skills." The web-search ablation is the only perturbation of the rewrite condition, and it shows the loss *grows* without search (−7.0 vs −4.3 points), which suggests the effect is about information access rather than prompt phrasing — but that is inference, not a test.

**Selection and construct issues in the "prior."** The prior is estimated from ten samples, and an item that the model writes only occasionally can land in 0/10 by chance. The 30-additional-sample check is the right response and the numbers are reassuring (0/10 items appear in 1.1% of the further documents vs 95.1% for 10/10 items; κ 0.75–0.84 between samples of ten), but note what it establishes: that 0/10 is a *stable* label, not that it means "knowledge the model lacks." A model may know a parameter name perfectly well and simply not volunteer it in a one-page document written from a topic sentence — the paper's own framing ("what the model does not write on its own") is careful here, and the causal experiments are about what happens to such content, so this is a conceptual precision issue rather than a flaw in the results.

**Benchmark and harness coverage.** SkillsBench supplies 87 tasks and one preselected skill per task; the SRA-Bench subsets paste the skill into a single prompt, so two of three benchmarks exercise a regime (skill as context, one call) that is quite different from the multi-step agent loop the paper's motivation is about. τ²-bench partially compensates (164 tasks, policy-as-skill), but the paper's Limitations correctly admit that multi-skill retrieval/composition and joint evolution of skill documents plus code are untouched — and the single-skill preselection means "which skill to load" is handled by GPT-5.6-Sol offstage, an unquantified dependency. The six models are all 2026 frontier or near-frontier systems from four families; nothing here speaks to small or open-weight models, where the prior would presumably be thinner and the out-of-prior fraction larger.

**Minor but worth flagging.** Restoration requires an LLM locator to excise "the passage carrying a weakened version" and to place reinserted items; the paper checks that every intended item returns and that the base is identical across the three versions, which is exactly the right audit, but the alternative (mechanical concatenation) is not compared, so it is unclear how much the locator's judgment shapes the +8.8/+4.4 contrast. SkillDiff's cost accounting is qualitative — 21 LLM calls per skill is cheap in dollars but the added 129 words of tags/notes compete for context with the skill itself, and no experiment measures SkillDiff under a tight context budget, which is the regime where skills get shortened in the first place (SkillReducer and related compression work are cited but not compared against). Finally, no feedback-based skill optimizer (SkillOpt, SkillFoundry, MetaSkill-Evolve) is run on the same 87 tasks, so the paper establishes that *feedback-free* rewriting is bad but not how much of the gap a verifier-driven loop would close.

## Strengths

- The degradation result is replicated across six models, three benchmarks, and 30 rounds with no counterexample — a rare level of coverage for a "this popular practice is harmful" claim.
- Item-level analysis turns a vague "skills get worse" into a falsifiable, mechanistically specific statement (selective loss of out-of-prior content), and the whole-text distance metric is explicitly reported *and* explicitly demoted when it fails to track the pass rate.
- The restoration/deletion pair is a genuinely causal design, and the deletion arm (equal word counts, opposite content) closes the "it's just length" alternative that most compression papers leave open.
- Judge reliability is taken seriously: second judge, cross-family judge, rerun, 30 extra samples, and two known-answer controls, with κ values reported rather than hidden.
- SkillDiff is minimal and honest: it changes no text, is byte-exactly reversible, needs no agent run at marking time, and the paper is clear that the gain comes from attention, not from new knowledge.
- The appendix is unusually complete — τ²-bench generalization, web-search off, pass@k, 16 full evaluations, three rewriting chains, cross-model execution swaps, and a cost accounting section.

## Limitations

- SkillDiff is measured with point estimates only on 87 tasks, without a placebo (random-item) marking control or a note-only control, so the +4.8 average cannot be separated from generic salience/effort effects, and the +1.1/+2.3 per-model values are within a couple of tasks of zero.
- Rewriting uses a single fixed "improve the skill" prompt across all conditions; prompt sensitivity — the most obvious moderator a practitioner would care about — is never tested.
- Two of three main benchmarks (LogicBench, MedCalc-Bench) read the skill once inside one prompt, a regime quite different from the multi-step agent loop motivating the work; only SkillsBench and the τ²-bench appendix exercise agentic use.
- SkillsBench uses one rewriting chain per skill (the three-chain robustness check covers LogicBench, MedCalc-Bench, and τ²-bench but not SkillsBench), so the headline 14.6-point loss rests on a single chain per skill.
- One preselected skill per task via GPT-5.6-Sol, only skill documents rewritten (scripts and reference files frozen), and no study of multi-skill retrieval, composition, or joint document+code evolution — as the authors acknowledge.
- No comparison against feedback-driven skill optimizers on the same benchmark, so the practical recommendation ("mark, don't rewrite") is not benchmarked against "rewrite with a verifier."
- All models are 2026 frontier systems; the out-of-prior fraction and the size of the degradation for small/open models are unknown.

## Relevance

与"自我进化 Agent / 技能库维护 / Agent 记忆与技能压缩"这条线直接相关：它给出了一个可复用的诊断工具（用模型自写的 N 份文档定义先验，再逐条目判定技能内容是否落在先验之外），并用它证明"无反馈重写"会系统性删除专家提供的、模型自己写不出来的操作性细节。对任何在做技能自动更新、技能压缩或上下文预算裁剪的系统，这都是一个"先做保真度审计再上线自进化"的强论据；SkillDiff 的原地标注思路也可以直接搬到 prompt/记忆/工具的自我修订流程里。

## Questions for Further Exploration

1. Does a placebo marking control (tagging an equal number of randomly chosen in-prior items, or only adding the "don't simplify" note without tags) reproduce any of SkillDiff's +4.8? This single experiment would decide whether the mechanism is "pointing at scarce knowledge" or "raising overall diligence."
2. How sensitive is the degradation to the rewriting instruction? A prompt that explicitly forbids dropping commands, paths, parameters, and exceptions — or that supplies a checklist of "operational anchors" — might cut the 14.6-point loss substantially, which would reframe the finding from "rewriting is harmful" to "unconstrained rewriting is harmful."
3. What happens with smaller/open-weight models whose prior is thinner? The out-of-prior fraction should grow, so both the degradation and SkillDiff's headroom should grow — a cheap and informative scaling test.
4. Is ten self-written skills the right prior estimator, and does the 0/10 threshold behave differently for skills whose content is mostly code (where "same meaning" judging is hardest) versus prose?
5. Can the item-level retention metric be used as a *training-time* or *merge-time* guard — e.g., reject a rewrite that drops out-of-prior items, or diff two candidate skills by their 0/10 overlap — and does that guard preserve quality better than SkillDiff's inference-time marking at equal cost?
6. Why does the loss keep growing after the document-level distance to the prior saturates at round 5? Tracking *which* 0/10 items are lost in rounds 6–30 (rather than how far the text has moved) would show whether late rounds are removing a small number of high-value items or steadily eroding a long tail.
