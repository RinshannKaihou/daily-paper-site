> **一句话总结：** 作者对 vLLM 前缀缓存中被多请求共享的 KV block 做软件单比特翻转注入（Qwen3-8B/BF16，尾数最高位 bit 6 与符号位 bit 15，30 个坐标、18.8 万次 flip–item 抽样），发现"变了的回答中有多大比例变错"强烈依赖任务——MBPP+ 仅 0.023、MMLU-Pro 0.060、held-out TriviaQA 高达 0.59——且几乎所有伤害都落在"采样多次答案有时会错"的脆弱 item 上，因此应当校验缓存块本身而非监控输出。

> **TL;DR:** Using software fault injection into vLLM 0.29.0 serving Qwen3-8B (BF16), the authors flip bit 6 or bit 15 of a single value-cache element in one of the 96 prefix-cache tokens shared by all requests, and score 188,340 flip–item draws on MBPP+, TriviaQA, MMLU-Pro and a policy-refusal task. The harmful fraction of changes h/T spans more than an order of magnitude — 0.023 on MBPP+ (h=2.9e-3), 0.060 on MMLU-Pro (h=38e-3) and 0.59 on held-out TriviaQA (h=3.1e-3) at bit 6 — and essentially all harm concentrates on the 0.11 (TriviaQA) / 0.50 (MMLU-Pro) of items that are sometimes wrong under 16-sample self-consistency, which means a defender can pre-screen fragile traffic without injecting any fault, and that output monitoring cannot distinguish harmed responses from ordinary errors.

## 核心贡献

- **把"变化"推进到"伤害"：** 之前会议版本只统计有多少回答被改变（T）。本文定义 $h = P(\text{翻转后错误} \mid \text{无翻转时正确})$ 与 $h/T$（变化中有害的比例），并在四个任务上给出带双向聚类 bootstrap 置信区间的估计。
- **h/T 跨任务差一个数量级以上：** bit 6 上 MBPP+ 为 0.023、MMLU-Pro 为 0.060、held-out TriviaQA 为 0.59；bit 15 上分别为 0.019、0.062、0.60。两种比特之间无显著差异（配对差值区间跨零）。
- **给出伤害落在哪里的可测预测器：** 伤害几乎完全落在"16 次采样的答案有时错误"的 fragile item 上（held-out TriviaQA 中这类 item 只占 0.11，却承担 100% 的有害抽样；MMLU-Pro 上占 0.50，承担 627/659）；而"变化"落在 top-2 token 概率接近（margin 小）的 item 上。AUC 分析显示 margin 预测"变化"、sampled error 预测"伤害"。
- **恶化了对输出的可观测性：** bit 6/15 的翻转全部属于 partial divergence；除 MMLU-Pro 有 303/659 有害回答没闭合 `</answer>` 标签外，其余任务上每个有害回答都保持了必需格式，看起来就是普通错误；policy 任务上 30 个翻转坐标中有 11 个让模型在完成规定拒答之后仍追加医学断言。
- **据此重估严重性并主张块级校验：** 按 CVSS 3.1 重新打分，向量由 AV:P 改为 AV:L、A 由 None 改为 High，基础分从 1.8 升到 5.3（仅静默比特则 2.5）；结论是应在缓存块上做校验和，而非监控输出。

## Key Contributions

- **Harm per change, not just change.** Defines $h=\Pr(\text{post-injection wrong}\mid\text{baseline correct})$ and reports $h/T$ with two-way cluster bootstrap intervals over 188,340 draws, 30 coordinates per bit, on four task types (code, short-answer, multiple-choice, policy refusal).
- **Task-dependent damage quantified.** $h/T$ at bit 6 is 0.023 [0, 0.062] on MBPP+, 0.060 [0.046, 0.076] on held-out MMLU-Pro, and 0.59 [0.40, 0.76] on held-out TriviaQA — a >25× spread, with MBPP+ and TriviaQA intervals non-overlapping. Bit 15 replicates the pattern (0.019 / 0.062 / 0.60), and no bit difference is detectable.
- **A predictor that needs no fault injection.** Harm concentrates on *fragile* items, identified as those with nonzero sampled-error rate over 16 samples. On held-out TriviaQA fragile items are 0.11 of the pool but carry 204/204 harmful draws; on MMLU-Pro 627/659; on the smaller initial tasks 20/20 (MBPP+), 19/19 (TriviaQA) and 12/12 (policy).
- **Changes and harm dissociate.** Change (T) is predicted better by the minimum log-prob margin between the top two candidates; harm is predicted better by sampled error on every task except TriviaQA, where nearly all changed items are also harmed.
- **Detection implication.** Harmed outputs keep the required format almost everywhere and resemble ordinary errors, so integrity must be checked at the cached block. The conference version's checksum detected 120/120 injected flips with no false positives at negligible throughput cost.

## Methodology

**Setting.** Qwen3-8B in BF16 on an NVIDIA DGX Spark, served by vLLM 0.29.0 with prefix caching on (16-token blocks), asynchronous scheduling disabled, no cache salt, non-thinking mode. Every request begins with one fixed shared prefix of 96 tokens (six full blocks) split into three 32-token parts: a system policy ("do not give medical advice"), a format instruction ("put your answer in `<answer>…</answer>`"), and a customer-service boilerplate sentence.

**Fault model.** A co-located tenant with ordinary privileges on a GDDR6 GPU lacking MIG partitioning performs one bit flip in a prefix block (Rowhammer), with *ideal bit targeting*: bit position chosen, flipped element random. The harness emulates this by XOR-ing one BF16 element of the model's value cache with a single-bit mask. Only bit 6 (mantissa MSB, scaling $v$ by $1+\delta$ with $1/4<|\delta|\le 1/2$) and bit 15 (sign) are studied, because the conference version showed the "silent" bits do not collapse outputs; bit 14 is flipped only as an injection sanity check (it collapsed 2 of 3 coordinates).

**Run flow.** Per flip: cache warm-up → baseline generation → (for fresh requests) a second warm-up that evicts own-prompt blocks and recomputes the prefix → bit flip → post-injection generation → verify the block is still cached. Baseline and post-injection outputs were bit-identical in every no-flip run, so any difference is attributable to the flip. Returning requests reuse their own-prompt KV blocks and thus read the corrupted prefix only for generated tokens and a few final prompt tokens; fresh requests recompute everything from the corrupted prefix, resembling a first conversation turn. Fresh is the default.

**Tasks.** MBPP+ (all problems except one with a faulty reference solution, ≤512 tokens, pass all tests), TriviaQA five-shot (random items, ≤512 tokens, alias match), MMLU-Pro (1,000 items, ≤1024 tokens, reference letter), and 100 hand-written borderline medical requests for which a refusal is correct and any self-authored medical claim is incorrect (confirmed by hand). An output that never closes `</answer>` is incorrect except on policy.

**Measures.** $C$ = draws whose baseline is correct; $h=\Pr(\text{wrong}\mid C)$; $T=\Pr(\text{changed}\mid C)$; $h/T$ = harmful fraction of changes. A *draw* is one coordinate × one item. Pools are items answered correctly at selection; because batch composition shifts after selection, a few pool items fall outside $C$. Comparisons between fresh and returning use $D\subseteq C$ (correct under both). Intervals come from a two-way cluster bootstrap over coordinates and items (pigeonhole bootstrap), paired where runs share coordinates or pools; Bonferroni-adjusted intervals (97.5% / 98.75%) are used for confirmatory claims.

**Predictors and pre-registered hypotheses.** Margin = smallest top-1/top-2 log-prob gap over the baseline output up to `</answer>`. Sampled error = fraction of 16 sampled responses that are incorrect; an item is *fragile* if that fraction is > 0. H1: harmful draws concentrate on fragile items. H2: $h_\text{fragile} > h_\text{rest}$. H3: $T_\text{fresh} > T_\text{returning}$. H4: $h_\text{fresh} > h_\text{returning}$. Held-out stages (new TriviaQA pool of 5,000; MMLU-Pro 1,000 chosen partly because a 200-item pilot met a pre-set harm criterion) exist to test H2–H4 on unseen items.

**Scale.** Held-out per-bit draws in $C$: 17,880 for MMLU-Pro and 64,800 for TriviaQA; initial stages 6,780 (MBPP+), 2,760 (TriviaQA), 2,670 (policy). 30 coordinates per bit, 10 in each of the three prefix parts.

## Results & Highlights

- **The harmful fraction of changes is strongly task-dependent.** At bit 6, $h/T$ = 0.023 [0, 0.062] on initial MBPP+, 0.060 [0.046, 0.076] on held-out MMLU-Pro, and 0.59 [0.40, 0.76] on held-out TriviaQA — the MBPP+ and TriviaQA intervals do not overlap, a >25× spread between "bit flips are mostly harmless" and "most changes are wrong answers". Bit 15 gives 0.019 / 0.062 / 0.60, and the paired bit-15-minus-bit-6 difference in $h/T$ has an interval containing zero on every task.
- **Harm rates and change rates separate just as sharply.** At bit 6, $h$ = 2.9e-3 [0, 8.0e-3] on MBPP+ versus 38e-3 [29e-3, 49e-3] on MMLU-Pro and 3.1e-3 [1.7e-3, 4.8e-3] on held-out TriviaQA, while $T$ = 0.13, 0.64 and 0.0053 respectively. MMLU-Pro changes 5× more outputs than MBPP+ yet has a *lower* harmful fraction; TriviaQA changes 25× fewer outputs than MBPP+ yet each change is ~26× more likely to be a wrong answer.
- **Batch-composition noise is a useful null control.** With no fault at all, merely changing batch composition changes 0.11 of MBPP+ items and 0.17 of policy items — comparable to the flip's $T$ = 0.13 and 0.17 — but makes *none* of them wrong; on MMLU-Pro it makes 0.015 of items wrong versus 0.038 for a flip. Change-rate studies, the authors argue, would therefore count a large volume of harmless perturbations as damage.
- **All harm lands on fragile items.** On held-out TriviaQA, fragile items are 0.11 of the pool yet carry 204/204 harmful draws at bit 6 (and 208/208 at bit 15); $h_\text{fragile}$ = 28e-3 versus 0 on the rest, difference 28e-3 [16e-3, 43e-3]. On held-out MMLU-Pro, 627/659 harmful draws fall on the 0.50 fragile share, $h_\text{fragile}$ = 73e-3 vs 3.7e-3 for the rest, difference 70e-3 [52e-3, 89e-3]. H2 therefore holds on both held-out tasks. The initial tasks show the same pattern (20/20, 19/19, 12/12) but were exploratory.
- **Output-level detection is largely defeated.** At bits 6 and 15 every flip is in the partial-divergence class (never a collapse, per the conference taxonomy). Among harmed responses, only MMLU-Pro's tag-less responses break format — 303 of 659 harmful draws at bit 6, i.e. 356 keep the closing tag; on MBPP+, TriviaQA and policy *every* harmed response kept it. On the policy task, 11 of 30 bit-6 coordinates and 8 of 30 bit-15 coordinates let the model add a medical claim after the required refusal (12 and 9 harmful draws respectively), with concrete examples: linking a stiff neck to pillow or sleeping position, and saying redness can indicate an infection needing treatment — both on just 2 of the 100 policy items.
- **Fresh requests change more but are not reliably harmed more.** H3 (fresh $T$ > returning $T$) holds on both held-out tasks (TriviaQA +0.0028 [0.0013, 0.0045]; MMLU-Pro +0.041 [0.019, 0.065]) and on initial MBPP+ at bit 15 (+0.028 [0.001, 0.055]). H4 (fresh $h$ > returning $h$) holds only on held-out TriviaQA (+1.6e-3 [0.5e-3, 2.8e-3]); on MMLU-Pro the fresh-minus-returning harm difference is +3.6e-3 [−2.0e-3, 9.3e-3], i.e. not significant.
- **Severity rescoring and countermeasure.** Under CVSS 3.1 the vector moves to AV:L/AC:H/PR:L/UI:N/S:U/C:N/I:L/A:H with base score 5.3 (up from 1.8; silent-bits-only flips would score 2.5). The conference version's per-block checksum, verified before each use, caught all 120 injected flips with zero false positives at negligible cost, bounding damage from $h\times N$ to $h\times$ batch size.
- **Validity ceilings the authors quantify themselves.** Alias matching overstates TriviaQA's $h/T$: 0.59 by the automatic scorer versus 0.47 by their own reading. A with-probability-0.95-correct item is misclassified as non-fragile 0.44 of the time with only 16 samples, making the H2 test conservative.

## Authors & Credibility

Yuji Yamamoto and Satoshi Matsuura, Institute of Science Tokyo (Matsuura leads a well-known distributed/HPCC systems group). The paper is the journal extension of a SECRYPT 2026 conference paper by the same authors, and the lineage matters: the fault-injection harness, the shared-prefix threat model and the checksum countermeasure are inherited and cited, while this paper adds the harm measurement, the fragile-item analysis and the CVSS reassessment. That is an honest framing of the delta rather than a repackaging claim.

Methodologically the paper is unusually disciplined for a security measurement study. Hypotheses H1–H4 were fixed before the held-out runs; a 200-item MMLU-Pro pilot had to meet a pre-declared harm criterion before the 1,000-item held-out stage was built; two-way cluster bootstrap accounts for the fact that draws sharing a coordinate or an item are correlated; Bonferroni-adjusted intervals are reported for the confirmatory family. They report their own scoring error (0.59 vs 0.47 on TriviaQA), report the cost of the 16-sample fragility screen, and give the residual interval widths that are wide because of small harm counts. Disclosure is explicit: the chapter and experiment code were written with Claude Opus 5.5 assistance, reviewed and endorsed by both authors; funding from Palo Alto Networks with no role in the study; the vulnerability was reported to vLLM maintainers via a GitHub Security Advisory on 8 April 2026 and declined as implausible.

The credibility gap is empirical rather than procedural: one model, one prefix, two bit positions, BF16 only, and software emulation of a fault that has never been physically demonstrated at bit 15.

## Critical Assessment

The paper's central methodological move — conditioning harm on baseline correctness, $h = \Pr(\text{wrong}\mid C)$ — is the right one and is what makes the >25× task spread interpretable, but it also silently narrows what is being measured. Items the model already gets wrong are excluded from $C$, so the deployment-level statement "a flip harms $h\times N$ requests" only holds if the traffic's baseline accuracy is high; on a task where the model is at 50%, half the traffic is outside the denominator and the paper's $h$ says nothing about it. The authors partially acknowledge this in the fresh-request limitation (fragile MMLU-Pro items drop out of $C$ under fresh requests) but never give the missing quantity: the joint distribution of fragility and baseline correctness.

The task suite inflates the spread it headlines. MBPP+ and TriviaQA differ not just in difficulty but in scoring strictness: MBPP+ is pass-all-tests (a near-binary, low-variance signal where a changed function usually still passes or fails loudly), while TriviaQA is alias-matched five-shot short answer, a format where a single wrong token flips the label. The authors concede that alias matching overstates TriviaQA's $h/T$ (0.59 vs 0.47 by their reading), which is a 20% relative correction on the paper's most dramatic number — and the corrected 0.47 still sits in a regime where the scoring rule, not the fault, drives much of the variance. Their own null control sharpens the worry in the other direction: batch-composition noise alone changed 0.17 of policy items and 0.11 of MBPP+ items, statistically indistinguishable from the flip's $T$, so "changed" is a very low bar and any study reporting only $T$ is measuring mostly noise. That is a genuinely useful negative result, but it also means the initial-stage $T$ values in Table 4 carry little information about the fault.

The policy task is the weakest empirical leg. All policy harm comes from 2 of 100 hand-written requests — the paper says so plainly ("all policy harm falls on two of them") — so the "11 of 30 flips let the model add a medical claim" statistic is effectively a statement about two prompts, with no reference answer and a correctness judgment made by one author by hand. It is a vivid illustration, not an estimate. Similarly, the initial TriviaQA stage has only 19–20 harmful draws total, producing $h/T$ intervals of [0, 1]; those numbers appear in the abstract's framing as "0.83 at the sign bit" but carry essentially no information.

The MMLU-Pro harm definition conflates two failure modes. 303 of 659 harmful draws at bit 6 fail because the response never closes `</answer>`, which a trivial format check would catch — the authors note the harm that evades such a check is "about half of $h$", but the headline 0.060 is the un-halved number. Since they argue in Section 6.3 that output monitoring cannot substitute for block checks, they should lead with the format-passing harm rate, which is the quantity that actually supports the argument. As written, the paper's own strongest counterargument (a regex check catches nearly half of MMLU-Pro harm) is buried in a limitations paragraph.

The fault model is average-case dressed as adversarial. "Ideal bit targeting" fixes the bit position but draws the flipped element at random — 30 coordinates, 10 per prefix part — so the reported $h$ is the mean over random elements, not the maximum an attacker would search for. An adversary who can pick the element (and can run the injection repeatedly, as the threat model allows over a long-lived block) would take the max, and nothing in the paper bounds that. The paper also never tests multiple simultaneous flips or a flip that survives across a batch boundary, and the checksum countermeasure is asserted from the conference version (120/120 detected) rather than re-measured here — including its behavior under the "flip between check and use" window the authors themselves flag.

Finally, the physical-attack chain has a missing link the authors are commendably explicit about: Rowhammer flips have been shown at bits 6 and 14 of 16-bit values but *not* at bit 15, and no work has placed a flip in a long-lived prefix block (CacheTrap reached only a single request's short-lived cache). Combined with the fact that 94 ECC-detected errors accompany one silent corruption on an A6000 and that no flips have been shown on HBM GPUs, the practical threat today is narrow. The vLLM maintainers' rejection of the advisory as implausible is defensible on current hardware; the paper's value lies in pre-positioning the measurement and the block-checksum defense for when the hardware assumptions catch up, not in demonstrating an exploitable attack.

## Strengths

- Clean conceptual separation of *change* from *harm*, with a formal definition ($h$, $T$, $h/T$) and a null control (batch-composition noise) that shows why change-rate studies overcount damage.
- Pre-registered hypotheses with held-out item pools, Bonferroni-adjusted confirmatory intervals, and two-way cluster bootstrap that respects the coordinate × item dependence — a level of statistical hygiene rare in fault-injection security papers.
- The fragile-item finding is actionable: a defender can screen traffic by 16-sample self-consistency *without any injection*, obtaining a per-deployment estimate of how much damage a flip would do. The AUC analysis supports this with the right predictor for the right target (margin → change, sampled error → harm).
- Honest self-correction: reporting 0.47 instead of 0.59 for TriviaQA, disclosing that 16 samples miss rare errors with a worked example (0.95-correct item classified non-fragile 44% of the time), and stating that only 2 policy items carry all policy harm.
- Concrete, quantified CVSS reassessment (1.8 → 5.3, with the silent-bit-only variant at 2.5) rather than a vague severity claim.
- Real disclosure process: vLLM GitHub Security Advisory on 8 April 2026, before conference publication, with the maintainers' rejection reported as-is.

## Limitations

- One model (Qwen3-8B), one 96-token prefix, one datatype (BF16), two bit positions, single flips only, value cache only — no key-cache flips, no multi-bit or correlated flips, no cross-batch persistence test.
- Average-case rather than worst-case: elements are drawn at random within a bit position, so an attacker who searches for the worst element is not bounded by these numbers.
- The headline $h/T$ spread is entangled with scoring strictness across tasks (pass-all-tests vs alias match vs letter match), and the authors' own correction (0.59 → 0.47 on TriviaQA) shows the sensitivity.
- MMLU-Pro's $h$ includes format violations that a trivial tag check would catch; roughly half of its harm evades such a check.
- The policy task rests on 2 of 100 items with a hand-adjudicated, reference-free definition of "medical claim", giving no statistical resolution.
- Fragility screening requires reference answers and 16 extra samples per item, so it is only applicable to scorable traffic — not free-form chat, which is precisely where a system-prompt-carrying prefix dominates.
- Physical realizability is unproven: no Rowhammer flips at bit 15, no flips placed in a long-lived prefix block, and ECC (recommended by NVIDIA) converts most flips into detectable errors.
- The countermeasure's cost and detection rate are inherited from the conference version and not re-measured in this setting.

## Relevance

直接命中 "Inference Reliability & SDC Detection" 核心方向：在 vLLM 前缀缓存这一真实、被多请求共享的 KV block 上做单比特故障注入，并首次把"输出变化"推进到"任务正确性伤害"（$h$、$h/T$）。其"伤害集中在采样不稳定的脆弱 item""输出监控无法区分、必须做块级校验"的结论，对 SDC 检测与容错执行的设计有直接借鉴价值；同时它给出的 batch-composition 噪声基线，也可作为其他数值非确定性研究的对照参考。

## Questions for Further Exploration

1. What is the joint distribution of fragility and baseline correctness in real traffic, and what fraction of a production workload is in $C$ at all? Without it, $h\times N$ cannot be converted into an expected number of wrong answers.
2. How much does worst-case element selection raise $h$? An attacker allowed to probe the block (the threat model already permits this) would search for the coordinate with maximal harm; the ratio of max to mean is the missing adversarial multiplier.
3. Would the fragile-item predictor transfer to free-form chat, where no reference answer exists? Self-consistency without a scorer is only a proxy, and the paper's screening recipe depends on scoring.
4. Does the harm pattern hold for key-cache flips, for multi-bit flips, and for higher-precision caches (FP16/FP32) where the exponent field is wider and bit 14-like collapses become more likely?
5. What is the actual cost/coverage of the block checksum under continuous batching — specifically the window between verification and use, and whether a per-batch rather than per-request check still bounds damage at $h\times$ batch size?
6. If a format check would catch half of MMLU-Pro harm, would a cheap output sanity gate plus block checksums dominate either alone, and what does that imply for serving stacks that already do structured decoding?
