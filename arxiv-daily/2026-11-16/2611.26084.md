> **一句话总结：** 作者在一个先验与正确答案都可精确计算的玩具解谜世界里从零训小 GPT，证明 GRPO 的成败由"训练前可算的一个数"决定——基座模型在多少组尝试里能碰到新动作并赢，约 35 个这样的成功组给出五成把握；低于此阈值 RL 只会强化旧习惯，高于此阈值则一举学会并泛化到全部九条规则。

> **TL;DR:** Trained from scratch on 9M Bayesian-played puzzle games (10.7M-param GPTs, 1.3M–85M in ablations), base models reproduce the Bayesian posterior almost exactly (22%/56% switch after 1/2 failures vs. 21%/57% ideal), yet GRPO post-training moves them to the *better decision* rather than the familiar one, closing 74%–95% of the reward gap to the dynamic-programming optimum on 2,000 held-out problems. When the switch is rare, however, 210 runs split cleanly into 138 that learn nothing and 53 that learn all nine rules; a pre-training count of *expected groups containing a winning switch* (D_rep) predicts take-off on 400 runs, with even odds at ~35 groups, and beats counts of attempts or distinct problems by ≥12 log-likelihood points in all 13 recipes (910 runs) — so "RL only sharpens" is a budget statement, not a property of RL.

## 核心贡献

- **构造了一个"先验已知"的世界**：解谜规则即数据，语料全部由脚本写就（900 万局、516–548M token、108 词表），由一个贝叶斯玩家按后验 π(k) 出牌，因此先验强度、证据强度与最优决策三者都可精确求解，并把模型的切换概率 s(k) 与理想后验 π(k) 直接对齐比较。
- **纠正了"RL 只会让模型更顽固"的直觉**：在 6 种目标×组大小组合里，RL 都沿着"更好下注"的方向移动，而不是沿"更熟悉"的方向；在可靠规则上第 1 次失败后 s 从 22% 降到 10%，第 2 次失败后从 56% 升到 77%，两个方向同时朝最优走。
- **发现"稀有即全有或全无"的相变式行为**：当语料中正确切换只出现 1/23 时 10 次运行中 9 次学会；降到 1/232 及以下时 90 次运行一次都没学会；210 次运行里 138 次零规则、53 次全九条规则，仅 19 次落在中间。
- **给出训练前可计算的预测量**：定义期望成功组数 D_rep = Σ r(1−(1−q_i)^N)，用最大似然拟合 400 次运行得 b0=−5.75、b1=3.47，约 35 组时五成概率起飞；该量在组大小 4–256、新题/重复题、以及 12 种配方改动（学习率、SGD、KL 惩罚、除以标准差、1.3M–85M）下都优于"数尝试"和"数不同题目"。
- **给出工程可操作的杠杆**：预算×4/×8 可把"学不会"的动作学会（20 次运行 15 次起飞，预测 15.1）；重复同一批题目比扩大题池更有效；从基座重启会浪费已有进展（1/2/4/8 次重启 → 9/7/5/3 次成功）。

## Key Contributions

- **A controlled world where the prior is exact.** Puzzle rules are data; 9M games are played by a scripted Bayesian player, so corpus-level exposure, reliability (ν) and rarity (ρ) are dials rather than unknowns, and the optimal action at every decision point is computable in closed form.
- **Reframes the "RL only sharpens" debate as a measurable quantity.** Rather than asking whether RL discovers new behaviour, the paper asks how many *groups of attempts containing a win* the base model can be expected to produce — a number computable before training.
- **Separates two failure modes that are usually conflated.** "RL makes agents stubborn" and "RL cannot discover rare actions" turn out to be the same phenomenon at different points of one logistic curve, with a sharp all-or-nothing transition at the run level.
- **Establishes that the transition is a run-level, not decision-level, event.** A run that takes off learns the abstract skill "after the default fails, try the other tool" and applies it to rules where the specific action was never sampled (one rule's switch probability rose 93 points despite never being tried).
- **Quantifies what each recipe change costs in the same currency.** Lower LR ×2.6, SGD ×2.2, KL β=1 ×1.5, advantage/std ×1.4, 85M model ×1.1 — all expressed as "successful groups needed relative to our setup".

## Methodology

**The world.** One room, 1–3 obstacles, two candidate tools per obstacle. Nine test rules plus 30 filler rules, each pairing obstacle O with default tool D (key→chest). In the normal world D works with probability 1−ε (ε=0.2) and the alternative A never works; in a changed world D never works and A always does. Every failure yields the identical observation "nothing", so a single failure is genuinely ambiguous. A is drawn from another rule's default tool, so both candidates are ordinary objects and only the learned O–D link distinguishes them. Each obstacle is changed with probability ν; after k failures the Bayesian posterior is π(k) = ν / (ν + (1−ν)·0.2^k), and switching beats holding once π(k) > τ = (1−ε)/(2−ε) ≈ 0.44. With ν=0.05: 5% → 21% → 57% → 87% after 0/1/2/3 failures.

**Measurement.** At fixed decision prefixes (k = 0..3 failures, 100 instances per rule and k, varying A / filler obstacles / item order), the authors teacher-force every valid action, sum token log-probabilities up to the action separator, and compute s(k) = p(A)/(p(A)+p(D)). Base and RL models are scored on *identical* instances, so every reported change is paired. Two distances are tracked separately: |s − π| (calibration against the ideal believer) and |s − 1[π > τ]| (quality of the decision).

**Base models.** nanoGPT-style: 6 layers / 6 heads / width 384 / context 256 → 10.7M params (plus 1.3M and 85M variants), trained for exactly one pass over a corpus of 9M games (516–548M tokens), one token per word, 108-token vocabulary. Exposure is set by the share of games containing a test rule (90k / 9k / 900 games); rarity is set by mixing a Bayesian player (share ρ) with a "late player" who never switches before the 4th failure, so the corpus shows the switch after two failures at rate ≈0.44ρ, from 1-in-23 down to 1-in-23,179. Controls: ≥96.5% success on 200 sampled normal-world games, validation loss drift <0.3% over the last fifth of training, and an unbiasedness check showing that 20 sampled episodes reproduce the exact s(k).

**RL.** GRPO with no KL penalty and no reference model by default, advantage = reward − group mean (not divided by the group spread), one gradient step per batch of N attempts, AdamW at 3e-5, temperature 1, 200 steps × 512 attempts = 102,400 attempts. Reward is 1 for leaving within a step limit drawn uniformly from 4–7, and crucially the limit is chosen so that the decision after k failures is the *last chance* — which makes "switch when π(k) > τ" exactly reward-maximizing. A rule counts as learned if s rises by >0.05 with a paired 95% CI; a run "takes off" if it learns more than half of the nine rules.

**The count.** For each problem the run will see, an exact estimator computes q, the probability that one temperature-1 attempt switches to A after 2–3 failures *and then wins*. From q the paper builds three pre-training counts: attempts D_att = Σ rNq_i, distinct problems D_prob = Σ(1−(1−q_i)^{rN}), and groups D_rep = Σ r(1−(1−q_i)^N). Take-off is modelled as P = 1 − exp(−exp(b0 + b1 log10 D)), fitted by maximum likelihood.

## Results & Highlights

- **Base models match the ideal Bayesian almost perfectly, and "well-exposed" means within 2 points.** For a rule that changes 5% of the time, the base model puts 22% on the hammer after one failure and 56% after two, versus 21% and 57% from π(k); across all conditions the average error of a well-exposed model is <2 percentage points, and at high exposure it reproduces 95% of the posterior's reliable-vs-unreliable gap. Exposure cuts the other way than expected: rules seen in only 900 games produce a *flatter* curve (the model hedges more, not less).
- **RL moves to the better bet, not the familiar one, and closes most of the reward gap.** After one failure of a reliable rule the share on the hammer falls 22% → 10%; after two failures it rises 56% → 77%; for an unreliable rule the one-failure share rises 67% → 84%. On 2,000 held-out problems scored against a dynamic-programming optimum, RL closes **74%–95% of the reward gap** between base model and optimal policy, across all six objective × group-size combinations.
- **Rarity produces a cliff, not a slope.** With the switch shown in 1 of 23 corpus decisions, 9 of 10 small-group runs learn it and RL closes 83% of the gap to optimal; at 1 in 232 or rarer, **0 of 90 runs** learn it, and RL *removes a quarter to a half* of the little probability the base model already assigned to the hammer — post-training strictly worse than doing nothing.
- **Runs are bimodal.** Of 210 runs, 138 learn none of the nine rules and 53 learn all nine; only 19 land in between. Take-off generalizes abstractly: in one run the hammer was never sampled on a particular rule, yet that rule's switch probability rose by 93 percentage points — so what is reinforced is the skill "switch after the default fails", not the specific action.
- **One count predicts which runs take off.** Fitted on 400 runs, the logistic-in-log-count law gives even odds at **~35 expected successful groups** (Figure 1 shows ~36), 1-in-10 at ~10 groups, 9-in-10 at ~80 groups; b0 = −5.75, b1 = 3.47. Counts of attempts and of distinct problems both fail by construction: **groups of 256 attempts and groups of 4 make the same 102,400 attempts, yet 1 of 10 vs. 9 of 10 runs take off**.
- **Repetition is a new group; restarts are a loss.** Seeing the same 400 problems 8 times instead of once moves take-off from 1 of 10 to 10 of 10 (while 1,600 distinct problems over 4× the steps stays at 6 of 10, ruling out "more steps" as the cause). Splitting the same budget into 1/2/4/8 restarts from the base model gives 9/7/5/3 of 10 seeds taking off at least once.
- **"Rare" means "under-budgeted".** A switch at 1-in-232 that no run learned at 1× budget is learned at 4× and 8×: 15 of 20 runs took off versus 15.1 predicted by the count. Repeating problems makes the count a *lower* bound, since the per-problem success chance grew six-fold after 100 steps on problems seen 8 times (vs. 2.5-fold on unseen ones).
- **The count survives 12 recipe changes but the threshold does not.** Across 910 runs in 13 settings, counting groups beats counting problems and attempts by **at least 12 points of log-likelihood** in every setting; a single shared slope fits all 910 runs (per-setting slopes give p = 0.46). Cost in groups: LR 4× lower ×2.6, SGD ×2.2, KL β=1 ×1.5, advantage/std ×1.4, 85M model ×1.1, no clipping ×1.0.
- **Real-model probe (OLMo-3-7B, Appendix C).** Across the nine rules the rank correlation between prior strength and switch-after-one-failure is **−0.97** (95% switching for weak rules vs. 74% for strong), and post-training widens the spread of prior strengths from 0.3 to 3.8 orders of magnitude — but the final model wins only 60% of normal-world games, almost all losses being invalid actions, so RL could not be run.

## Authors & Credibility

Four authors, all at the University of Deusto (Spain): Ruben Sanchez-Corcuera, Unai Zulaika, Aritz Bilbao-Jayo, Miguel Fernandez-de-Retana. Deusto is a solid but not top-tier NLP/ML group; this is a single-lab, single-paper study with no external collaborators from the RL-post-training community. That is visible in the framing: the work is careful and unusually well-controlled experimentally, but it engages with the "RL only sharpens" literature (Yue et al. 2025, Zhao et al. 2025, Chen et al. 2026a, Foster et al. 2025) mostly through citation rather than through direct comparison. The paper is transparent about compute (NVIDIA A6000, bfloat16) and commits to open-sourcing code and data, which is the right posture for a claim this structural. No evidence of overclaiming about scale — the limitations section is unusually candid, including the admission that the fitted law does not extrapolate to recipes outside the fitted range.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty.** The core reframing is genuinely useful: the long-running "does RL reward discovery or just reweighting?" argument (Yue et al. 2025) is usually answered with pass@k curves on math benchmarks, whereas here it is answered with a *pre-training* quantity — how many attempt-groups contain a win — that can be computed before a single gradient step. That connects cleanly to the coverage arguments of Chen et al. (2026a) and Foster et al. (2025), and the D_rep vs. D_prob vs. D_att decomposition is a crisp way to say why group size and problem repetition matter in opposite directions. The finding that the transition is run-level and abstract ("learn the skill, not the action") is the most interesting empirical result and is not something I have seen stated this sharply.

**Rigor.** Experimental hygiene is high for a synthetic study: every change is paired on identical decision points, the s(k) estimator is validated against direct sampling, base models pass explicit behavioural and loss-drift controls, arms are replicated across 10 seeds / 20 corpora, and the predictions in Figure 5 are made from a law fitted on a *different* set of 400 runs. That is a real held-out test, not a post-hoc fit. On the other hand, several important negatives rest on modest n: "gradient clipping and model size make no difference we can detect" comes from 70 runs per setting, and the confidence intervals in Figure 6 (95% bootstrap over 20 corpora) are wide enough that ×1.1 vs. ×1.0 is not a meaningful separation.

**Red flags and gaps.**
1. *The world is engineered so that the RL problem is unusually clean.* The step limit is drawn from 4–7 precisely so that the decision after k failures is the last chance, which makes "switch when π(k) > τ" exactly reward-maximizing. Real agent tasks have no such last-chance structure, no closed-form τ, and delayed, sparse rewards — so the claim that RL "sides with the evidence when the evidence should win" is demonstrated in a regime where the reward signal is a perfect oracle for the Bayes-optimal action.
2. *The all-or-nothing result may be an artifact of the shared relational template.* All 39 rules share one pattern (obstacle, default tool, another rule's default tool as alternative), so every successful group pushes the same mechanism. The authors themselves concede in the Discussion that they "have not looked inside the models"; the induction-head / grokking analogy is speculation with no probing, no activation analysis, and no ablation that breaks the shared template (e.g. rules with heterogeneous structure) to test whether the sharp transition survives.
3. *The threshold is not portable, and the authors say so.* Leave-one-setting-out prediction works for settings *between* fitted ones and fails beyond them (lower learning rates, unseen model sizes). Combined with a ~35-group threshold tuned to a 108-token templated language and 10.7M-param models, the practical claim reduces to "the functional form is right, the constant is not" — which is a weaker contribution than the abstract's phrasing implies.
4. *Estimating q is the whole method, and it is only tractable here.* Computing reach requires knowing the correct action and scoring its exact probability at every decision point, which is why the OLMo-3-7B probe collapses to nine single decisions and 60% game-completion. For real RLVR on code or math there is no analogue of "the probability that this attempt switches to the right tool and then wins" that can be computed exactly before training — sampling-based estimates would inherit exactly the rarity problem the count is meant to diagnose.
5. *Missing baselines.* There is no comparison against entropy bonuses, temperature schedules, rejection sampling, or explicit exploration methods — the very interventions a practitioner would use when a run fails to take off. The paper's advice ("more budget, repeat problems, don't restart") is sound but is the cheapest possible advice, and it is not benchmarked against the cheap alternatives.
6. *The "late player" manipulation conflates two things.* Rarity is induced by mixing in a player who only switches from the 4th failure on; this changes not only how often the switch appears but the *conditional structure* of the corpus (early-switch examples become systematically rarer relative to late ones). A cleaner manipulation would resample early switches while holding the rest of the trajectory distribution fixed.

**Significance.** If the group-count law generalizes even qualitatively, it reframes a lot of RLVR practice: the question "why didn't GRPO learn this?" becomes "how many groups in this run could possibly have contained a win?", which is checkable. The observation that restarts destroy progress and that repetition beats a wider problem pool is immediately actionable and, in my reading, under-appreciated. But the paper's own scope statement — synthetic, templated, ≤85M, one algorithm family, one conflict type — means this is a mechanistic hypothesis with strong internal evidence, not yet a law about frontier post-training.

## Strengths

- Exceptionally clean experimental design: the prior is *written*, not estimated, so "the model hedged like its corpus" is a measurement rather than an inference.
- Paired before/after measurement on identical decision points removes the usual confound in "did RL change the model?" studies.
- A genuine held-out prediction: the take-off law is fitted on 400 runs and then used to predict the outcomes of the group-size, repetition, restart and budget experiments in Figure 5.
- The count decomposition (attempts vs. problems vs. groups) is conceptually sharp and explains two otherwise puzzling results — why 256-attempt groups underperform 4-attempt groups at equal budget, and why repeating problems helps more than the count predicts.
- Honest limitations section, including the failure to extrapolate the fitted law and the inability to run RL on OLMo-3-7B.
- The 93-percentage-point gain on a rule where the action was never sampled is a striking, memorable demonstration that the learned object is a skill rather than an action.
- 13 recipes / 910 runs is a serious robustness sweep by the standards of mechanistic RL papers.

## Limitations

- Synthetic 108-token templated language and models of 1.3M–85M parameters; nothing here tests natural language, real pre-training priors, or frontier scale.
- "Prior strength" is operationalized as corpus exposure, not as a Bayesian prior or as parametric knowledge in the sense used in the knowledge-conflict literature, so the mapping to real "the model learned this in pre-training" claims is indirect.
- The step-limit construction (4–7 moves) makes the reward-maximizing action coincide exactly with the Bayes-optimal one; the clean "RL sides with the evidence" result depends on this.
- Only GRPO (plus KL / advantage-normalization variants), no PPO, no reference-model-free vs. reference-model comparison as a primary axis, one reward type, one horizon.
- The ~35-group threshold is world- and recipe-specific; the shared-slope fit predicts interpolated settings but not extrapolated ones.
- No mechanistic evidence for the all-or-nothing explanation; the induction-head / grokking analogy is untested.
- Small per-arm sample sizes (10 runs) make the null results (model size, gradient clipping) weak evidence.
- No comparison to standard exploration interventions (entropy bonus, temperature, rejection sampling), so the practical recommendation is not benchmarked against alternatives.
- Real-model validation is limited to single decisions in OLMo-3-7B; the model cannot play well enough for RL, so the central claim remains untested outside the toy world.

## Relevance

直接命中「RL Post-Training Dynamics & Failures」：它把 GRPO 的"学不会 / 学得会"归约为一个训练前可计算的覆盖量，并给出 collapse-to-prior、restart 有害、重复题目优于扩池等可复用的失败诊断与缓解线索。对做 RLVR 训练稳定性与失败归因的读者，这是本周最值得读方法细节的一篇。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- Does the group-count law survive when the 39 rules no longer share one relational template — i.e. is the sharp all-or-nothing transition a property of RL or of the templated world's single shared mechanism?
- Can q be estimated well enough in a real RLVR setting (code, math) for the count to be predictive, given that exact scoring requires knowing the correct action at every decision point?
- What happens with PPO, asynchronous/off-policy RL, or a reference-model KL at realistic β (0.04) rather than the deliberately harsh β=1 stress test?
- Would an entropy bonus or temperature schedule shift the threshold, or does it only change *which* groups contain a win without changing the required count?
- Why do larger groups underperform at equal budget beyond the counting explanation — is there an additional optimization effect (fewer distinct problems per step, larger gradient variance) on top of the group-level signal sparsity?
- Does the "restarting hurts" result hold when the restart preserves optimizer state or replays the successful groups, rather than resetting to the base model?
- In OLMo-3-7B, is the −0.97 rank correlation between prior strength and switch-after-one-failure stable across renderers and prompt formats, or is it an artifact of the single chat template used?
