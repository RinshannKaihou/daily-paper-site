> **一句话总结：** 论文把网络拥塞控制搬到 LLM 推理调度上：在真正饱和的资源处做"快速失败"的 Cap（超额即返回 HTTP 429），再用 Track 这个只吃 429 反馈的 MIMD 控制器在容量域内上下试探，从而不需要预测输出长度、也不需要人工重调全局速率；在 Meta 生产环境的同流量 A/B 中，分类器负载用 43% 更少的 H100（39 对 68）跑出同等覆盖与打分，稳态 replay 下 goodput 与固定速率的差距在 1% 以内而拒绝率只有其约四分之一。

> **TL;DR:** Meta's traffic shaper implements Cap-and-Track for asynchronous post/live-video understanding inference. In replay on a real A100 with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, removing the per-replica cap collapses goodput from 8.64 req/s to 0 by minute 4 even though the engine still runs at its throughput plateau (14,557 vs 14,572 classifier tokens/s) — i.e. busy but useless. With caps, Track reaches 108.5 req/s/host against 107.0 for a fixed rate at 100% of measured capacity and 109.5 at 120%, while drawing 3.8% refusals versus the 120% rate's 15.5% (0.04 vs 0.18 refusals per completion). In a concurrent same-traffic production A/B, the classifier ran on 39 H100s against the precursor's 68 (−43%), and on equal ~600-MI300X fleets zero-shot drained every shared backlog first by a median 3 hours with engines at a median 124 of 128 sequences versus 41.

## 核心贡献

- **把 "限流" 拆成两个可组合的原语**：Cap 是部署在真正饱和资源处（模型服务器每副本请求上限、云厂商准入、网关按调用方配额、引擎队列深度感知路由器）的快速失败准入检查，超额立刻返回 429 而不是让请求排队到超时；Track 是每个容量域一个的派发循环，只用 429 的比例作为信号向上探测、向下回退。二者都不需要输出长度预测、不需要引擎内部指标、不需要人工重调的全局速率。
- **给出一个反直觉的失效证据：不设 Cap 时引擎"满负荷但零有效吞吐"**。在真实引擎 replay 中，未设上限的副本 token 吞吐与设上限时几乎相同（14,572 对 14,557 tok/s），但 goodput 在分类器形态第 4 分钟、生成形态第 20 分钟跌到 0 并且不再恢复——请求在模型服务器里排队超过超时后，引擎仍在为已经离开的调用方服务（metastable failure）。丢弃、提前取消都救不回来，只有"拒绝"能救。
- **证明一套极简控制器足以跟随移动的天花板**：15 秒一次、按 429 占比决策（≤2% 升 3%；>50% 乘 0.99，50–65% 乘 0.85，>65% 乘 0.5），部署后经历九次集群扩缩容、一次弹性池抢占（4,163 次抢占，0.0004% 超时）与一次云厂商准入更新，全程没有重新调容量。
- **把"简单规则跑在大规模动态集群上"的工程代价显式化**：一致性哈希放置 + 迟滞、无共识服务的字典序 leader、滚动重启期间冻结速率、被拒请求留在本地 ready 队列而不是回共享队列、安静时段禁止爬升的利用率闸门、重试只在本 worker 内。这些不是 LLM 特有的，但都是让简单规则可用的前提。
- **在两个同流量生产 A/B 上量化收益与代价**：分类器 43% 更少 H100；零样本等规模机队下 backlog 先清空 3 小时、每 GPU 多服务 9% 请求、引擎序列占用 124/128 对 41/128；代价是分类器回填峰值期最低优先级 band 的中位等待从 0.5 小时升到 6.6 小时。

## Key Contributions

- **A two-primitive decomposition of dispatch control.** Cap = fail-fast admission at the resource that actually saturates; Track = one MIMD controller per capacity domain that treats labeled 429s as the only feedback signal. The design deliberately refuses to model inference-engine internals, provider accounting, or output length.
- **A negative result that motivates the cap.** Without a refusal path, an engine sits at its token-throughput plateau while producing zero goodput; the collapse is caused by work that has already timed out still occupying the batch (Bronson-style metastable failure). Dropping or canceling requests before vLLM does not fix it; a CoDel-style queue-delay refusal does (classifier 0.3% above the cap's goodput, generative 1.82 vs 1.83 req/s).
- **Evidence that a 3% multiplicative step is enough.** On an emulated provider, Track holds ~96% of capacity where classic AIMD holds ~66%, and a 10% step is faster to recover (217 s vs 671 s to 95%) but swings 0.222 of capacity versus Track's 0.065 at a 25×-scaled ceiling.
- **Operational machinery for stateful dispatch at fleet scale**: consistent-hash placement sized to the queue's rate, freeze-on-worker-count-change with 10% hysteresis, local retry to avoid re-staging media, quiet-hour utilization gate, and every controller constant as a runtime knob.
- **Production evidence at a scale rarely reported in this literature**: >300M online requests/day, 1.125B in the busiest 24 hours, up to ~16,000 req/s, 45 shaper workers in seven clusters, >4,000 accelerators at peak across six workloads.

## Methodology

The system is Meta's *traffic shaper*, a shared dispatch service for online asynchronous LLM inference in social-media applications (post and per-minute live-video understanding). Producers enqueue into FOQS (Meta's durable priority queue); Track workers lease requests, stage media, and dispatch through a gateway to either an external provider or an internal model server.

**Cap.** A labeled refusal at the constrained resource exposes overload before a wasteful wait. For external providers the 429 is already there. For internal vLLM replicas the model server checks in-flight count and returns an overload exception that the RPC layer maps to HTTP 429; the classifier and zero-shot deployments set `max_num_seqs`=128 and the server's request/processing caps to 140 per replica, so fleet-level admission scales automatically with the number of live replicas and needs no reconfiguration on resize.

**Track.** Every 15 s, one leader worker (lexicographically smallest hostname in fleet discovery — no consensus service) pools fleet-wide successes and 429s per queue and sets each queue's dispatch rate from its refusal share. At or below a 2% target it raises the rate 3%; above it, three cut tiers (×0.99 up to 50%, ×0.85 to 65%, ×0.5 above). The controller is multiplicative in both directions; the target only switches the rate between rising and falling. Only capacity refusals count — timeouts are excluded because a timeout may be a network fault where backing off would idle capacity. A utilization gate blocks increases when dispatch is below 75% of target (otherwise quiet overnight hours let targets climb for days). Rates freeze for 10 polls after a worker-count change with 10% hysteresis, so a rolling restart's duplicate dispatchers do not push the controller into the harder cut tiers.

**Waiting discipline.** Each engine keeps only a short queue behind its running batch; everything else waits durably in FOQS, outside the timeout clock. A refused request returns to the *front of its own worker's ready queue* with media already staged — returning it to the shared queue would bounce it across workers and re-stage media until retry limits fired. Lease expiry is the only path back to FOQS, and a worker only dispatches when the remaining lease exceeds the request timeout, so no request is still running at redelivery.

**Evaluation design.** Three levels: (i) controlled replay on one A100 80GB with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, vLLM 0.23.0, production model-server and gateway builds, Poisson arrivals with fixed seeds, three runs per arm; (ii) a three-host, six-replica real-engine testbed where only dispatch varies (one of two replicas removed for 10 minutes at minute 15); (iii) production, of which exactly two comparisons are concurrent same-traffic A/Bs (classifier on H100, zero-shot on ~600 MI300X), and the rest are labeled observational.

## Results & Highlights

- **No cap ⇒ zero goodput at full token rate.** At ~2× measured capacity, the uncapped arm held 14,572 classifier tokens/s versus the capped arm's 14,557 — the engine was equally busy — yet goodput fell from 9.14 req/s plateau to **0 by minute 4** (classifier shape) and to 0 by minute 20 (generative, plateau 1.85 req/s). With the cap, goodput held at **8.64 req/s** (94% of plateau) and **1.83 req/s**. Median wait in the uncapped arm was 172–180 s against a 100 s timeout.
- **Track matches fixed rates at a stable ceiling and does it with far fewer refusals.** Table C1 (cap 40): Track **108.5 req/s/host** vs **107.0** for a rate fixed at 100% of measured capacity (+1.4%) and **109.5** at 120% (−0.9%); refusals per completion **0.04** vs **0.18** for the 120% rate — the "about a quarter" claim, bought at the cost of a higher whole-run median latency (0.54 s vs 0.34 s at 100%).
- **The trade under a capacity cut is refusals versus recovery time.** With one of two replicas gone for 10 minutes: Track held 54.7 req/s with 0.49 refusals/completion, the 100% rate held 54.9 with 0.97, the 120% rate 54.9 with 1.35. Track needed **220 s** to return to 95% of full capacity after the restore, while the fixed rates returned instantly — the paper's own admission that multiplicative-up control is the weak direction.
- **Production classifier A/B: 39 H100s vs 68 (−43%).** Same traffic, model, prompts and vLLM settings; coverage and scores at parity. The paper decomposes the 29 saved cards: 5 are the precursor's margin, and the precursor began erroring just above its usual online load, at about **two-thirds of its profiled per-card rate** because it had no per-engine cap.
- **Latency parity outside peaks.** Over 8 matched days spanning a four-day backfill, the shaper/precursor end-to-end latency ratio was at most **1.04** in every band at p50/p95/p99, with the median day at 0.997–1.006. On the two peak days the trade is stark: the top band's p99 was **0.35×** the precursor's, but the lowest band waited a median **6.6 hours** versus the precursor's **0.5 hours**.
- **Zero-shot A/B on equal ~600-MI300X fleets: 124 of 128 sequences vs 41.** Engines behind caps plus Track ran a median **124/128** sequences against the precursor's **40.7**, with KV-cache utilization **31.6% vs 13.2%**, serving **1.09×** the model requests per GPU. Backlogs were shared and both paths were backlogged at 8 peaks; the shaper drained first at all 8, by a median of **3 hours**.
- **Reserved provider capacity: 94.8% vs 88.2% at launch, 95.8% over 15 weeks.** Utilization rose from 88.2% (static system, prior 23 days) to 94.8% (first four full metered days), then to 95.8% over 15 weeks after the provider's admission update, and 99.1% across a backlogged week. Refusal share at launch was **28.3%** (first 4 days) / 27.6% (20 days) against the static system's **16%** overall and **24.4%** in its busiest quarter — i.e. the shaper converts spare attempts into utilization rather than eliminating refusals.
- **Pre-launch observational test: 22.6% → 2.8–4.9% refusals within two hours.** The two systems shared the same provider admission and differed only in dispatcher; refusals climbed back to 14.0–21.0% when the static system resumed. An unintended upstream media-resolution change shifted the mix to 1.75 input tokens per output token, and the shaper still held 97–99% of the reservation within a 12-hour ramp.
- **Emulated provider, controller shoot-out.** Before a capacity drop, classic AIMD served ~**66%** of capacity where Track (and a 10% step, and AIMD with Track's decrease) served ~**96%**; during the drop Track drew 0.25 refusals/completion, the 10% step 0.29, classic AIMD 0.12, a fixed rate **2.87**, and Gradient2 **3.63** (its target rate ran to ~2.5× capacity).
- **Operational scale.** Online volume grew from ~160M requests/day to >300M within about a month, then **1.125B** in the busiest 24 hours of a backfill, 308–351M/day over the five days after it drained; nine internal fleet resizes and an elastic pool's 4,163 preemptions with **0.0004%** timeouts; 31 rolling deploys at a median **1.001×** pre-deploy throughput under the freeze.

## Authors & Credibility

Nine authors, all at Meta (one now at Google), writing about a system they built and operate: the paper is unusually concrete about the surrounding infrastructure (FOQS, fleet discovery, consistent-hash placement, RPC-layer overload mapping, vLLM 0.23.0 and 0.29 release behaviour). The provenance is the strongest possible for a production-serving paper — the numbers come from the authors' own deployment, not a benchmark harness — and the paper includes a detailed AI-usage disclosure (Claude/GPT/internal model used for architecture review, harness code, emulator, queries, citation audit, LaTeX and figures; all reported numbers traced to production measurements and experiment logs). Citations are current through 2026 and cover both the overload-control lineage (SEDA, DAGOR, Breakwater, Svalinn, Protego, CoDel, DCTCP, Netflix concurrency-limits, Envoy) and the LLM-serving lineage (Orca, vLLM/PagedAttention, Sarathi-Serve, DistServe, Mooncake, QLM, CONCUR, JITServe). The candor in Table 1 — every production row labeled "observational" or "A/B", with explicit caveats such as "no baseline after the update" and "untested estimate" on the 48-fewer-GPU projection — is a credibility signal, not a weakness.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty.** The individual pieces are borrowed: fail-fast admission is Breakwater/SEDA-style, refusal-ratio pacing is DCTCP-flavoured, per-replica caps exist in Ray Serve and Knative, and vLLM 0.29 shipped `--max-num-queued-reqs` (503) after this deployment. The contribution is the composition and the empirical claim that *nothing else is needed* — no output-length predictor, no engine metrics, no per-workload rate. That is a real and useful claim, but it is an engineering-synthesis paper, not a new mechanism; the paper is honest about this ("Cap-and-Track composes established mechanisms above a serving framework").

**The headline 43% number is a bundle, not a dispatcher effect.** Of the 29 saved H100s, 5 are the precursor's safety margin, and the precursor "began to error just above its usual online load, at about two-thirds of the profiled per-card rate" because it had no per-engine cap. So the comparison conflates (a) enabling a cap, (b) the dispatch policy, and (c) fleet sizing. Appendix A.2 says it plainly: "This A/B tests the whole design. Replay isolates each part." Fair, but readers who quote "43% fewer GPUs from Track" are quoting the bundle.

**Most production evidence is observational, and the most-quoted utilization comparison is confounded.** The 94.8% vs 88.2% reserved-capacity result is a before/after over different windows, explicitly without backlog control — the paper concedes that "utilization tracks backlog, and short-queue hours fell in the evening and at night." Worse for a clean read, the target refusal share was raised from 5% to 30% *during* the launch window, so part of the utilization gain is deliberately purchased with a 28.3% refusal share rather than with better dispatch. Only the classifier and zero-shot comparisons are same-traffic A/Bs, and only classifier/zero-shot are covered.

**The real-engine Track testbed is narrow.** It uses only the classifier request shape, synthetic prompts, a mean of ~247 input tokens with **one-token outputs**, `max_num_seqs`=16 and 16,384 batched tokens. That is a prefill-dominated microbenchmark; the decode-heavy generative regime where output length is genuinely unknown appears only in production observationally. The paper's own zero-shot workload is also prefill-heavy at 17.3 input tokens per output token, and it says the modest +9% per-GPU gain is "because the workload is prefill-heavy" — so the design's central motivation (unknown generative output length) is the least directly measured part.

**Adaptation asymmetry is understated relative to interactive serving.** Losing 40% of capacity draws ~40% refusals, which still falls in the mild ×0.99 tier, and recovery takes about 51 decisions — 13 minutes. The 0.99-tier design means the controller is deliberately slow precisely in the moderate-overload regime; the justification ("each refusal returns fast and the work stays queued") only holds for latency-tolerant asynchronous work. The paper does say it has not applied this to interactive or agent-session traffic, but the abstract's framing is more general than the evidence.

**Missing system-level baselines.** Related work discusses QLM (SLO-ordered queues), Mooncake (early rejection on predicted load), CONCUR (KV-cache-signalled admission), Sarathi-Serve and DistServe, but none is compared against — not in replay, not in emulation. The only controller comparison is against classic AIMD, a 10% step, and Gradient2 on an emulated provider, which is a weak field for a paper whose thesis is "existing admission control is insufficient." A latency-based adaptive concurrency limiter (Envoy/Netflix) on the real engines is argued against rather than measured; the argument (a fail-fast cap flattens the latency signal that limiters need) is plausible and interesting but untested here.

**Hard dependency on a refusal path.** The design "needs only a labeled refusal," but that is exactly what many backends do not offer: providers may block, engines queue silently, and the paper's own uncapped replay shows 27.8–38.0% goodput loss when timeouts substitute for refusals. So the "no engine metrics required" property is conditional on being able to install Caps everywhere — a deployment constraint presented as a design virtue.

**Smaller concerns.** Leader election by lexicographic hostname without consensus tolerates split-brain rather than preventing it ("a brief two-leader overlap only mis-sets a rate"), which is fine for this workload but is a convention, not a guarantee. Two Track controllers sharing one capacity domain are only shown to split evenly (Jain ≥ 0.998) in emulation, and production avoids the case by an operational rule — the fairness property that makes MIMD safe in the general case is untested where it matters. Prefix-aware queue ordering and queue-depth autoscaling are left to future work, and the freshness cost for low-priority work during backfills (44–55% of items within goal; 6.6 h median wait) is real and would be unacceptable in most user-facing settings.

## Strengths

- **Genuine production scale with unusually honest accounting.** >300M requests/day, 1.125B in a peak day, 45 workers, 4,000+ accelerators, nine resizes, 4,163 preemptions — and Table 1 labels every row's evidence class and caveat instead of blurring them.
- **The "no cap ⇒ busy but zero goodput" replay is a first-class contribution.** Holding token throughput constant (14,572 vs 14,557 tok/s) while goodput goes 9.14 → 0 is the cleanest demonstration I have seen that inference overload is a metastable failure, not a throughput problem, and that only refusal (not dropping, not canceling) restores service.
- **The design is small enough to reason about.** Two primitives, one control law, four named constants, all hot-tunable. Most serving papers propose a scheduler with a dozen coupled knobs; this one argues that a 3% multiplicative step and a refusal-ratio target suffice across provider admission, model-server caps, gateway quotas, and a queue-depth router.
- **The operational section earns its space.** Freeze-on-worker-change, local retries with media kept staged, quiet-hour climbing gates, placement sized so a low-rate queue still gets enough attempts to estimate its refusal share — these are the details that decide whether a control loop survives contact with a rolling deploy, and they are rarely written down.
- **Good experimental hygiene where it exists.** Three runs per arm with fixed seeds, both caps 40 and 64 tested (goodput within ~0.8%), a per-replica token budget ablation (matches cap goodput on classifier, −27% p99 service latency, no gain on generative), and the honest note that caps at 2×/4× plateau cost 2–4× service time.

## Limitations

- Only two controlled production comparisons (classifier, zero-shot); the reserved-capacity, on-demand-loss, and pre-launch results are observational before/after windows with acknowledged confounds (backlog, token mix, target-refusal-share changes).
- The real-engine Track testbed is classifier-shaped, synthetic, one-token-output, `max_num_seqs`=16 — decode-heavy generative dispatch is not directly measured under controlled conditions.
- Requires a prompt, labeled refusal at every saturated resource; backends that block or queue silently cannot be used, and substituting timeouts for refusals costs 27.8–38.0% goodput in the authors' own ramp experiment.
- Not evaluated on interactive or agent-session traffic, where a 13-minute adaptation to a 40% capacity drop and multi-hour low-priority waits would be disqualifying.
- Freshness is explicitly traded away: two lowest classifier bands met their goals for 44–55% of items during a backfill, with a 6.6 h median wait in the lowest band versus 0.5 h for the precursor.
- Multi-controller fairness on a shared capacity domain is emulator-only; production relies on the operational rule that callers sharing a pool share one queue.
- No comparison against SLO-aware scheduling/rejection baselines (QLM, Mooncake, CONCUR, Sarathi-Serve); related work is positioning, not evaluation.
- Leader election is convention-based (lexicographic hostname) with split-brain tolerated rather than solved; no discussion of behaviour under network partitions longer than a poll interval.
- All results are from Meta's stack (FOQS, fleet discovery, gateway, model server); portability to Kubernetes/Ray Serve-style stacks is asserted in the conclusion, not measured.

## Relevance

本文属于"大规模 ML 计算基础设施"：它讨论的是推理机队的准入控制、派发速率与容量规划，并把"过载 = 亚稳态失效"这一现象用生产级证据讲清楚（引擎满负荷但零有效吞吐），对任何做 serving 调度或过载保护的系统工作都有直接参考价值。它不涉及 SDC、数值精度或训练稳定性，因此与核心方向的交集主要在"失效检测与容量观测"这一层。

## Questions for Further Exploration

1. How much of the 43% H100 saving survives if the precursor is also given per-replica caps but keeps its hand-tuned global rate? That ablation — cap-only, no Track — is the missing decomposition, and replay suggests caps carry much of the collapse-prevention benefit.
2. What happens when the backend's refusal is delayed rather than immediate (e.g. provider admission that decides after queueing for 2 s)? The paper's stability argument assumes refusals are cheap and fast; a slow refusal changes the control loop's effective dead time.
3. Why is the mild cut tier ×0.99 all the way to a 50% refusal share? A 40%-refusal episode taking 13 minutes to resolve seems to indicate the tier boundaries (50%/65%) were fit to the reserved workload's provider behaviour rather than derived; a sensitivity study of those two thresholds is absent.
4. Does the local-retry design (refused request returns to the front of its own worker's ready queue) create head-of-line unfairness across priorities when one worker holds a disproportionate share of high-priority work? The paper measures cross-path fairness only via the emulator's Jain index.
5. Would prefix-aware ordering interact badly with the refusal-ratio signal, since cache-hit requests have very different service times and would change what a given dispatch rate means for the engine?
6. Can the backlog depth itself be used as the control signal for a flex pool (the paper's "queue depth shows where the pool is needed" hint), and how does that loop interact with Track's rate control on the same queue?
