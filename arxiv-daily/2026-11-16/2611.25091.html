How priority is manufactured outside the stackMethodology · shim + daemon, zero privileges

Tenants stay unmodified. A preloaded shim (1,355 lines of C++) hooks the driver’s synchronous launch callback; a 571-line daemon (berthd) holds a 16-slot shared table per GPU. Priority is not scheduled — it is produced by making best-effort work have nothing to run.

  1. Request gate. Block best-effort launches while a latency-critical request is in flight, with a 0.5 ms grace window.
  2. Deadline reference. Arrival deadlines for request servers; per-step need_by = b + D − r − g for LLM engines.
  3. Queue gate. Admit only while outstanding best-effort work q ≤ t + f, with f = 1 ms per GPU.
  4. One shared budget. p processes cannot each queue f ahead of a request; the daemon shares it.
  5. 500 µs slice. Intercept the driver’s own unprivileged SET_TIMESLICE, best-effort context only.
  6. Stock slicer preempts. Berth adds no preemption mechanism; the hardware switches in ≈0.2 ms.

Visibilitylibsmctrl’s driver callback sees cuBLAS / cuDNN private launches; a CUDA graph is gated as one launch of its profiled duration.

Calibrationeach best-effort job profiled ~12 s with CUPTI, per kernel position; engines need no profile.

Bounddelay ≤ min(f + k_max, n·s) + n·c_s, so past n·s a request’s delay stops growing with kernel length — why a 62.8 ms GEMM can be capped.

Strengths

  • Measured, not assumed. The 0.2 ms stock preemption and 0.5 ms grace need are probed, and the §3.6 bound predicts the 2.3× p99 penalty before it is observed.
  • Genuinely user space. No kernel module, driver patch or framework hook; closed cuBLAS/cuDNN, unmodified PyTorch and vLLM all work.
  • Wins on the right axis. Near-alone SLOs at 48% best-effort throughput, versus 14–18% for baselines that nearly match its SLOs.
  • Honest baselines. Four released systems run as shipped; unrunnable points reported as 0%; per-cell run counts and losses (§5.6, §5.9) disclosed.
  • Reusable ideas. The arrival-vs-per-step deadline reference, and the rule that a short slice must be disabled beside iteration-deadline engines.

Limitations

  • Single GPU, single node. No multi-GPU or NVLink, no KV-cache or memory-capacity governance, no L2/DRAM bandwidth control — LLM points already sit at 38–47% of read bandwidth.
  • Blind to request queues. The shim cannot see a server’s backlog, so Berth is weakest under bursts and near saturation; held tenants’ requests back up.
  • Profile-shaped. Each best-effort job needs ~12 s of profiling plus a declared SLO/solo time; device-side graph launches are invisible and variable sequence lengths mispredict by up to 2.9×.
  • One undocumented assumption. The 500 µs slice rests on SET_TIMESLICE staying unprivileged — verified on Ampere/Hopper, three drivers, unchecked on Tegra; a driver change erases the long-kernel advantage.
  • Fault domain and trust. Grouped layout puts all critical tenants in one MPS fault domain, its ordering is “a heuristic without a bound”, and a tenant can raise its own slice.
  • Reproducibility gap. No artifact link, and much of the shim, daemon and evaluation code was LLM-written — higher re-implementation risk than usual.

Questions for further exploration

Open problems
  1. Do MIG, TPC quotas and MPS limits hold up if the “p99 within 60% of SLO” sizing target is swept to 50% or 90%? Is 2.7–3.5× robust to that knob?
  2. What decomposes the 2.1-point gap to “Alone” — MPS server overhead, slice cache refill, the 5.9% launch tax, or grace-period residue?
  3. Can the shim see accept-backlog or socket state to fix the burst weakness without touching the framework?
  1. Given a working kernel-level preemptor (GPreempt, Hummingbird) and the early-execution hint it needs, does the 500 µs slice still earn its keep?
  2. Does the per-step deadline reference survive tensor-parallel serving, prefill/decode disaggregation, or chunked prefill with order-of-magnitude step variance?
  3. Is the 1 ms queue bound still safe once KV-cache paging and memory limits — explicitly ungoverned here — force eviction?
