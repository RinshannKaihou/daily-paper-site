> **一句话总结：** 作者用 7 种进化方法、6 个模型、150 个配置、1030 次独立进化运行和 210 万条测试轨迹证明：agent 自进化的瓶颈不是"提不出好 artifact"，而是评估分辨率不足——单次运行在 Terminal-Bench v2.1 上只有 33% 概率选对四种文本进化方法中的最优者（随机为 25%），候选级选择器的成对准确率仅 49.7%（随机 50%），而修复评测后最大报告增益的中位数从 17.6 pp 塌到 0.8 pp。

> **TL;DR:** A large-scale reproducibility study of agent evolution (7 methods × 6 models × 4 benchmarks, 1,030 evolution runs, 2.1M test trajectories, >10M LLM calls) argues that evolution stalls because evaluation cannot resolve real improvements. On Terminal-Bench v2.1 a single run identifies the best of four text-artifact methods only 33% of the time vs. 25% chance, and publication margins (median 3.4 pp) are smaller than the 1.7–4.1 pp single-run standard deviation; at the candidate level, execution-based selection reaches 49.7% pairwise accuracy vs. 50% for random, while Quality@K still rises from 49.2% (K=1) to 61.8% (K=100), i.e. +3.4 pp over a matched equal-quality null. Auditing the largest reported gains, a single appended instruction recovers a median 90% of the gain, and repairing the grader cuts the median gain from 17.6 pp to 0.8 pp.

## 核心贡献

- **提出并系统检验"分辨率假说"（Resolution Hypothesis）**：agent evolution 的瓶颈是"识别哪些 artifact 真正带来改进"，而不是"提出改进"。作者把评估管线的 resolution 定义为"能可靠区分于噪声的最小真实改进"。
- **给出方差分解框架**：把观测分数的方差拆成评估噪声 σ²（同一 artifact 反复评测的波动）与 run-to-run 方差 τ²（不同进化运行产出的 artifact 真实质量差异），并指出比较两个方法时受 (τ²+σ²) 限制，比较单个 artifact 与基线时只受 σ² 限制。
- **方法层证据（Section 4）**：在 150 个配置、1030 次独立运行上估计 σ 与 τ，发现文献中 171 个 benchmark–model 结果的中位领先幅度只有 3.4 pp（IQR 1.2–6.6），小于单次运行 1.7–4.1 pp 的标准差；要识别该幅度，在 Terminal-Bench 上每个方法需约 23 次独立运行（下四分位幅度需 181 次）。
- **增益审计（Section 4.2）**：对 12 个 benchmark 上最大的报告增益做"一句话复现"与"修复评测"双重审计——单条追加指令即可复现中位 90% 的增益，修复评测后中位增益从 17.6 pp 降至 0.8 pp，说明大增益主要来自评测捷径而非能力提升。
- **候选层三条可证伪预测全部获支持（Section 5）**：P1 候选池中确实存在有用 artifact（Quality@K 显著高于等质量零假设）；P2 常见选择器（执行分数、LLM judge）接近随机；P3 每轮选择误差跨轮累积，造成 in-loop 分数上涨而真实能力不涨、run-to-run 方差随轮数增长。
- **给出可操作的改进方向（Section 6）**：候选层用低方差过程指标（未屏蔽的失败诊断率，噪声比终端 pass/fail 低 13–57 倍）；方法层做组件级评测（proposer 用 Quality@K、selector 用 selected-artifact 曲线），并建议论文同时报告 σ̂ 与 τ̂。

## Key Contributions

- **Names and tests a single bottleneck for agent evolution.** The paper reframes the field's instability ("methods trade wins across papers") as an evaluation-resolution problem rather than a proposal-quality problem, and states it as a falsifiable hypothesis with three candidate-level predictions (P1–P3).
- **Separates the two variance sources.** Using the law of total variance, it decomposes observed benchmark score variance into within-artifact evaluation noise σ² and across-run artifact-quality variance τ², then estimates both per configuration from R=8 independent evolution runs (R=16 for text methods on GPT-5.4 nano) and M=8–16 evaluations per artifact.
- **Quantifies the resolution gap at method level.** Surveys 171 benchmark–model columns from nine agent-evolution papers (median margin 3.4 pp, IQR 1.2–6.6; none report a variance estimate, only 6 average over more than one run) and shows the required runs per method at 80% power: ~5 on SpreadsheetBench, 10 on OfficeQA, 19 on ALFWorld, 23 on Terminal-Bench at the median margin, 181 on Terminal-Bench at the lower quartile.
- **Audits the largest reported gains.** A one-rule test (append the single instruction judged most responsible to the unevolved baseline) recovers a median 90% of the gain across 27 model–benchmark pairs with reported gain >3 pp; repairing the released graders (LibreOffice recalculation, semantic grading, restoring official instructions) collapses the median gain to 0.8 pp, and re-evolving under repaired evaluations does not bring it back (≤1.9 pp except one +5.8 pp case).
- **Isolates proposal from selection with fixed candidate pools.** A 100-skill Proposal Pool (16,000 rollouts) shows genuine out-of-sample headroom (+3.3 to +3.4 pp above a matched equal-quality null at K=100), while a 100-skill Selection Pool (11 Terminal-Bench tasks) shows execution-based and LLM-judge selectors at 45.4–49.7% pairwise accuracy against a 50% random baseline.
- **Shows errors compound and offers a partial fix.** Re-evaluating all intermediate artifacts of 8 SkillOpt-Lite and 8 GEPA runs with 8 rollouts per task shows in-loop gains of 6.9–9.0 pp versus re-evaluated gains of +0.4 to −3.2 pp, with run-to-run variance growing to 4.2–24.5 pp²; averaging 16 validation rollouts per candidate raises the mean final test score from 61.4% to 64.4% and the best artifact from 64.8% to 72.7%.

## Methodology

**Setup.** Seven evolution methods in two families: four text-artifact methods (GEPA for prompts, SkillOpt-Lite and Trace2Skill for skill documents, ReasoningBank for memory playbooks) and three harness methods that evolve the agent scaffolding (HarnessLens, Meta-Harness, AHE). Six agent models: GPT-5.4 nano, GPT-5.6 Luna, GPT-5.6 Terra, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, Kimi-K3, GLM-5.2. Four grid benchmarks: ALFWorld (100/70/67 train/val/test), SpreadsheetBench Verified-400 (80/39/281), OfficeQA (49/49/148), and Terminal-Bench v2.1 (89 tasks split 18/18/53, stratified by difficulty). All roles of a method use the same model, so no method benefits from a stronger model writing its artifacts.

**Variance decomposition (Section 3).** For artifact A produced by one evolution run, an observed score is Ŷ(A) = Y(A) + ε with E[ε|A]=0; τ² = Var(Y(A)) is run-to-run variance. By the law of total variance, Var(Ŷ) = E_A[Var(Ŷ|A)] + Var_A[E(Ŷ|A)] = σ² + τ². σ² is estimated by the mean within-artifact sample variance over M repeated evaluations; τ² by a random-effects moment estimator that subtracts σ̂²/M from the variance of artifact means, with negative estimates clipped to 0 (9 of 117 method configurations). Design: R=8 runs per configuration (16 for text methods on GPT-5.4 nano), M=16 evaluations on SpreadsheetBench/OfficeQA/ALFWorld and M=8 on Terminal-Bench and for harness methods.

**Method-level experiments (Section 4).** 150 configurations, 1,030 evolution runs, >2.1M test trajectories. Single-run ranking simulations draw one run and one evaluation per method over 20,000 draws per model–benchmark and compare the resulting top-1 / full ranking against the all-run mean ranking. Required-runs formula N ≈ 2(z_{1−α/2}+z_{1−β})²(τ²+σ²)/Δ² at α=0.05, 80% power, M=1. The literature survey records 178 result columns (171 with an optimization competitor) from the main tables of nine papers, using accuracy-type metrics only, and computes each proposed method's margin over its strongest optimization competitor.

**Gain audit (Section 4.2, Appendix E).** Twelve benchmarks: the three auditable grid benchmarks plus nine from the GEPA and SkillOpt-Lite papers (HotpotQA, PUPA, HoVer, IFBench, AIME 2025, LiveBench-Math, LiveMathematicianBench, SearchQA, DocVQA). An LLM agent flags candidate instructions in released and self-evolved artifacts; the authors hand-check each and write the single rule most responsible as one sentence appended to the otherwise unchanged baseline. Repairs restore the official evaluation or replace surface-form matching with semantic grading by two independent LLM judges (DeepSeek-V4-Flash and GPT-5.6 Luna). Re-evolution under repaired evaluations is run for GEPA and SkillOpt-Lite (OfficeQA, SpreadsheetBench, HotpotQA, LiveMathematicianBench) and for the three harness methods (ALFWorld, SpreadsheetBench, OfficeQA).

**Candidate-level experiments (Section 5, Appendix F).** Proposal Pool: n=100 standalone skills generated offline by a SkillOpt-Lite-based proposer (DeepSeek-V4-Flash) from 78 trajectories on 10 Terminal-Bench training tasks, evaluated on 10 disjoint test tasks with 16 rollouts per skill–task pair (16,000 rollouts). Quality@K is estimated with an empirical-Bayes hierarchical Beta model (κ_max=100) and compared against a matched equal-quality null from 1,000 simulations per task, which calibrates the pure selection-luck effect of taking a maximum over K noisy candidates. Selection Pool: n=100 skills from Terminal-Bench and DeepSWE trajectories, evaluated on 11 difficulty-stratified Terminal-Bench tasks (8 rollouts per pair, 15 for the top-10 artifacts per task, 12 for the baseline); each selector sees only the first rollout (two for native SkillOpt-Lite/GEPA operators), while true quality is measured on held-out rollouts. Selectors compared: random, execution score, native SkillOpt-Lite and GEPA parent selection, and three LLM-judge variants (pointwise, pairwise in both orders, ACE-style).

**Trajectory re-evaluation (Section 5.3, Appendix G).** All 119 distinct within-run artifact versions from 8 SkillOpt-Lite and 8 GEPA runs (DeepSeek-V4-Flash, GPT-5.6 Luna) are re-evaluated on the same 18 Terminal-Bench validation tasks with 8 rollouts per artifact–task pair instead of 1. Run-to-run variance τ̂_t² is tracked per round with negative corrected estimates retained.

## Results & Highlights

- **A single sentence reproduces most of the headline gain, and repairing the grader removes it.** Across the 27 model–benchmark pairs with a reported gain above 3 pp, appending one instruction to the unevolved baseline recovers a median **90%** of the gain; after repairing the evaluation, the median gain falls from **17.6 pp to 0.8 pp**. Concretely on SpreadsheetBench with GPT-5.4 nano, the released grader gives 33.5% → 64.6% (+31.1 pp), the one-rule baseline reaches 62.5% (+29.0 pp), and under LibreOffice recalculation the baseline rises to 58.0% and the evolved artifact to 64.7%, i.e. **+6.7 pp** instead of +31.1 pp.
- **Large gains on math/QA benchmarks are mostly grader artifacts.** AIME 2025 with GPT-5.6 Luna: +39.5 pp under the released parser (11.5% → 51.0%) but **+2.5 pp** after an equivalence-check repair (80.3% → 82.8%). LiveMathematicianBench with GPT-5.4 nano: +27.1 pp released (29.0% → 56.1%) versus **−1.7 pp** repaired (49.6% → 47.9%), because the "stronger result" option is correct in all 85 questions where it appears. DocVQA with GPT-5.4 nano: +21.0 pp released (61.0% → 82.0%) versus **−0.2 pp** under semantic grading (88.3% → 88.1%).
- **A single run cannot rank methods.** On Terminal-Bench v2.1 a one-run-per-method table picks the best of the four text-artifact methods only **33%** of the time (random: 25%); on OfficeQA 51%, SpreadsheetBench 67%, ALFWorld 73%. Full-ranking agreement with the all-run ordering is 8% on Terminal-Bench (random 1/24 ≈ 4%), 24% on ALFWorld, 29% on OfficeQA, 33% on SpreadsheetBench. Across 341 same-benchmark configuration pairs, **217 (64%)** have a gap between all-run means below one standard deviation of a single-run comparison; per method the share ranges from 53% (HarnessLens) to 79%.
- **Resolution demands far more runs than the literature uses.** At the median reported margin of 3.4 pp and 80% power, identifying a method difference needs about **5 runs on SpreadsheetBench, 10 on OfficeQA, 19 on ALFWorld, and 23 on Terminal-Bench** per method; at the lower-quartile margin of 1.2 pp, Terminal-Bench needs **181**. The measured single-run standard deviation is 1.7–4.1 pp (median per benchmark), with σ = 1.0–3.7 pp on SpreadsheetBench/OfficeQA/ALFWorld rising to **4.5 pp on Terminal-Bench**, and τ = 0–12.3 pp.
- **Proposal is not the bottleneck: helpful artifacts exist.** In the Proposal Pool, Quality@K climbs from **49.2% at K=1 to 61.8% at K=100** under a 2-hour budget, versus a matched equal-quality null of 58.4% at K=100 — a **+3.4 pp** excess that separates from the null for K ≥ 4; under Terminal-Bench's original timeouts it rises from 39.7% to 51.6% (+3.3 pp over null). Shared skills also differ substantially: the best shared skill (artifact-063) reaches **57.5%** across all 160 test rollouts versus a 49.2% pool mean (**+8.3 pp**) and 41.9% for the weakest skill.
- **Selection is statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip.** Execution-based selection achieves **49.7% ± 1.9** pairwise accuracy (random: 50.0%), with a rank correlation of **0.054** between a single execution score and posterior artifact quality; regressions are 30.5% of the pool but **62.1%** of accepted baseline replacements. The native SkillOpt-Lite and GEPA parent selectors reach 48.6%, and LLM judges do no better: pointwise **45.4% ± 4.5**, pairwise 49.0% ± 2.2, ACE-style 46.9% ± 3.5. No selector benefits from more candidates: at K=100 every practical selector is still near the random baseline, while the oracle already reaches that quality with K < 5.
- **Selection errors compound.** In-loop validation gains grow to **6.9–9.0 pp** over evolution, but independent re-evaluation with 8 rollouts per task yields **+0.4 to −3.2 pp**; corrected run-to-run variance τ̂_t² grows from 0 to **4.2–24.5 pp²** by the final round, comparable to the literature margins that separate methods.
- **More validation rollouts partially fix it.** Running SkillOpt-Lite with 16 validation rollouts per candidate instead of 1 raises the mean final test score across 16 runs from **61.4% to 64.4%** and the best evolved artifact from **64.8% to 72.7%**, because single-rollout validation lets a lucky 80% draw become an unbeatable incumbent and stall the loop within four rounds.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty.** The individual ingredients are not new: variance decomposition, best-arm identification, and the observation that agent benchmarks are noisy all have prior art (the paper cites PACE, Kaufmann et al., Agarwal et al., and the fragility analyses of Ye et al. and Liu et al.). What is new and genuinely useful is the *joint* framing — treating method-level ranking instability and candidate-level selection failure as two manifestations of one resolution constraint, then testing both at a scale nobody has attempted. The one-rule gain audit is the most original piece: it converts "these gains look suspicious" into a controlled sufficiency experiment.

**Rigor.** Strong overall: pre-registered-style predictions P1–P3, matched equal-quality nulls for Quality@K (a subtle and important control, since max-over-K curves rise even for identical candidates), held-out rollout references so selectors never see the quality ground truth, empirical-Bayes shrinkage with an explicit κ_max sensitivity analysis (30 / 100 / 300 / ∞), and a power formula rather than post-hoc significance claims. But several inferential gaps remain:

- **The "selection ≈ chance" conclusion is weaker than the headline.** The false-acceptance intervals are enormous (execution selection 62.1% ± 42.9; SkillOpt-Lite 59.4% ± 46.2), so the claim that selection actively admits regressions is barely identified; only the pairwise-accuracy point estimates (49.7% ± 1.9, 48.6% ± 2.6) are tight enough to exclude 50%, and they do so by 1–2 pp. A selection accuracy of 49.7% is "no better than chance" in the statistical sense but the practical claim "selectors cannot identify helpful artifacts at all" outruns a 0.3 pp deficit.
- **Near-chance selection may be partly definitional.** If the 100 skills in the Selection Pool have true quality spread of only a few pp (the paper shows a 15 pp min–max spread but does not report the *distribution*), then any selector faces a signal-to-noise problem that is as much a property of the pool as of the selector. The paper would be stronger if it reported selection accuracy as a function of the true quality gap between the pair being compared, which would show whether selectors fail uniformly or only on small gaps.
- **The gain audit chooses the rule after inspecting the artifacts.** The authors acknowledge this (Appendix E.5), and it is the right concession, but it means "one sentence recovers 90% of the gain" is a sufficiency result. It does not establish that the evolved artifact contains nothing else of value, and HoVer is a concrete counterexample where one rule recovers only 41–43% for two models because the gain is spread over several retrieval strategies. The 90% median is therefore an upper bound on shortcut prevalence, not a lower bound.
- **Repair changes the baseline, not just the artifact.** In Table 11, repairing the grader lifts the baseline dramatically (DocVQA 61.0 → 88.3, SearchQA 52.0 → 84.2, AIME 2025 Luna 11.5 → 80.3). The statement "the gain vanishes after repair" is true, but part of what vanishes is headroom: the repaired baseline is near ceiling, so no method could show a large gain. The paper does address this with an explicit headroom analysis (Appendix E.4) and names the three benchmarks where real gains survive (HotpotQA +5.5–7.6 pp, SpreadsheetBench +5.7–6.7 pp, HoVer +8–13 pp), which is the right way to handle it — but the abstract's framing ("large gains vanish") is blunter than the evidence, which is "large gains were mostly grader artifacts, and where real gains exist they are small."
- **Heterogeneous baselines across methods.** Harness methods evolve their own Mini-SWE-agent workspace starting from v1.14.4 while text-artifact methods run Mini-SWE-agent 2.4.6, and the paper's pairing rule excludes cross-grader comparisons. This is handled correctly in Figure 2a, but it means the "method ranking" results are not a clean single-agent comparison, and the harness methods' very large ALFWorld gains (Meta-Harness +30.3 pp, AHE +31.7 pp with DeepSeek-V4-Flash in Table 12) are traced to a single incidental setting — the initial agent keeping only the last two history steps — which is a harness bug being fixed, not an evolution capability.
- **Scope of the candidate-level evidence.** All of Section 5 is Terminal-Bench v2.1, one benchmark, with pools generated by DeepSeek-V4-Flash and evaluated largely with DeepSeek-V4-Flash; the trajectory re-evaluation covers two methods and two models. The paper's own limitations acknowledge this, but the abstract's "agent evolution is often bottlenecked by reliably identifying improvements" is stated at a generality the experiments do not cover (e.g., program evolution with deterministic graders, which the paper explicitly flags as out of scope and where the opposite pattern is documented).
- **A red flag worth noting.** The 16-rollout validation experiment (Appendix F.3) is the paper's strongest *constructive* result — it shows resolution can be bought with compute and that doing so raises the best artifact from 64.8% to 72.7% — yet it appears only in an appendix, on one benchmark, one method, and 16 runs, with no cross-benchmark replication. If this effect is real it deserves to be a main result; its placement suggests the authors know it is preliminary.

**Significance.** High. If the resolution framing holds, it redirects the field from "invent a better proposer" to "make improvements detectable," which is a more tractable and more honest research program. The concrete diagnostics (Quality@K for proposers, selected-artifact curves for selectors, reporting σ̂ and τ̂ alongside scores) are immediately usable and cost little. The gain audit is likely to become a standard checking procedure for agent-evolution papers.

## Strengths

- Scale and replication discipline that is rare in this literature: 1,030 independent evolution runs, up to 16 evaluations per artifact, 150 configurations, and a literature survey of 171 result columns with explicit inclusion rules.
- A clean, exact variance decomposition that separates evaluation noise from run-to-run artifact variance, with unbiased moment estimators and a documented clipping rule for negative τ̂².
- The matched equal-quality null for Quality@K is a genuinely careful control — without it, the rising Quality@K curve would be uninterpretable, and the paper shows the observed curve separates from the null from K ≥ 4–5.
- The one-rule gain audit plus grader repair is an elegant causal-ish test of the shortcut hypothesis, and the per-benchmark table (Table 11) makes every claim checkable.
- Honest and specific limitations (frozen weights, Terminal-Bench-only candidate pools, two methods for trajectory re-evaluation, model-strength confound, program evolution out of scope), plus an appendix that explicitly bounds what the one-rule test proves.
- Practical, cheap recommendations: process metrics with 13–57× lower evaluation noise than terminal pass/fail, component-wise proposer/selector evaluation, and variance reporting.
- The 16-rollout validation result is a concrete existence proof that higher-resolution selection *does* accumulate larger gains, which keeps the paper from being purely negative.

## Limitations

- **Frozen weights only.** The analysis covers evolution of external artifacts (prompts, skills, memories, harnesses); methods that update weights via RL have different gradient dynamics and may face a different resolution constraint. The paper says so, but the title's generality is not matched by the scope.
- **Candidate-level evidence is one benchmark.** Proposal Pool, Selection Pool, and trajectory re-evaluation all use Terminal-Bench v2.1; pool generation is DeepSeek-V4-Flash-centric, and the 100-skill pools may not represent larger or differently generated candidate sets.
- **Small R for variance estimation.** R=8 runs per configuration (16 for one setting) yields wide confidence intervals on τ̂², especially where τ̂ is clipped to zero in 9 of 117 configurations; the paper reports intervals but the method-level conclusions lean on point estimates of τ.
- **The near-chance selection result is pool-dependent.** Without reporting selection accuracy as a function of the true quality gap, it is hard to tell whether selectors are uniformly uninformative or simply unable to resolve the small gaps that dominate this pool.
- **Grader repair conflates evaluation fix with headroom loss.** Several repaired baselines jump 20–30 pp, so "gain vanishes" partly reflects a ceiling effect; the headroom analysis mitigates but does not eliminate this.
- **Model-strength confound is only partially addressed.** Evaluation noise shrinks for stronger models mainly on ALFWorld where success approaches ceiling; on Terminal-Bench the median stays at 3.5–4.0 pp across three models. Whether a substantially stronger model would have better resolution is left open.
- **No released artifacts yet.** Code, trajectory data, and pools are promised upon acceptance, so the central numbers cannot be independently reproduced today.
- **Program evolution is out of scope** even though it is the setting where deterministic re-evaluation makes resolution cheap — arguably the most important boundary case for the hypothesis.

## Relevance

直接命中"自进化智能体与自动化研究"：它把自进化循环的失败归因于评估分辨率而非提议能力，并给出可复用的诊断量（Quality@K、selected-artifact 曲线、σ̂/τ̂ 报告），对我们关心的长时自主 agent 可靠性、评测噪声导致的假阳性接受、以及"分数上涨但能力不涨"的失效模式非常有参考价值。

## Questions for Further Exploration

1. Does the 16-rollout validation fix generalize? Running it across all four grid benchmarks and all seven methods would show whether resolution can be bought with compute uniformly or only where the signal-to-noise ratio is already favorable.
2. How does selection accuracy scale with the true quality gap between the pair being compared? A per-gap breakdown would distinguish "selectors are uninformative" from "selectors cannot resolve small gaps," which have very different implications for pool design.
3. Would anytime-valid sequential acceptance tests (e.g., SPRT or the PACE-style test the paper cites) recover most of the resolution at a fraction of the 16× rollout cost, and how do they compare with simply averaging more rollouts?
4. Do the conclusions hold under weight-updating continual learning, where the "artifact" is the model itself and evaluation noise may be correlated across rounds rather than independent?
5. Can process metrics be turned into a *selector* rather than a diagnostic? The 13–57× lower noise is promising, but the paper explicitly notes that passing more intermediate checks need not solve more tasks — an experiment that uses process metrics as the acceptance signal would test whether the resolution gain survives.
6. What is the right unit of replication for the field? The paper's power analysis implies most published agent-evolution tables (single run, no variance estimate) are uninformative at their claimed margins; a follow-up could estimate how many published rankings would flip under 8–23 runs per method.
