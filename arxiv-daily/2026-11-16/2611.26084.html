Why this matters

It turns “why didn’t GRPO learn this?” into a number computable before a single gradient step — and shows restarts waste progress while repeating problems pays.

Critical edge

The toy world is engineered so reward is a perfect oracle for the Bayes-optimal action; real agent tasks have no last-chance step limit and no closed-form threshold.

Methodology

A world where the prior is written, not estimated

01Scripted Bayesian world
02Base GPT, one epoch
03Measure s(k) vs π(k)
04GRPO, 102k attempts
05Count D_rep → predict

The world. One room, 1–3 obstacles, two candidate tools each; nine test rules plus 30 filler rules pair obstacle O with default tool D (key→chest). In normal worlds D works 80% of the time; in a changed world (probability ν = 0.05) D never works and alternative A always does. A is another rule’s default tool, so both objects are ordinary — only the learned O–D link separates them — and every failure yields the identical observation “nothing”, so one failure is genuinely ambiguous.

Measurement. At fixed decision prefixes (k = 0…3 failures, 100 instances per rule and k), every valid action is teacher-forced and s(k) = p(A)/(p(A)+p(D)). Base and RL models are scored on identical instances, so every reported change is paired. Two distances are tracked apart: |s − π| (calibration to the ideal believer) and |s − 1[π > τ]| (quality of the decision).

Models & RL. nanoGPT-style, 6 layers / 6 heads / width 384 / context 256 → 10.7M params (1.3M and 85M in ablations), exactly one pass over 9M scripted games (516–548M tokens, 108-token vocabulary). GRPO with no KL and no reference model by default, advantage = reward − group mean, AdamW at 3e-5, temperature 1, 200 steps × 512 attempts = 102,400 attempts.

The step limit is drawn from 4–7 moves so that the decision after k failures is the last chance — which makes “switch when π(k) > τ” exactly reward-maximising. That design choice is what the critical edge above is about.

The counting problem

Three ways to count a budget — only one predicts

qi = the probability that one temperature-1 attempt switches to A after 2–3 failures and then wins. From q, three pre-training counts:

D_att = Σ rN qi // attempts

D_prob = Σ (1 − (1 − qi)rN) // problems that ever win

D_rep = Σ r (1 − (1 − qi)N) // groups containing a win

Same 102,400 attempts, opposite outcomes: groups of 256 succeed in 1 of 10 runs; groups of 4 succeed in 9 of 10. Attempts and distinct problems are blind to that by construction — groups are not.

Take-off is modelled as P = 1 − exp(−exp(b₀ + b₁ log₁₀ D)), fitted by maximum likelihood. Across 910 runs in 13 recipes, counting groups beats counting problems or attempts by ≥ 12 log-likelihood points in every setting; one shared slope fits them all (per-setting slopes p = 0.46).

Results

RL moves toward the better bet — then hits a cliff

The base model is already a near-perfect Bayesian. Ask it to switch after a failure and it hedges exactly as the posterior says it should. Post-training then pushes it toward the correct decision rather than the familiar one: after one failure of a reliable rule the share on the hammer falls 22% → 10%; after two failures it rises 56% → 77%. On 2,000 held-out problems scored against a dynamic-programming optimum, GRPO closes 74%–95% of the reward gap in all six objective × group-size combinations.

And what gets learned is a skill, not an action: in one run the hammer was never sampled on a particular rule, yet that rule’s switch probability rose 93 percentage points. The model learned “after the default fails, try the other tool”.

Repetition is a new group; restarts are a loss. Seeing the same 400 problems 8× instead of once moves take-off from 1/10 to 10/10, while 1,600 distinct problems over 4× the steps stays at 6/10 — so it is not “more steps”. Splitting the same budget into 1 / 2 / 4 / 8 restarts from the base model gives 9 / 7 / 5 / 3 of 10 seeds taking off at least once.

Strengths

  • The prior is written, not estimated. “The model hedged like its corpus” becomes a measurement instead of an inference.
  • Paired before/after on identical decision points removes the usual confound in “did RL change the model?” studies.
  • A genuine held-out prediction: the law is fitted on 400 runs, then used to predict the group-size, repetition, restart and budget experiments.
  • The count decomposition is sharp and explains two otherwise puzzling results at once — 256-attempt groups losing to 4-attempt groups, and repetition beating a wider pool.
  • 13 recipes / 910 runs is a serious robustness sweep for a mechanistic RL paper.
  • Honest about failure: the law does not extrapolate, and RL could not be run on the 7B probe.

Limitations

  • Toy scale throughout: a 108-token templated language and 1.3M–85M-param models. No natural language, no real pre-training priors, no frontier scale.
  • The step limit (4–7 moves) makes the reward a perfect oracle for the Bayes-optimal action. Real agent tasks have no such last-chance structure.
  • All 39 rules share one relational template, so every winning group pushes the same mechanism. No probing, no activation analysis, no heterogeneous-rule ablation.
  • q is only tractable here. The OLMo-3-7B probe collapses to nine single decisions and 60% game completion — real RLVR has no exact analogue of “probability this attempt switches and then wins”.
  • The ~35 threshold is world- and recipe-specific; leave-one-setting-out interpolates but does not extrapolate.
  • No entropy bonus, temperature schedule, rejection sampling or PPO baseline — the advice is the cheapest possible advice, unbenchmarked against cheap alternatives.

Questions for further exploration

What would break this story

Q1Does the law survive when the 39 rules no longer share one template — is the all-or-nothing transition a property of RL or of the templated world?
Q2Can q be estimated well enough in real RLVR (code, math) for the count to be predictive, given exact scoring needs the correct action at every step?
Q3Would an entropy bonus or temperature schedule shift the threshold, or only change which groups contain a win without changing the required count?
Q4Why do larger groups underperform at equal budget beyond counting — fewer distinct problems per step, or larger gradient variance?
