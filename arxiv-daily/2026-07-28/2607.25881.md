> **一句话总结：** 研究团队让人类专家和三种2025年中的LLM（ChatGPT-4o、Claude Sonnet 4、DeepSeek V3）为8个物理/天体物理/宇宙学研究项目各写一份一页提案（共32份，8人写+24AI写），再让4位人类评审和2个2026年中的前沿AI评审（Claude Opus 4.8、ChatGPT Pro 5.5）盲评作者身份与质量：人类评审判断AI提案正确率79%、人类提案72%，而两个AI评审对全部32份提案的作者身份判断均达到100%准确；更关键的是，AI评审对AI撰写的提案评分系统性地比人类提案高出约1分（5分制），而人类评审组不存在这种偏向。

> **TL;DR:** Across 8 expert-conceived astrophysics/cosmology/physics projects, 32 blindly-reviewed one-page proposals (8 human-written, 24 written by ChatGPT-4o/Claude Sonnet 4/DeepSeek V3) were scored by 4 human reviewers and 2 frontier AI reviewers (Claude Opus 4.8, ChatGPT Pro 5.5). Human reviewers rated human- and AI-written proposals almost identically (mean total score 3.52±0.59 for humans vs. ~3.51 average for the three AI models), and correctly identified authorship only 72% (human-written) to 79% (AI-written) of the time — while both AI reviewers classified all 32 proposals with 100% accuracy and simultaneously scored AI-written proposals roughly one full point higher than human-written ones (e.g., Claude Opus 4.8: 4.47 vs. 3.59; ChatGPT Pro 5.5: 4.41 vs. 3.47), a pro-AI bias entirely absent from the human panel.

## 核心贡献

- **首个"人类 vs AI 提案撰写"的双向盲评实验**：8个由领域专家构思的研究项目（涵盖AGN、大尺度结构、内禀取向、激光烧蚀仪器、CMB前景、引力波多信使、脉冲星测时阵列、SU(2)杨-米尔斯理论），每个项目由1位人类专家和3个AI（ChatGPT-4o、Claude Sonnet 4、DeepSeek V3）各写一份一页提案，共32份提案，经匿名格式化后统一评审。
- **人类识别AI作者身份的能力有限但优于随机**：4位人类评审正确识别AI提案79%、人类提案72%，跨评审者差异很大（59%–88%），说明并非依靠单一可靠信号。
- **前沿AI评审识别作者身份近乎完美**：Claude Opus 4.8与ChatGPT Pro 5.5对全部32份提案的作者判断均达100%准确，且两者引用的判断依据高度一致（模板化五段结构、笼统的"公开数据集"措辞、整月为单位的时间线等表层特征），而较小模型Claude Sonnet 4.6识别AI提案的准确率仅67%，说明该能力集中在前沿模型。
- **发现系统性的"亲AI偏差"（pro-AI bias）**：两个AI评审给AI撰写的提案打分比人类提案高约1分（5分制），且在改变呈现顺序（先评分后判断作者 vs 先判断后评分）、更换评审模型（Codex 5.5 Pro、Claude Sonnet 4.6）后依然稳健存在；人类评审组则不存在这一偏差（4个作者的评分都落在3.25–3.74窄区间内）。
- **项目层面的强反相关**：AI相对人类的评分优势与人类提案本身质量强反相关（人类评审组 Pearson r=-0.95，AI评审组 r=-0.96）——人类计划写得越弱（如GW项目仅2.4/5），AI优势越大；人类计划写得扎实的项目（RG、SU2、IA），人类评审甚至更偏好人类提案。

## Key Contributions

- **First bidirectional blind study of human-vs-AI research proposal writing**, covering 8 expert-conceived projects spanning theoretical, computational, observational, and instrumentation work in physics, astrophysics, and cosmology (AGN duty cycles, Lyman-break galaxy–halo connection, intrinsic alignments, laser-ablation debris prediction, CMB radio-galaxy foregrounds, GW black-hole binary environments, pulsar timing array forecasting, and SU(2) Yang–Mills inflation).
- **Quantified human ability to detect AI authorship**: four human reviewers correctly flagged AI-written proposals 79% and human-written proposals 72% of the time — above chance but far from reliable, and highly variable across individual reviewers (59%–88%).
- **Quantified near-perfect AI authorship detection by frontier models**: Claude Opus 4.8 and ChatGPT Pro 5.5 both classified all 32 proposals correctly (100%), citing overlapping cues (uniform five-phase templates, generic tool lists, round-number timelines), while a smaller model (Claude Sonnet 4.6) detected AI-written proposals only 67% of the time, indicating this capability is concentrated in the most capable models rather than a general LLM property.
- **Discovery and robustness-testing of a "pro-AI bias"** in AI-based review: both AI reviewers scored AI-written proposals ~1 point higher (5-point rubric) than human-written ones, a gap that persisted under two additional robustness checks (a rate-first ordering variant using Codex 5.5 Pro, and a smaller-model reviewer, Claude Sonnet 4.6) and was absent in the human review panel.
- **Project-level decomposition** showing the aggregate human–AI parity and AI pro-bias are driven by a handful of projects with weak human-written plans, not a uniform effect (Pearson r ≈ −0.95/−0.96 between AI-human score gap and human-plan quality, in the human and AI panels respectively).

## Methodology

The study isolates the "project planning" stage of the scientific workflow. For each of 8 expert-conceived projects, a domain expert supplied a fixed seed (project title, one-paragraph background, one-sentence goal). This seed was given verbatim to (a) the human expert who designed the project and (b) an "AI prompter" (a student outside that subfield) who fed the same seed into a fixed prompt template to three AI assistants — ChatGPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4, and DeepSeek V3 (mid-2025 models, default tool access, single pass, no iterative refinement) — producing one proposal per assistant. This yielded 8 human + 24 AI = 32 one-page proposals, each following an identical four-section template (Title, Background, Goal, Methodology ≤300 words) capped well below typical grant length to keep the task tractable. Human-written proposals were passed through ChatGPT-4o for a grammar/typo-only cleanup pass to remove superficial authorship tells, and all 32 proposals were anonymized and uniformly reformatted before review.

Each proposal was then independently scored by 4 human reviewers (co-authors of this paper, serving as blind reviewers with grant-panel-level but not necessarily topic-specific expertise) and 2 AI reviewers (Claude Opus 4.8, ChatGPT Pro 5.5; mid-2026 models), on two tasks per reviewer: (1) a binary human-vs-AI origin judgment, and (2) a 1–5 rubric score across four aspects — clarity/structure, appropriateness of methods, resource/tool planning, and feasibility/timeline/risk awareness (full rubric in Table II). To rule out ordering/priming artifacts, the authors also ran a two-step "rate-first" variant (score before judging origin) with Codex 5.5 Pro, and repeated the AI-reviewer protocol with a smaller model, Claude Sonnet 4.6, to test whether model scale drives the observed effects.

## Results & Highlights

- **Origin-detection accuracy (Table III)**: Human reviewers correctly identified AI-written proposals 79% of the time and human-written proposals 72% of the time (chance = 50%). Claude Opus 4.8 and ChatGPT Pro 5.5 each achieved 100% accuracy on both classes across all 32 proposals; the rate-first Codex 5.5 Pro variant also reached 100%; the smaller Claude Sonnet 4.6 reached only 67% on AI-written proposals (vs. 100% on human-written), showing the near-perfect detection is a frontier-model property, not universal.
- **Pro-AI scoring bias (Table IV)**: Human reviewers scored all four "authors" within a narrow 3.25–3.74 band (out of 5) — ChatGPT-written proposals scored lowest (3.25±0.47), DeepSeek-written highest (3.74±0.52), human-written proposals in the middle (3.52±0.59). AI reviewers, in contrast, rated AI-written proposals roughly one point higher than human-written ones: Claude Opus 4.8 gave AI authors a mean of 4.47 vs. 3.59 for human authors; ChatGPT Pro 5.5 gave 4.41 vs. 3.47. The same ~1-point gap appeared in the rate-first Codex 5.5 Pro (AI-written 4.51 vs. human-written 3.50) and Claude Sonnet 4.6 (4.24 vs. 3.72) robustness checks.
- **Aspect-level breakdown (Table V / Fig. 3)**: The pro-AI bias is largest for "Clarity and Structure," where AI reviewers rate AI authors near the ceiling (Claude Opus 4.8: ChatGPT-written 4.88±0.35, DeepSeek-written 5.00±0.00) while rating the human author only 3.75±0.46 — versus human reviewers' own human-author score of 3.69±0.56 on the same aspect. The bias is smallest for "Appropriateness of Methods to Scientific Goal," where human reviewers place the human author at/near the top (3.72±0.49) and even the AI-reviewer gap narrows. "Feasibility, Timeline, and Risk Awareness" is the weakest-scoring aspect for AI-written proposals under every evaluator (e.g., human reviewers give AI authors only 3.06–3.38 here vs. 3.19–3.84 on other aspects), consistent with the qualitative observation that AI plans favor unrealistic or round-number timelines.
- **Project-level anti-correlation (Fig. 4)**: The AI−human score gap correlates strongly and negatively with the quality of the human-written plan itself, in both the human panel (Pearson r = −0.95) and the AI panel (r = −0.96). The human panel actually preferred the human-written proposal over all three AI ones in 5 of the 8 projects (e.g., RG, SU2, IA had strong, detailed human plans); the AI advantage was concentrated in projects with a comparatively weak human plan — most starkly GW, where the human proposal scored only 2.4/5 from the human panel.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty:** The controlled, blinded, bidirectional design (human and AI both write, human and AI both judge, with attention to evaluator bias) is a genuine methodological step up from prior single-sided studies cited in the paper — e.g., Si et al. (ICLR 2025) found LLM-generated research ideas rated more novel than human ones but did not check judge bias; Shcherbiak et al. and Ye & Hashimoto found AI-generated review feedback useful but noted quality-rating inflation. This paper's explicit measurement of a ~1-point AI-reviewer pro-AI bias, robust to reviewer model choice and question ordering, is a distinct and policy-relevant contribution.

**Rigor:** The core design choices (fixed seed/template, grammar-normalization pass on human proposals, anonymization, blind scoring, two independent robustness checks — reordering the origin/score tasks and swapping in a different-size reviewer model) are reasonable safeguards against superficial confounds. However, the sample is small: only 8 projects (32 proposals total), and the "human reviewer panel" is drawn entirely from the paper's own co-authors rather than external, disinterested judges — a non-independence the authors disclose but that could shape both the scoring rubric's application and the qualitative interpretation of cues. The authors themselves caution that the AI reviewers' 100% detection accuracy "should not be read as evidence of reliable authorship detection in general," since the free-text justifications show the AI reviewers keyed on surface template features (uniform five-phase structure, generic tool lists, round-number timelines) rather than necessarily deeper content understanding — a paper-specific caveat that meaningfully tempers the headline 100% number.

**Reproducibility:** The four-aspect rubric (Table II) and prompt template (Appendix B, referenced but not reproduced here) are shared identically across human and AI evaluators, and project backgrounds/goals are given in Appendix A — reasonably reproducible for a study of this kind. A notable data quirk the authors flag themselves: the three proposal-writing AI assistants (ChatGPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4, DeepSeek V3, "mid-2025" vintage) were already outdated relative to the two "mid-2026" AI reviewers (Claude Opus 4.8, ChatGPT Pro 5.5) by the time of submission, since the study spanned roughly a year — meaning the reported numbers are explicitly a snapshot of "a specific point in time," not a benchmark of current frontier models.

**Significance:** The pro-AI review bias finding has direct, concrete stakes: the paper explicitly ties it to funding-agency policy (NIH prohibits generative AI in peer review, NSF bars uploading proposal content to non-approved AI tools, ERC requires non-delegation of evaluative judgment), providing empirical grounding for these already-adopted restrictions rather than speculative concern.

**Red flags:** (1) Reviewer non-independence — the four human "blind reviewers" are paper co-authors, not external panelists; (2) n=8 projects is explicitly acknowledged by the authors as small, driven by the practical difficulty of coordinating this many domain experts, and the paper itself states the aggregate parity/bias findings are "concentrated in a few projects rather than uniform"; (3) one human reviewer's free-text justification, quoted in the paper, admits guessing "AI" simply because they had "already selected two humans" — an openly heuristic (not evidence-based) judgment that the paper transparently reports rather than hides; (4) no assessment of idea novelty — the underlying scientific idea was fixed and shared between human and AI proposals by design, so the study measures only planning/writing quality, not the harder question of whether AI can originate comparably good research directions.

## Strengths

- Clean, symmetric experimental design: identical seed, template, and rubric for both human and AI authors, and for both human and AI reviewers, isolating the planning-stage capability question from confounds like topic selection or formatting.
- Multiple independent robustness checks (reordering the scoring/origin-judgment tasks, swapping in a smaller AI reviewer model, cross-model reviewer comparison) all reproduce the central pro-AI bias finding, strengthening confidence it is not an artifact of a single prompt or model.
- Diverse project portfolio (theoretical, computational, observational, and instrumentation work) spanning multiple physics/astronomy subfields, reducing the risk that findings are specific to one methodology type.
- Unusually transparent reporting of qualitative reviewer reasoning (free-text justifications, including an admittedly heuristic guess) and explicit statement of caveats (small sample, outdated models by submission time, no novelty assessment).
- Direct, well-argued connection to real funding-agency AI policies, giving the empirical results immediate practical relevance beyond an academic curiosity.

## Limitations

- Only 8 projects and 32 total proposals; the authors' own project-level analysis shows the aggregate effects are driven by a handful of projects rather than being uniform, which limits statistical generalization.
- Proposals were capped at one page/≤300 words for methodology, far shorter than a real grant proposal — the paper explicitly leaves it open whether findings hold for full-length, multi-round-reviewed proposals.
- AI proposals were generated in a single prompt pass with no iterative refinement, understating what AI-assisted writing could achieve with human-in-the-loop editing.
- Human reviewers are the paper's own co-authors rather than an independent panel, and the AI-writing models (mid-2025) were already outdated relative to the AI-reviewer models (mid-2026) by publication time.
- The study evaluates only planning/methodology quality on a fixed, human-conceived idea — it does not address whether AI can originate the underlying research idea itself, which the authors note is addressed by other recent work with different (weaker feasibility) tradeoffs.

## Questions for Further Exploration

- Does the ~1-point pro-AI bias persist if AI reviewers are explicitly instructed to ignore surface style and focus only on content substance, as the authors suggest testing in future work?
- Would allowing iterative refinement (multi-turn prompting, self-critique passes) for AI-written proposals close the gap in the weakest-scoring dimension (feasibility/timeline/risk), or does the round-number-timeline tendency reflect a more fundamental limitation?
- Since the four human reviewers are also paper co-authors, would a fully external, non-author reviewer panel replicate the same ~72–79% detection accuracy and the absence of pro-AI bias, or could shared context with the projects have influenced their judgments?
- Does the strong anti-correlation (r ≈ −0.95/−0.96) between AI-human score gap and human-plan quality generalize to other scientific domains (e.g., biology, computer science) or only reflect idiosyncrasies of these 8 physics/astronomy projects?
- How would the pro-AI bias and detection-accuracy numbers shift for full-length, multi-page grant proposals evaluated across multiple review rounds, rather than single-pass one-page summaries?
