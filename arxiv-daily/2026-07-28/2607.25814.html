Evaluation of Adversarial Robustness in Arabic Language Models

Why This Matters

Arabic sentiment models are used for content moderation and social monitoring — invisible vowel marks or paraphrasing can silently flip their verdicts.

Critical Edge

The headline 92-point collapse partly reflects distribution shift, not pure robustness failure: diacritics are stripped from the training data entirely.

Methodology

Five BERT-family models — AraBERT, MARBERT, CaMeLBERT, mBERT, and XLM-T — were fine-tuned for binary Arabic sentiment classification on a ~16K-tweet corpus (14.7K train / 1.08K test) spanning Modern Standard and dialectal Arabic. Six attacks are applied, all black-box and non-targeted: three character-level (diacritic insertion, visual letter substitution, dialectal phonetic substitution), two word-level (SHAP-ranked AraBERT-MLM synonym substitution, conjunction substitution), and one sentence-level (AraT5 paraphrasing — generated once and shared across all five victims, unlike the per-model SHAP-driven attacks). Each attack is scored on five axes: accuracy, Attack Success Rate, Levenshtein-based perturbation rate, cosine similarity, and Universal Sentence Encoder similarity, balancing attack potency against semantic and grammatical fidelity. As a defense, every victim model is then adversarially fine-tuned on a mix of clean and attack-generated examples and re-evaluated against the same six attacks.

Strengths

  • Broadest granularity coverage of any Arabic-LM robustness study — character, word, and sentence level in one framework, closing a gap where prior work covered only one or two levels.
  • Five-metric evaluation (accuracy, ASR, perturbation rate, cosine, USE) exposes cost/stealth trade-offs, not just raw attack potency.
  • Fine-grained per-model, per-attack breakdown (six tables) diagnoses exactly which architecture is weak against which linguistic phenomenon — dialect, diacritics, visual confusables, syntax.
  • Honest about defense limits: reports where adversarial training plateaus (character-level noise) rather than presenting it as a universal fix.

Limitations

  • Restricted to BERT-family encoders and one task (binary sentiment); no generative/decoder LLMs, no other NLP tasks — explicitly flagged as future work.
  • Word-level "importance" is defined entirely by SHAP; results may not hold under gradient-based or optimization-based word-ranking attacks.
  • Paraphrasing is generated once and shared across all five models — a different regime from the other five per-model attacks, complicating head-to-head "strongest attack" claims.
  • The diacritics collapse may partly reflect distribution shift: diacritics are stripped from the training data entirely, so models never see them during fine-tuning.
  • No statistical significance testing, and a modest 1.08K-sample test set, on any reported accuracy/ASR delta.
  • No released code, and no comparison against non-BERT baselines or the existing Arabic-specific defenses the paper itself surveys.

Open Questions

  1. Would gradient- or optimization-based word-importance ranking, instead of SHAP, change which words get targeted and how much accuracy drops?
  2. How much of the diacritics attack's power is genuine semantic ambiguity versus simple out-of-distribution input the model never trained on?
  3. Does adversarial training generalize across attack types, or is each fortified model robust only to the specific attack it trained against?
  4. How would these six attacks transfer to instruction-tuned or generative Arabic LLMs instead of BERT-style encoders?
  5. Would combining adversarial training with passive defenses (misspelling detectors, KNN token recovery, Dirichlet Neighborhood Ensemble) close the remaining character-level robustness gap?
