How Do LLMs Read Bug Reports? An Empirical Study of Attention in LLMs for Automated Program Repair

Why This Matters

Offers an interpretable signal — not just accuracy — for diagnosing and improving LLM-based bug-repair pipelines and agentic coding systems.

Critical Edge

The paper's most detailed findings — fine-grained attention (RQ2) and developer-alignment (RQ3) — are validated almost entirely on qwen3-32b, the weakest of the three models, while claude-4-sonnet, the strongest performer, is excluded entirely.

Methodology

319 real, single-function bugs — 248 Python (SWE-bench Verified) and 71 Java (Multi-SWE-bench) — are repaired by three LLMs (claude-4-sonnet, gpt-oss-20b, qwen3-32b) at temperature 0 with a standardized prompt. Since internal attention weights aren't accessible across all three, attention is estimated behaviorally: inputs are perturbed and the change in output is measured.

RQ1 — SECTION-LEVEL — bug reports are split into six sections (description, reproduction, expected/actual behavior, version, additional info); every masking coalition is regenerated and Kernel SHAP over CodeBLEU similarity assigns each section a per-report attention score.

RQ2 — COMPONENT-LEVEL — sections are decomposed into 5 NL and 4 code sub-components (stacktrace, test, class/method, imports); UniXcoder-embedding perturbation classifies each report as diffused, localized, or no-attention. Run mainly on qwen3-32b for cost reasons (2,873 perturbations).

RQ3 — HUMAN ALIGNMENT — four developers (5+ yrs experience) rate section importance and key phrases for 100 bugs, compared against model attention via Spearman correlation and phrase-level F1.

Strengths

  • Real, function-level bugs from two established GitHub-derived benchmarks (SWE-bench Verified, Multi-SWE-bench), not synthetic toy examples, with manually verified difficulty labels.
  • Cross-model (1 proprietary + 2 open-source) and cross-language (Python + Java) design shows the diffused-vs-localized pattern holds across models and languages.
  • Introduces a genuinely new artifact — a human-annotated developer-attention dataset — filling a documented gap in existing APR benchmarks.
  • Statistically disciplined: effect sizes reported alongside significance tests, FDR multiple-testing correction, and a difficulty-controlled robustness check rather than raw p-values alone.
  • Concrete case studies (e.g., near-duplicate matplotlib bugs #16344 vs. #16420) ground the statistics in interpretable examples.

Limitations

  • Perturbation-based attention is a black-box behavioral proxy, not a direct measurement of internal attention weights or reasoning.
  • Fine-grained (RQ2) and alignment (RQ3) findings rest almost entirely on qwen3-32b, with only a partial Java-subset check on gpt-oss-20b; claude-4-sonnet is never analyzed at this depth.
  • NL/code decomposition relies on regex heuristics and NLTK tokenization verified by a single author, not cross-checked by multiple annotators.
  • The 100-bug developer dataset uses one annotator per bug; the "ground truth" partly reflects individual subjective judgment.
  • All results use single-sample, temperature-0 greedy decoding — never validated against repeated sampling or pass@k.
  • The Hard-difficulty subset (11 bugs) is too small for reliable stratified conclusions, and the paper's own difficulty-vs-success chi-square test is not significant (p=0.06).

Questions for Further Exploration

  1. Would diffused-vs-localized attention, and its success correlation, replicate on agentic APR systems (SWE-Agent, AutoCodeRover, OpenHands) that iteratively retrieve and re-read context, rather than single-shot repair?
  2. Does the developer-alignment finding hold if claude-4-sonnet — the strongest model, excluded from RQ2/RQ3 — were analyzed directly, given it has the most successful repairs to compare?
  3. Can the developer-attention dataset be used prescriptively — attention-aware prompt reordering, section reweighting, fine-tuning — to actually raise fix rates, beyond a purely correlational relationship?
  4. How sensitive are the reported attention-success associations to non-zero-temperature sampling and multiple repair attempts (pass@k), rather than single deterministic greedy decodes?
  5. Would the same section/component attention patterns hold on noisier, less-curated real-world bug reports, or on languages beyond Python and Java?
