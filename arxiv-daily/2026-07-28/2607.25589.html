Why This Matters

Shows how prompt binding, DICOM polarity flips, and patient-identity leaks can silently corrupt a benchmark that still looks statistically rigorous on the surface.

Critical Edge

The same author designed the flawed benchmark, ran this audit, and adjudicated every claim alone — retrospective, non-preregistered, with no independent lab replication.

Methodology

A retrospective, non-preregistered, single-author review of a project snapshot frozen on 27 Jul 2026 — no model was re-called, no image re-rendered, no label re-annotated. The audit applies a six-state ladder (Intended → Executed → Observed → Analyzed → Reported → Released) with fail-closed identity rules: a filename or console label never counts as proof of identity — only a rendered-image hash, a request-payload trace, or a resolved provider model ID does.

Tracing Python imports, module-level mutable state, and monkey-patch order across the batch runner exposed the Claude prompt-collapse bug. DICOM headers were cross-checked against the case manifest for view position and photometric polarity; call logs were reconciled by byte count and status to separate "missing" from "zero-byte" from "logged-ok-but-empty." Every retained value was then rekeyed by (case, finding) identity instead of positional truncation, and Cochran's Q plus 45 pairwise McNemar tests were recomputed across four statistical variants with Holm correction.

Results

Headline finding · prompt routing

All 60 Claude calls labeled "A" and "B" were traced, via Python import-order analysis, to the same third prompt ("System C") — the reported "A vs. B" contrast never existed as an executed treatment.

Strengths & Limitations

Strengths

  • Pinpoints the exact mechanism — a Python import-by-value plus module-level monkey-patch — that collapsed the A/B prompt contrast, invisible to any filename-level check.
  • Principled three-level repair boundary cleanly separates "fixable arithmetic" from "unrecoverable identity loss," consistently used to withdraw claims rather than quietly patch numbers.
  • Ships a working 8-clause "machine-verifiable benchmark contract" with 36 offline regression tests on synthetic fixtures — directly reusable on other VLM pipelines.
  • Discloses generative-AI tool use and self-audit conflicts of interest, and replaces "hallucination" with the precise "automated report-label discordance."

Limitations

  • Single low-n case study (30 studies / 28 patients) — not a prevalence estimate of how often such failures occur elsewhere.
  • Retrospective, non-preregistered, and adjudicated entirely by the same author who built the flawed original benchmark; no independent replication.
  • Cannot recover provider-side request payloads, historical model snapshots, raw extractor responses, or final train/test split membership.
  • Reconstructed Q/McNemar statistics ignore within-study clustering (14 nested labels per study) and repeated patients — flagged, but not corrected.
  • The contract's Model and Annotation clauses remain "specification only," so roughly a quarter of the 8 clauses is not yet executable code.

Questions for Further Exploration

  • Could the 8-clause contract be prospectively trialed on an independently-run VLM radiology benchmark to measure real false-alarm and missed-failure rates?
  • How much of the +17.8% Cochran's Q change is specifically attributable to the positional-truncation-vs-keyed-join fix, versus confounded effects from the unresolved prompt-collapse and polarity-inversion defects?
  • Would a clustering-aware statistical model (findings nested within studies, repeated patients) meaningfully change the McNemar/Cochran conclusions, even under "forensic reproduction only" framing?
  • Is "automated report-label discordance" likely to be adopted more broadly in place of "hallucination," and how does it relate to prior taxonomies?
  • What would it take to make the Model and Annotation clauses executable, given that provider-resolved model IDs and raw extractor responses are often not exposed by commercial VLM APIs?
