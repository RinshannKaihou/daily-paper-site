Execution-free benchmark for multi-agent orchestration

OrchBench judges multi-agent orchestration plans with a deterministic simulator, and the simulated score tracks real execution quality at r=0.816.

OrchBench: Evaluating Multi-Agent Orchestration Plans in Isolation via Deterministic Simulation ↗

Key Metric
1.3%of tokens
— and 10.3% of the wall-clock time — that real multi-agent execution costs (Table 8): ≈389× token / 110× time reduction on WideSearch, ≈74× / 9.7× on MultiAgentBench.
Why This Matters
Lets researchers screen orchestration strategies and models cheaply before committing to costly real multi-agent runs, informing agent-system design.
Critical Edge
Remove just one of the six validation models (GLM-5.1) and the headline r=0.816 correlation collapses to r=0.421 — statistically indistinguishable from no correlation.

Methodology

1 · DEPENDENCY RESOLUTION
Process subtasks in topological order; each waits on parents + agent availability.
→
2 · AGENT SCHEDULING
Assign subtask to an agent per the plan's α.
→
3 · CONTEXT ACQUISITION
Cross-agent edges apply declared retention ratio R(u,v)=e∈(0,1].
→
4 · CONTEXT COMPRESSION
Compress stored memory once it exceeds per-agent limit L.
→
5 · SUBTASK EXECUTION
Missing declared transfers multiply upstream quality by penalty λ=0.5.
→
6 · STATE UPDATE
Record tokens, time, quality — no worker LLM is ever invoked.
final Score = (Q + E_time + E_token) / 3 — quality, makespan-efficiency, token-efficiency, averaged (Eq. 7)

OrchBench fixes the task-decomposition DAG G=(V,E) as input and asks only that a planner Fθ output an orchestration plan π=(α,R): which agent runs each subtask, and how much information survives each cross-agent handoff. DAGs (10 to 1,000+ subtasks) come from real seed tasks — Finance Agent, DS-1000, Qasper, BIRD-SQL — refined through a generator → structural-judge → semantic-judge → refiner loop (34.3% of sampled DAGs still get rejected). A deterministic six-stage simulator then replays the plan without re-invoking the planner or any worker LLM, scoring quality, makespan-efficiency, and token-efficiency. Nine planners (six base models: GLM-5.1, DeepSeek-V4-Pro/Flash, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Kimi-K2.6, Doubao-Seed-2.0-Mini; three frontier: Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview, Claude-Opus-4.8, GPT-5.5) are evaluated at scales n∈{10,20,50,100} (main set) and {200,500,1000} (MultiAgentBench correlation study), with A_max=100 and λ=0.5 as defaults.

Between 500 and 1,000-task DAGs, cross-agent transfer coverage falls by more than half for Claude and DeepSeek, producing 872.3 and 907.4 missing transfers respectively. Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview is the exception, retaining near-complete coverage (exact figure not reported) and substantially higher quality at n=1,000.

  • Multi-agent orchestration beats a single agent by +0.302 quality under a tight L=16k context limit (0.423→0.725) — but the gap nearly vanishes at L=128k (+0.007), so the benefit is mainly relieving context pressure, not parallelism itself (Table 6).
  • Real-executing only the top-5 tasks by cross-model simulated-score disagreement reaches Spearman ρ=0.754 vs. ρ=0.176 for random task selection (Table 7) — cheap simulation can triage which tasks are worth running for real.
  • Ablating the missing-transfer penalty collapses the benchmark's ability to separate a strong planner (Gemini) from a weak one (Doubao): ΔScore shrinks from 0.079 to 0.001 (Table 10) — the penalty mechanism is what makes the benchmark discriminative.
  • Simulator-guided refinement transfers to reality: adding one simulator-selected handoff raised real execution score from 3.754 to 4.150 / 5 on a 20-task MultiAgentBench workflow (Table 9).

Strengths

  • Clean conceptual separation of orchestration planning from worker execution, addressing a genuine gap in MAS evaluation methodology.
  • Large (74×–389×) and clearly quantified token/time reductions vs. full execution, demonstrated on two independent benchmarks.
  • Transparent reporting of validation weaknesses (leave-one-model-out correlation drop, cross-framework non-transferability) rather than only favorable numbers.
  • Systematic 10–1,000 subtask sweep reveals a coordination-failure phenomenon largely invisible to end-to-end evaluation.
  • Concrete downstream applications shown — cost-efficient model selection, task-difficulty estimation, simulator-guided plan refinement with measured real gains.

Limitations

  • Headline r=0.816 correlation rests on just six models and is highly sensitive to single-model removal (drops to r=0.421, non-significant).
  • Cross-framework correlation is uneven: strong for Claude Code/SWE-mini, markedly weaker for OpenHands (0.63, and only 0.09–0.27 cross-checked).
  • Simulated time/token metrics don't reliably predict real resource use across frameworks (near-zero cross-framework correlation) — only the composite score is validated.
  • DAG generation itself has a ~34% rejection rate, with residual failure modes persisting into the accepted pool at larger scales.
  • The missing-transfer penalty λ and several simulator cost constants are hand-tuned design choices that materially shape discriminative power.

Questions for Further Exploration

  • How does the r=0.816 simulation-to-real correlation hold up with a substantially larger and more diverse set of planner models, given its demonstrated fragility to single-model removal?
  • Could the simulator's quality/compression model be learned or calibrated directly from real execution traces rather than hand-specified, improving cross-framework transfer of the efficiency metrics?
  • What architectural or prompting interventions would most directly reduce the "missing transfer" failure mode at extreme scale (1,000+ subtasks), beyond simply adding more agents?
  • Would the coordination-failure findings replicate on non-DAG-shaped real workflows — cyclic negotiation or dynamic re-planning — outside the fixed-DAG assumption?
  • How sensitive is the "transfer coverage matters more than agent count" conclusion to the specific λ=0.5 penalty, and would a differently-calibrated penalty change which models rank best at each scale?
