> **一句话总结：** 论文对五个阿拉伯语/多语言BERT模型（AraBERT、MARBERT、CaMeLBERT、mBERT、XLM-T）在阿拉伯语情感分类任务上系统评测了三类粒度（字符级、词级、句级）共六种黑盒非定向攻击，发现变音符号插入可让AraBERT准确率从94%暴跌至2%（降92%），而释义改写（paraphrase）攻击对所有模型平均造成76%的准确率下降；对抗训练能提升鲁棒性（如MARBERT在释义攻击下提升48.4个百分点），但对字符级噪声（尤其变音符号和视觉形近字替换）的防御效果仍然有限。

> **TL;DR:** This paper benchmarks adversarial robustness of five BERT-family Arabic/multilingual models (AraBERT, MARBERT, CaMeLBERT, mBERT, XLM-T) on binary Arabic sentiment classification using six black-box, non-targeted attacks spanning character-level (diacritics, visual, phonetic), word-level (SHAP-guided AraBERT-MLM synonym substitution, conjunction substitution), and sentence-level (AraT5 paraphrasing) granularity. Diacritics insertion is the most damaging character-level attack, cutting AraBERT's accuracy from 94% to 2% (a 92% drop) while MARBERT only drops 5% (99%→94%) and mBERT keeps the highest similarity scores (90% cosine, 68% USE) at just 10% perturbation rate. Paraphrasing is the strongest attack overall, degrading every victim model by an average of 76% (e.g., MARBERT 99%→14%, an 85-point drop) despite an 83% perturbation rate, while preserving semantic similarity (39.4% cosine, 61.5% USE). Adversarial training then recovers substantial robustness — e.g., MARBERT gains +48.40 points post-training accuracy under paraphrase and XLM-T gains +37.40 points — but character-level noise remains hard to defend: even after adversarial training, AraBERT's diacritic-attacked accuracy stays at only 22.61% (a 71.39-point residual drop) and its visual-attack accuracy at 20.87% (73.13-point residual drop).

## 核心贡献

- 首次针对阿拉伯语NLP模型进行系统性对抗鲁棒性评测：覆盖字符级（变音符号插入、视觉形近字替换、方言语音替换）、词级（基于SHAP重要性排序的AraBERT MLM同义词替换、连接词替换）、句级（AraT5释义改写）共六种黑盒非定向攻击。
- 设计了完整的多维评测体系：准确率、攻击成功率（ASR）、基于Levenshtein距离的扰动率（perturbation rate）、余弦相似度和通用句子编码器（USE）语义相似度，兼顾攻击有效性与语义/语法保真度约束。
- 探索基于对抗训练（adversarial training）的防御手段，量化其在六种攻击、五个模型上的鲁棒性提升幅度与残余弱点，指出对抗训练能显著改善词级/句级攻击下的表现，但对字符级噪声（尤其是变音符号和视觉替换）改善有限。
- 揭示模型间的显著差异：MARBERT综合最鲁棒（除释义攻击外多数攻击下准确率降幅在个位数），AraBERT对变音符号、视觉替换、连接词操纵最脆弱，CaMeLBERT对方言语音替换（phonetic）最脆弱（暴露出跨方言泛化问题），XLM-T对MLM词替换攻击降幅最大（44%）。

## Key Contributions

- First systematic adversarial robustness study specifically targeting Arabic NLP models, applying a diverse suite of Arabic-tailored attacks across all three granularity levels (character, word, sentence) using edit-based, importance-based (SHAP), and paraphrase-based generation strategies.
- A thorough multi-metric evaluation protocol combining accuracy, Attack Success Rate (ASR), Levenshtein-distance-based perturbation rate, cosine similarity, and Universal Sentence Encoder (USE) similarity — jointly capturing attack potency and semantic/grammatical fidelity constraints.
- An adversarial-training defense study across all five victim models and six attacks, quantifying both robustness gains (up to +48.40 points) and persistent vulnerabilities (character-level noise resistance remains weak even after fine-tuning).
- Comparative model-level findings: MARBERT is the most robust overall (e.g., only 1.5% decrease under conjunction attack even before adversarial training), AraBERT is the weakest against diacritics/visual/conjunction attacks, CaMeLBERT is uniquely vulnerable to phonetic (dialectal) substitution, and XLM-T suffers the largest degradation under MLM-based word substitution.

## Methodology

**Task and data.** Binary Arabic sentiment classification (positive/negative) on a ~16K-tweet dataset (14.7K train / 1.08K test) combining MSA and dialectal Arabic tweets, built from prior sentiment corpora (ArSAS, XLM-T Twitter data) and released by one of the authors on HuggingFace. Preprocessing strips special characters, non-Arabic characters, punctuation, and emojis (note: diacritics are absent from the raw dataset — diacritic attacks therefore inject vowel marks the models essentially never saw during fine-tuning), applies Farasa segmentation, and tokenizes with SentencePiece. Class imbalance is addressed via down-/up-sampling.

**Victim models.** Five BERT-based models fine-tuned on the sentiment task: AraBERT (MSA-focused), MARBERT (MSA + dialectal Arabic), CaMeLBERT-mix (classical + MSA + dialectal), mBERT (104-language multilingual BERT), and XLM-T (cross-lingual model further trained on Twitter data). Pre-attack (clean) test accuracies: MARBERT 99%, CaMeLBERT 95%, AraBERT 94%, XLM-T 94%, mBERT 87%.

**Attack setting.** All attacks are black-box (no access to victim parameters/gradients) and non-targeted (goal is any misclassification), following the adversarial-attack taxonomy of Alajmi et al. (the same first author's prior spam-classifier robustness work). Six attacks are used:
- *Character-level (edit-based)*: Diacritics (insert vowel marks that can flip word meaning, e.g., الحُمّة/الحَمّة/الحِمّة), Visual (substitute visually similar letters differing by a single dot, e.g., خ‑ح‑ج), Phonetic (character substitutions that mimic dialectal spelling variants across ~25 Arab dialects).
- *Word-level (importance-based)*: importance is computed via SHAP (`shap.Explainer` with a token masker, `fixed_context=1`, masking one token at a time), ranking words by |SHAP value| and perturbing the highest-scoring word first. "AraBERT MLM" replaces top-scoring words with AraBERT masked-language-model synonym predictions; "Conjunctions" replaces Arabic conjunctions with semantically similar alternatives, prioritized by SHAP score.
- *Sentence-level (paraphrase-based)*: full-sentence rephrasing via the AraT5 abstractive-summarization/paraphrase model, generating a single shared adversarial example per input applied identically to all five victim models (unlike the other attacks, which are generated per-victim using each model's own SHAP scores).

**Metrics.** Accuracy (Eq. 1), Attack Success Rate = successful flips / total adversarial samples where the clean prediction was originally correct (Eq. 2), Levenshtein-distance-based perturbation rate (Eq. 3), cosine similarity between original/adversarial sentence embeddings (Eq. 4), and USE-based semantic similarity via arccos angular distance (Eq. 5).

**Defense.** Standard adversarial training (fine-tuning each victim model on a mix of clean and attack-generated adversarial examples) is applied per model per attack type, and the fortified models are re-evaluated on the same attacks (Table 10).

## Results & Highlights

- **Diacritics (character-level)**: AraBERT drops from 94% to 2% accuracy (92% decrease, the single worst result in the paper), CaMeLBERT drops from 95% to 15% (80% decrease), while MARBERT is by far the most robust, dropping only from 99% to 94% (5% decrease). mBERT achieves the lowest perturbation rate (10%) alongside the highest cosine (90%) and USE (68%) similarity among the models (Table 4).
- **Visual substitution (character-level)**: AraBERT collapses to 0% post-attack accuracy (94% decrease from 94%), whereas MARBERT retains 86% accuracy (only 13% decrease). mBERT requires only an 18% perturbation rate to induce a 39% accuracy drop while keeping the highest similarity scores (53% cosine, 72% USE) (Table 5).
- **Phonetic/dialectal substitution (character-level)**: CaMeLBERT is the weakest model here, dropping 31% (95%→64%) despite being pretrained on dialectal data — the paper flags this as a generalization gap to unseen dialectal perturbations. MARBERT is barely affected (99%→97%, only 2% decrease) (Table 6).
- **AraBERT-MLM word substitution (word-level)**: XLM-T is hit hardest, dropping 44% (94%→50%, the highest perturbation rate among models for this attack), followed by mBERT (39.2% decrease, but highest cosine similarity at 36%) and MARBERT (39% decrease). AraBERT is comparatively the least vulnerable here, retaining 81% post-attack accuracy (13% decrease) (Table 7).
- **Conjunction substitution (word-level)**: AraBERT and XLM-T suffer the heaviest drops, 58% (94%→36%) and 39% (94%→55%) respectively, while MARBERT is barely affected (99%→97.5%, only 1.5% decrease) and CaMeLBERT achieves the best perturbation rate (24%) and similarity scores (96% cosine, 52% USE) (Table 8).
- **Paraphrase (sentence-level, AraT5, shared across models)**: this is the single most effective attack, reducing accuracy by an average of 76% across all five models — MARBERT 99%→14% (85-point drop, the largest of any model/attack combination in the paper), XLM-T 94%→11% (83-point drop), AraBERT 94%→14% (80-point drop), CaMeLBERT 95%→22% (73-point drop), and mBERT 87%→28% (59-point drop, the smallest, making mBERT the most paraphrase-resistant model). The perturbation rate reaches 83% (the highest of any attack) while cosine similarity averages 39.43% and USE similarity averages 61.48% (Table 9, Figs. 9-10).
- **Adversarial training defense (Table 10)**: gains are substantial for word- and sentence-level attacks but weak for character-level noise. MARBERT gains the most under paraphrase (+48.40 points, reaching 62.40% post-attack accuracy) and CaMeLBERT gains +45.30 points under paraphrase (67.30%); XLM-T gains +37.40 points under paraphrase and +28.33 points under MLM substitution. In contrast, character-level defenses remain weak: after adversarial training, AraBERT's diacritic accuracy is only 22.61% (still a 71.39-point residual decrease, improvement of just +20.61 points) and its visual-attack accuracy is only 20.87% (73.13-point residual decrease). Clean accuracy after adversarial training drops only 1-2% for the fortified models, at the cost of 25-40% additional training time.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty**: The paper's main claim to novelty is being the first comprehensive adversarial-robustness study across all three granularity levels specifically for Arabic BERT-family models. This is a reasonable and underserved niche, and the six-attack, five-model, multi-metric design (accuracy + ASR + perturbation rate + two similarity metrics) is more thorough than most prior single-attack Arabic studies cited in Table 1/Table 2 of the related work.

**Rigor and paper-specific weak points**:
- The word-level attacks (AraBERT-MLM substitution and Conjunctions) both rely on the *same* SHAP-based importance ranking and the same masking scheme (`fixed_context=1`) to select which token to perturb; there's no comparison against alternative importance methods (e.g., leave-one-out deletion scoring, gradient-based WIR used by TextFooler/DeepWordBug) that the paper's own related-work section discusses. This means "word-level vulnerability" findings are conditioned entirely on SHAP's particular notion of importance, not word-level attacks in general.
- The paraphrase attack is fundamentally different in construction from the other five — it generates one shared adversarial set applied identically to all victim models, whereas character/word-level attacks are generated per-model using each model's own SHAP scores. The paper acknowledges this (Section 4.1) but the cross-attack "which attack is strongest" comparisons in the conclusion somewhat blur this methodological asymmetry.
- The diacritics attack exploits a data quirk rather than purely linguistic vulnerability: diacritics are explicitly stripped from the raw dataset during preprocessing, so victim models never see diacritized text during fine-tuning at all. The 92% AraBERT accuracy collapse is therefore partly a distribution-shift artifact (out-of-training-distribution input) rather than solely evidence of poor robustness to genuine adversarial semantics — the paper does not disentangle these two explanations.
- Test set size (1.08K samples, drawn from a rebalanced 16K corpus) is modest for a binary classification benchmark; no confidence intervals, variance across runs, or statistical significance testing are reported for any of the accuracy/ASR deltas, so small differences (e.g., MARBERT's "1.5%" or "0.1%" changes in Table 8/10) should be read with caution.
- No comparison against non-BERT baselines (CNN, LSTM) or against existing Arabic-specific defense techniques cited in the related work (misspelling detectors, KNN-based token recovery, Dirichlet Neighborhood Ensemble) — the only defense evaluated is vanilla adversarial training, so the paper cannot say how it stacks up against alternatives it itself surveys in Section 2.
- Table 3's illustrative attack examples and the SHAP force plots in Appendix A are useful qualitative evidence, but no human evaluation of grammaticality/semantic preservation is reported beyond the automated cosine/USE similarity scores, despite the paper explicitly listing grammatical correctness as a required attack constraint.

**Reproducibility**: The sentiment dataset is public on HuggingFace, and the attacks/metrics are described with enough formulaic detail (Eqs. 1-5) to reimplement. However, no code repository is linked in the paper, and key SHAP/attack hyperparameters (e.g., number of top-k words perturbed, AraT5 decoding settings) are only partially specified.

**Significance**: The findings are practically useful for anyone deploying Arabic sentiment models — the diacritics/visual/paraphrase vulnerabilities are concrete and severe (single-digit residual accuracy in the worst cases), and the adversarial-training results usefully show where standard defenses plateau (character-level noise). The results are unlikely to generalize immediately beyond binary sentiment classification or beyond BERT-family encoders, which the authors acknowledge as future work (extending to other tasks and to GPT-style models).

## Strengths

- Broadest granularity coverage (character/word/sentence) applied to Arabic LMs specifically, addressing a well-documented literature gap (Table 1/Table 2 show almost all prior black-box Arabic-adjacent attack studies cover only one or two granularity levels).
- Rich, multi-metric evaluation (accuracy, ASR, Levenshtein perturbation rate, cosine, USE) rather than accuracy alone, allowing readers to judge attack cost/stealth trade-offs, not just potency.
- Clear per-model, per-attack breakdown (Tables 4-10) enables fine-grained diagnosis of which architectures are weak against which specific linguistic phenomena (dialect, diacritics, visual confusables, syntax).
- Honest reporting of adversarial training's limits (character-level noise remains hard to defend) rather than presenting the defense as a universal fix.

## Limitations

- Restricted to BERT-family encoder models and a single task (binary sentiment classification); no evaluation of generative/decoder LLMs (GPT-style) or other NLP tasks (dialect ID, NER, QA), which the authors explicitly flag as future work.
- Word-level attack importance scoring depends entirely on SHAP; results may not generalize to attacks using different importance-ranking or optimization-based strategies.
- Paraphrase attack uses a single shared adversarial set across models, an architecturally different attack-generation regime from the other five, complicating direct "most effective attack overall" comparisons.
- Diacritic attack effectiveness may partly reflect a training-data distribution gap (diacritics absent from fine-tuning data) rather than purely a robustness deficiency.
- No statistical significance testing on reported accuracy/ASR deltas; modest test-set size (1.08K samples).
- No code release accompanying the paper; only the dataset is public.

## Relevance

该论文与阿拉伯语等低资源、复杂形态语言下的大模型安全评测直接相关，其"变音符号/形近字/方言替换+SHAP重要词替换+释义改写"的攻击分级框架和对抗训练防御的量化结果，可为设计更细粒度、更贴合具体语言学特征的鲁棒性评测方法提供参考。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- Would gradient-based or optimization-based word-importance ranking (rather than SHAP) change which words get targeted and how much word-level accuracy degrades?
- How much of the diacritics-attack effectiveness is attributable to out-of-distribution input (diacritics absent from training data) versus genuine semantic ambiguity introduced by vowel marks?
- Does adversarial training generalize across attack types (e.g., does training against diacritics also improve robustness to visual substitution), or is each fortified model only robust to the specific attack it was trained against?
- How would these six attacks transfer to instruction-tuned or generative Arabic LLMs (e.g., Arabic-adapted GPT variants) instead of BERT-style encoders?
- Would combining the adversarial-training defense with the passive defenses surveyed in Section 2 (misspelling detectors, KNN-based token recovery, Dirichlet Neighborhood Ensemble) close the remaining character-level robustness gap?
