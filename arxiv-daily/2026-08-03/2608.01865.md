> **一句话总结：** 本文通过对 Whisper-small 编码器在中文构音障碍语音上进行逐层探测（音素边界、音素识别、识别难度），发现构音障碍主要破坏中高层表征而非低层声学特征，并据此指导 LoRA 适配——仅在第 7 层适配即可达到全编码器适配 3.5% 相对误差范围内的 CER，而高层适配反而损害构音障碍语音识别。

> **TL;DR:** This paper presents a layer-wise probing study of a Whisper-small encoder on Mandarin dysarthric speech (CDSD corpus) under three transcript-matched conditions (original dysarthric, speaker-conditioned TTS-resynthesized, unconditioned TTS), revealing a task-dependent hierarchy where phoneme boundary information stays weak at every layer, phoneme identity matures in middle layers, and recognition difficulty is encoded in the deepest layers; guided by these probes, single-layer LoRA at layer 7 reaches 71.26% CER on dysarthric speech, within a 3.5% relative margin of full 12-layer adaptation (68.85%), while the 5–8 subset reaches 70.56% (within 2.48%), showing that middle-layer adapters are the most parameter-efficient targets for low-resource Mandarin dysarthric ASR.

## 核心贡献

- 提出一种转录本匹配的三条件分析框架（原始构音障碍语音 d、说话人条件化 TTS 重合成语音 r、无条件 TTS 语音 u），三者共享相同文本，从而在表征分析中消除了词汇分布混杂，可隔离"病理导致的发音偏差"与"说话人/合成因素"。
- 在 Whisper-small 编码器的全部 12 层上系统性部署三个互补探针（帧级音素边界检测、序列级音素识别 CTC、句级识别难度预测），首次给出中文构音障碍 ASR 的逐层信息分布图谱。
- 发现任务相关的层级依赖结构：音素边界信息在构音障碍语音的每一层都很弱；音素身份在中-上层（第 6 层后趋于平台）变得可恢复；识别难度集中在最深几层（第 5 层后陡升，第 12 层达峰）。
- 证明跨条件表征相似度发散随深度增长——d–u（构音障碍 vs 无条件 TTS）发散最大，d–r（约匹配说话人）在深层的上升说明构音障碍扭曲的是高层表征而非早期声学特征。
- 将探测结论直接用于 LoRA 适配层选择：单层适配第 7 层即接近全适配效果，而 9–12 层适配反而恶化构音障碍 CER，支持"中层感知性适配"这一低资源策略。

## Key Contributions

- A **transcript-matched, three-condition framework** (original dysarthric d, speaker-conditioned zero-shot TTS-resynthesized r via OmniVoice, and unconditioned TTS u) that holds the lexical content invariant so that pathology-induced articulatory distortions can be isolated from speaker identity and synthesis artifacts.
- A **multi-level, layer-wise probing analysis** of the frozen Whisper-small encoder (layers 0–12) on Mandarin dysarthric speech, covering phoneme boundary detection (linear probe, F1), phoneme recognition (CTC probe, tone-sensitive and tone-agnostic PER), and utterance-level recognition-difficulty prediction (linear softmax, Macro-F1).
- Identification of a **task-dependent representational hierarchy**: boundary information is weak for dysarthric speech at every layer, phoneme identity matures through the middle layers and plateaus after layer 6, and recognition difficulty is encoded predominantly in the deepest layers.
- A **cross-condition similarity divergence analysis** showing that representational distance between dysarthric and synthetic speech grows with depth, with the d–u gap largest in deep layers — evidence that disordered articulation distorts high-level rather than low-level acoustic representations.
- A **probing-to-PEFT bridge**: layer-selection LoRA experiments confirming that single-layer adaptation at layer 7 and subset adaptation on layers 5–8 recover most of the gain of full encoder tuning for dysarthric speech, while upper-layer (9–12) adaptation is counterproductive — motivating layer-aware, parameter-efficient adaptation for low-resource Mandarin dysarthric ASR.

## Methodology

The study probes a frozen Whisper-small encoder (12 Transformer blocks, 768-dim hidden states) on Mandarin dysarthric speech. Hidden states are extracted from the encoder input (layer 0) and the output of each encoder block (layers 1–12). Probing is restricted to the encoder because motor-speech disorders primarily degrade acoustic-articulatory realizations, whereas the decoder mostly encodes linguistic and sequence-generation priors.

**Three-condition framework.** For each dysarthric utterance x_d with transcript y, two synthetic counterparts sharing the identical lexical content are generated: a speaker-conditioned zero-shot TTS resynthesis x_r (OmniVoice conditioned on the source transcript and the dysarthric audio, preserving speaker identity and atypical acoustics) and an unconditioned TTS x_u (same transcript, neutral speaker, no dysarthric conditioning). The tuple (x_d, x_r, x_u, y) controls for lexical confounds.

**Paired similarity.** Mean-pooled cosine similarity between conditions is computed at every layer for the three contrasts d–r, d–u, and r–u, interpreted as controlled approximations rather than perfect causal decompositions.

**Three probes.**
- *Probe A — Phoneme Boundary Detection:* frame-level linear probe over a 4-frame context, trained with BCEWithLogitsLoss and positive-class weighting; boundary labels from Montreal Forced Aligner at Whisper's 20 ms resolution; reported as F1.
- *Probe B — Phoneme Recognition:* a lightweight CTC probe (layer norm, bottleneck projection dim 128, residual temporal convolution, linear classifier) predicts initial-final-tone units; optimized with CTC loss; reported as tone-sensitive and tone-agnostic PER.
- *Probe C — Recognition Difficulty Prediction:* difficulty tiers (low/medium/high via 33.33rd/66.67th percentiles) are derived from CER produced by an external Whisper-large decoder; a linear softmax classifier over mean-pooled states is trained with 5-fold cross-validation; reported as Macro-F1.

**LoRA adaptation.** Adapters (rank r=64, α=128, dropout 0.1) are inserted into the encoder self-attention projections (q/k/v/out_proj) of selected layers while the decoder stays frozen; trained with AdamW (lr 3e-5, batch 128, 6 epochs, cosine schedule, weight decay 0.03). Single-layer (1–12), contiguous subsets (1–4, 5–8, 9–12), and full encoder adaptation are compared across all three speech conditions.

**Dataset.** CDSD (Chinese Dysarthria Speech Database): 133 hours, 44 speakers, ~55,850 utterances, speaker-disjoint 70/15/15 split, all audio resampled to 16 kHz mono.

## Results & Highlights

- **Baseline ASR gap (Table II, Whisper-small, no adaptation):** Dysarthric speech scores **CER 105.08% / WER 118.46%** (CER > 100% reflects CDSD's severity), versus TTS-resynthesized **CER 44.26% / WER 55.02%** and unconditioned TTS **CER 36.63% / WER 38.99%** — a >68 percentage-point CER gap between dysarthric and unconditioned TTS under identical transcripts, isolating acoustic-phonetic factors as a major contributor to recognition failure.
- **Single-layer LoRA (Table III, dysarthric condition):** adapting only **layer 7 reaches 71.26% CER**, within a **3.5% relative margin** of full 12-layer adaptation (**68.85% CER**); layer 8 is best for the resynthesized condition (26.78%), consistent with speaker-conditioned resynthesis carrying over atypical acoustics.
- **Subset LoRA (Table III):** the **layers 5–8 subset reaches 70.56% CER** on dysarthric speech, within a **2.48% relative margin** of full adaptation, and is the strongest of the three contiguous groups tested (1–4, 5–8, 9–12).
- **Upper-layer adaptation hurts dysarthric speech:** adapting **layers 9–12 steadily worsens dysarthric CER, rising to 80.28% at layer 12**, whereas unconditioned TTS shows the opposite trend — its best single layer is **layer 9 at 13.39% CER**, even edging out full adaptation (14.18%).
- **Phoneme recognition plateau (Fig. 3):** PER drops quickly through early-to-middle layers and plateaus after **layer 6**, so phoneme identity is most recoverable from the middle-to-upper encoder; tone-sensitive PER is consistently higher than tone-agnostic PER, with the largest gap for dysarthric speech — Mandarin tone is a persistent error source.
- **Boundary probing (Fig. 2):** the three conditions are sharply separated and stay separated across the whole encoder, with dysarthric F1 lowest and nearly flat — no layer recovers strong boundary structure for atypical speech.
- **Difficulty prediction (Fig. 4):** Macro-F1 stays near chance through early layers and climbs steeply beyond **layer 5**, peaking at layer 12 — recognition difficulty is encoded predominantly in the deepest representations.
- **Similarity divergence (Fig. 1):** divergence is low in layers 0–3, rises through middle layers 4–8, and spikes in deep layers 9–12, with the d–u pair largest — disordered articulation distorts high-level rather than low-level representations.

## Critical Assessment

- **Novelty:** The combination of (a) a transcript-matched three-condition contrast with speaker-conditioned zero-shot TTS, (b) multi-granularity probing, and (c) using the probe profiles to *select* LoRA layers is genuinely novel for Mandarin dysarthric ASR. However, the closest related work (Yue et al., ICASSP 2026, ref [16]) already probes Whisper for dysarthric detection and severity assessment; this paper extends that thread rather than inaugurating it, and the layer-wise probing methodology itself borrows heavily from Pasad et al. (ASRU 2021, ref [15]).
- **Rigor:** Probe hyperparameters, splits, and metrics are reported in full. The interpretive caution around the d–r / d–u / r–u contrasts being "controlled approximations rather than perfect causal decompositions" is honest, because TTS-resynthesis introduces its own synthesis artifacts that the design cannot fully remove — the authors themselves note that the d vs TTS comparison "does not fully isolate articulation from synthesis effects." A missing ablation is the absence of a tone-only or initial-only phoneme recognition breakdown that would localize where tone errors concentrate across layers.
- **Reproducibility:** Strong. The backbone (Whisper-small), dataset (CDSD), TTS engine (OmniVoice), forced aligner (MFA), and key hyperparameters (LoRA r=64/α=128, lr 3e-5, 6 epochs) are all named. The speaker-disjoint 70/15/15 split and 5-fold CV for difficulty prediction are specified. Code release is not mentioned in the text, which slightly lowers practical reproducibility.
- **Significance:** The CER numbers are not deployable in absolute terms (dysarthric CER of 105.08% baseline, 68.85% after full LoRA), and the authors explicitly state their focus is the *relative* layer-wise structure rather than a deployable system. The practical takeaway — middle-layer LoRA matches full adaptation within ~2.5–3.5% relative margin — is useful for low-resource settings but the deltas are modest and untested against stronger PEFT baselines such as AdaLoRA or self-training (refs [11], [12]), which are cited as related work but not compared.
- **Red flags:** (1) The baseline dysarthric CER exceeds 100%, signaling an extremely hard setting where even "improved" CER (~69%) is far from usable — generalization to milder dysarthria populations is unexamined. (2) Only one backbone (Whisper-small) and one dataset (CDSD) are studied; the hierarchy claims (boundary weak, identity mid, difficulty deep) are not validated on UASpeech/TORGO or on larger Whisper variants, so their universality is unproven. (3) The difficulty tiers are derived from Whisper-large CER, i.e., a *model-specific* proxy rather than a clinical severity marker — the authors acknowledge this, but it means the difficulty probe measures "Whisper-large confusion," not articulatory impairment per se.

## Strengths

- Clean, controlled three-condition design that holds the transcript invariant to isolate articulatory from speaker/synthesis factors.
- Three complementary probes (boundary / phoneme / difficulty) at three granularities give a richer layer-profile than single-task probing.
- Direct link from probing insights to a concrete, parameter-efficient downstream recipe (middle-layer LoRA), closing the loop between analysis and adaptation.
- Honest interpretation of TTS contrasts as approximations and explicit acknowledgment that absolute CER is not deployable.
- Detailed reporting of probe and adaptation hyperparameters, dataset split, and the use of an external Whisper-large decoder for a more stable difficulty signal.

## Limitations

- Single backbone (Whisper-small) and single corpus (CDSD); layer-hierarchy claims are not validated on English dysarthric corpora (UASpeech, TORGO) or on larger Whisper models.
- No comparison against other PEFT baselines (AdaLoRA, self-training, prompt-based methods) that the paper itself cites as related work — the LoRA results are only compared to full-encoder adaptation.
- The d vs TTS comparison cannot fully separate articulatory distortion from TTS synthesis artifacts, so some of the reported divergence may be synthesis-induced.
- Absolute CER remains very high (≥69% even after full LoRA), so the method is an analysis contribution rather than a deployable system.
- The recognition-difficulty target is derived from a specific ASR model (Whisper-large) rather than from clinical severity scores, limiting its interpretability as a disorder marker.
- Tone analysis is reported only as tone-sensitive vs tone-agnostic PER aggregates; no per-tone or per-initial/final error breakdown is provided.

## Relevance

对于关注低资源/病理语音 ASR、参数高效微调（PEFT/LoRA）以及 Transformer 表征可解释性的研究者，本文提供了一个可直接借鉴的"逐层探测 → 层选择 → LoRA 适配"闭环方法论，特别是在中文构音障碍场景下验证了中层适配的优势，对面向特殊人群的鲁棒语音识别与表征分析具有参考价值。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- Does the task-dependent hierarchy (boundary weak everywhere, phoneme identity mid, difficulty deep) replicate on English dysarthric corpora (UASpeech, TORGO) and on larger backbones such as Whisper-medium/large-v3?
- How do middle-layer LoRA gains compare against AdaLoRA, self-training, and Perceiver-prompt baselines under matched parameter budgets on CDSD?
- Would combining boundary-targeted auxiliary losses (to strengthen the weak boundary signal at every layer) with middle-layer LoRA further reduce dysarthric CER?
- How sensitive are the probing conclusions to the choice of zero-shot TTS engine — would a different speaker-conditioned TTS (or a non-TTS voice-conversion reference) change the d–r divergence profile?
- Can the recognition-difficulty probe be re-grounded against clinical severity scores (e.g., intelligibility ratings) instead of Whisper-large CER, to test whether the deepest layers encode disorder severity itself?
- Does the upper-layer counterproductivity for dysarthric speech (9–12 worsens CER) persist when adapters are inserted into feed-forward rather than self-attention projections, or when the decoder is jointly adapted?
