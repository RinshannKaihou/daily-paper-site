01

How it works

The ruler — verification frozen before search

A human curator supplies the question plus the "ruler": submission format, feasibility conditions, objective, normalization, comparison value, and a gain floor δ — the smallest improvement counted as meaningful. All frozen before the agent searches.

  • A record requires feasibility V(x)=1 and improvement Δ(x)≥δ — structurally excluding reward hacking and precision artifacts.
  • Rigorous bounds must use exact arithmetic or outward rounding that controls numerical error.
  • Theorems need both finite checked witnesses and a mathematical reduction covering the full claim.
  • Within a campaign every branch is scored by the same frozen ruler; between campaigns, a new record becomes the next reference.

The agent — three pillars, model weights fixed

Research state (code, running jobs, intermediate results, decision rationale) lives outside model context, on "Breadboard": isolated environments where long-running jobs survive session boundaries, with persistent storage and an isolated verifier.

  • Versioned branches + backtracking — each alternative keeps implementation, tests, evidence; any commit recoverable.
  • Hierarchical memory — short-term investigation context vs. long-term lessons and documented dead ends; handover notes bound the working context.
  • Independent review — a different model (identity recorded) audits whether evidence justifies the claimed progress.
Épi-turbo: lightweight config for short algorithm-design tasks — one continuous session, per-call budget reservation, stop rule on stagnation; independent review and extra validation switched off.

03

Strengths vs. limitations

Strengths

  • Verification-first design — ruler and gain floor fixed before search; explicit tolerances; exact arithmetic or controlled outward rounding for rigorous bounds.
  • Every headline ships a checkable artifact — dual vectors, rational witnesses, the explicit 8×8 Spencer matrix, Tuza certificates; independent checkers on the largest computations.
  • Real breadth under one protocol — 12 numerical + 8 construction families, a theorem extension, and a coding benchmark, including a limitation theorem (Cohn–Elkies non-sharpness), not just better numbers.
  • Unusually honest reporting — single-run disclosure, weak-reference acknowledgment, archived comparisons kept traceable as registries evolve.
  • Human-understanding agenda — branches, dead ends, and review assessments retained for inspection.

Limitations

  • No agent-vs-agent comparison on the math tasks; single-run, n=30 FrontierOR evaluation with no significance testing.
  • Épi-turbo's win is speed-dominated — loses raw quality on the majority of comparable instances and produced the only infeasible solution.
  • Safeguards disabled where they matter — review and extra validation off in turbo, justified only as "cost larger than benefit," with no data.
  • "Self-improving" means method-level — weights are fixed; the term invites conflation with test-time-training systems.
  • Reproducibility gaps — campaign compute undisclosed; proprietary frontier models; agent code not released (result artifacts only).
  • Uncosted human curation — rulers, references, gain floors are manual; record references are a snapshot of fast-moving registries; Tuza remains open in general.
04

Open questions this paper leaves

Q1How much record output survives on an open-weight mid-tier model — architecture vs. raw capability?
Q2Would FunSearch / AlphaEvolve reproduce the same records under equal budgets, enabling an agent-vs-agent leaderboard?
Q3Can the ruler (feasibility, normalization, gain floor) be auto-generated and cross-checked safely, removing the human-curation bottleneck?
Q4Does independent review measurably reduce reward hacking or wasted exploration in long campaigns?
Q5Do the Tuza certificate techniques extend to four neighborhood types or clique parts larger than 8?
Q6What is the per-record compute/energy accounting — do memory, branching, and review compound across campaigns?
