> **一句话总结：** 针对MoE大模型RL训练中rollout与训练引擎路由不一致导致的"路由器漂移"（router drift）问题，北大/上海创新研究院等提出RDC（Router Drift Control）：用每个响应在rollout与训练两阶段的逐层专家使用分布的TV距离作为响应级漂移分数，按长度分桶过滤漂移最高的约8%响应、其余响应保留原生训练路由（与TIS叠加使用），从而避免R3路由重放引入的梯度偏置——在Qwen3-30B-A3B上，同步训练（2048/128小批次）下比TIS在ZebraLogic/AIME24/AIME25分别+7.9/+6.4/+4.3个百分点，全异步训练下AIME24+25平均峰值比TIS高5.5分、比R3高4.5分，且训练后期不塌缩。

> **TL;DR:** RL for LLMs is silently off-policy (Megatron-BF16 trainer vs vLLM-FP8 rollout engines, sequential mini-batch updates, asynchronous policy lag), and in Mixture-of-Experts models this shows up as *router drift*: the same token gets routed to different experts at rollout vs training, changing which expert parameter blocks receive gradients — an effect invisible to token probabilities (two different expert sets can produce identical logits). RDC scores each response by the layer-averaged total-variation distance between rollout and training expert-usage distributions, then filters the top ~8% highest-drift responses (ranked within response-length buckets) while letting retained responses keep their native training routes, unlike R3/Rollout Routing Replay which the authors prove (Props. 1–2, Appendix B) biases gradients by zeroing out experts selected only by the current training router. On Qwen3-30B-A3B trained with GRPO+DAPO objectives on DAPO-Math-17K: at high staleness (2048-rollout/128-update) RDC+TIS beats TIS alone by +7.9 pts ZebraLogic (86.1 vs 78.2), +6.4 pts AIME24 (77.0 vs 70.6), +4.3 pts AIME25 (67.2 vs 62.9); under fully asynchronous RL it improves the average peak on AIME24/25 by 5.5 pts over TIS and 4.5 pts over R3 (71.2/59.6 vs TIS 65.0/54.8 and R3 68.7/53.1), while uncorrected GRPO collapses after ~100 steps (reward turns negative, response length shrinks ~6000→~2000 tokens). Caveats: single model + single math training set, peak-of-trajectory reporting with no seed variance, no q-ratio ablation, and at low staleness RDC actually trails plain TIS on AIME24 (78.5 vs 80.0) and MATH-500 (97.8 vs 98.2).

## 核心贡献

- **定义并量化"路由器漂移"（router drift）**：指出LLM强化学习的off-policy失配有三大来源——训练/推理引擎不一致（BF16 vs FP8、不同kernel与并行策略）、小批量顺序更新、异步训练的策略滞后；在MoE模型中，这些微小差异会改变top-k路由决策，使同一token在rollout和训练时走不同的计算路径。
- **理论证明路由携带token概率无法恢复的梯度信息**：未选中的专家参数梯度恒为零（式8），因此路由决定"哪些专家能收到学习信号"；进一步构造性证明（3.4节）两个不同专家集合可产生完全相同的输出分布和似然比，故TIS等概率空间校正从原理上检测不到路由变化；附录B的命题1–2证明R3式路由重放相对原生路由目标可引入非零梯度偏置。
- **提出RDC：序列级选择性过滤而非路由重放**：对每个响应计算rollout与训练两阶段逐层专家使用分布的TV距离，平均得到[0,1]区间的响应级漂移分数D_route(y)；按响应长度分桶排序，过滤漂移最高的q=0.08比例的响应；保留的响应使用当前训练策略的原生路由，避免重放的梯度偏置，同时与TIS在token级概率失配上互补。
- **同步+全异步双重验证且增益随陈旧度增大**：在(2048,128)小更新批次下RDC对TIS的领先最大（ZebraLogic +7.9），全异步训练下AIME平均峰值+5.5（对TIS）；未校正的GRPO在异步下约100步后奖励转负、响应长度从约6000坍缩到约2000 token，而RDC全程保持低且稳定的训练-推理KL与梯度范数。

## Key Contributions

- **Identifies router drift as a distinct off-policy failure mode in MoE RL.** The paper enumerates three practical sources of rollout–training mismatch (engine-level numerical/kernel/parallelism differences, sequential mini-batch updates, asynchronous policy lag) and shows that in MoE models these perturb discrete top-K routing decisions, so the same token trains through a different expert set than it was generated through — changing both the computation path and expert-level gradient attribution.
- **Proves routing carries gradient information that token probabilities cannot see.** Section 3.3 shows an unselected expert receives zero direct gradient; Section 3.4 constructs two routing assignments (K−1 shared experts + two distinct replacements with equal outputs) that yield *identical* logits and likelihood ratios yet direct gradients to different expert parameter blocks — so importance ratios and token-level KL are provably blind to routing changes. Appendix B (Props. 1–2) formalizes that Rollout Routing Replay (R3) zeroes local gradients for experts chosen only by the current training router, introducing a nonzero gradient bias b_replay(θ) = ∇J_R3 − ∇J_cur relative to the native-routing objective.
- **RDC: response-level selective filtering instead of route replay.** For each response, RDC builds per-layer expert-usage distributions u_l(e|y) (fraction of the T·K selections going to expert e), measures rollout-vs-training difference via total variation distance, averages over layers into a drift score D_route(y) ∈ [0,1], then filters the top-q (default q = 0.08, ranked within response-length buckets at 2048/4096/8192/12288 boundaries) before each mini-batch update. Retained responses keep their native training routes, so the method avoids replay's gradient bias while shedding the most stale samples; it composes with TIS for token-level correction. Scores are recomputed with the current policy before every update.
- **Empirically, gains grow with staleness, in both synchronous and asynchronous regimes.** Trained on Qwen3-30B-A3B (thinking mode off) with GRPO + DAPO Clip-Higher on DAPO-Math-17K, evaluated on AIME24/AIME25/MATH-500/ZebraLogic every 5 steps; RDC's margin over TIS widens from mixed/negative at (2048,2048) to +7.9 ZebraLogic at (2048,128), and under fully asynchronous training RDC+TIS is the only method that both avoids collapse and keeps improving late in training.

## Methodology

RDC operates on top of a standard decoupled RL loop (vLLM FP8 rollout engine, Megatron BF16 trainer) running GRPO with the DAPO Clip-Higher objective (ε_low = 0.2, ε_high = 0.27).

**Drift score.** For a response y with T tokens and an MoE with L layers, E experts, top-K routing, the expert-usage distribution at layer l is u_l^a(e|y) = (1/TK) Σ_t 𝟙[e ∈ R_l^a(t)] for engine a ∈ {µ (rollout), π (training)}. The layer distance is the total variation distance d_l(y) = ½‖u_l^µ − u_l^π‖₁, and the response-level drift score is the layer average D_route(y) = (1/L) Σ_l d_l(y). Training routes are obtained by teacher-forcing the sampled response through the current policy — notably, in the asynchronous implementation RDC reuses the BF16 routing assignments already recorded during old-log-prob computation, so the extra cost is just the distance computation.

**Filtering.** Responses are ranked by D_route within response-length buckets (rather than across very different lengths), and the top ⌈q·|bucket|⌉ are masked out of the policy update (Eq. 6); q = 0.08 by default. The mask is recomputed for every optimizer mini-batch. This is the conceptual pivot away from prior art: probability-space methods (TIS, CISPO, GSPO, GMPO, CTPO, SIS, TRM) reweight data but cannot see routing; routing methods (R2/R3 replay, PR2 predictive replay, RSPO router-aware importance weights) alter the computation path and — per the paper's analysis — bias gradients. RDC instead decides *which responses participate at all* and leaves the computation path native.

**Analysis supporting the design.** (i) Routing determines gradient support: e ∉ R_l(t) ⟹ ∂h_out/∂θ_{l,e} = 0, so rollout-active experts can silently lose their learning signal. (ii) Token probabilities do not identify routing: two expert sets sharing K−1 experts and swapping the last can produce identical outputs (hence identical likelihood ratios) while granting gradients to different experts. (iii) Replay bias: Propositions 1–2 show R3's substituted routes zero the local gradients of natively-selected-but-not-replayed experts and can shift the overall gradient away from the native-routing objective.

## Results & Highlights

All numbers are best validation accuracy on Qwen3-30B-A3B trained on DAPO-Math-17K; "RDC" in the tables means RDC+TIS.

- **Synchronous, high staleness (2048-rollout / 128-update batch), Table 1:** RDC reaches 86.1 on ZebraLogic vs 78.2 for GRPO+TIS (+7.9 pts), 77.0 on AIME24 vs 70.6 (+6.4 pts), 67.2 on AIME25 vs 62.9 (+4.3 pts), and 97.8 on MATH-500 vs 97.6 (+0.2); the same table's vanilla GRPO manages only 61.1/53.4/40.0/92.2 and R3 actually *hurts* ZebraLogic relative to GRPO there (53.1 vs 61.1) — consistent with the replay-bias analysis.
- **Synchronous, medium staleness (2048/512):** RDC is best on all four benchmarks — 68.1/71.5/62.5/97.6 (ZebraLogic/AIME24/AIME25/MATH-500) vs TIS 61.3/70.6/60.3/97.0, R3 55.2/69.9/57.9/96.6, GRPO 46.3/62.2/50.8/96.0.
- **Synchronous, low staleness (2048/2048) — the honest negative:** RDC *loses* to plain TIS on AIME24 (78.5 vs 80.0) and MATH-500 (97.8 vs 98.2), winning only ZebraLogic (73.9 vs 66.1) and AIME25 (72.4 vs 71.3), i.e., the method pays off only as data grows stale.
- **Fully asynchronous training (Table 2):** RDC achieves the best AIME24 71.2 (vs TIS 65.0, R3 68.7, GRPO 63.4), best AIME25 59.6 (vs TIS 54.8, R3 53.1), and best MATH-500 97.2; on ZebraLogic R3 edges it out (53.9 vs 53.0). Headline: average peak across AIME24+25 is +5.5 points over TIS and +4.5 over R3.
- **Training stability (Figure 5, async):** uncorrected GRPO collapses after ~100 steps — training reward rises early then turns negative, response length collapses from ~6,000 to ~2,000 tokens, and training–inference KL plus gradient norm blow past the plotted range. TIS and RDC keep KL and grad norm low and stable throughout; R3 avoids collapse but shows late-stage validation degradation and more pronounced late KL/grad-norm growth, while RDC maintains strong late-stage accuracy on both AIME benchmarks (Figure 4).
- **Setup scale:** rollout batch of 2,048 responses from 256 prompts × n=8; async trainer consumes 32 prompt groups (256 trajectories) per local update with parameters synced to rollout workers every 4 updates (staleness threshold 0.5); max response length 20,480 training / 30,720 evaluation; TIS truncation C = 2.0; AIME evaluated with 32 samples per problem.

## Critical Assessment

- **Novelty: moderate but genuine.** The core insight — expert selection determines gradient support and is not identifiable from token probabilities — is a simple, clean argument (the K−1-shared-experts construction in Section 3.4 is almost trivially true, which is partly why it is convincing), and reframing routing mismatch as a *data-filtering* signal rather than something to replay or reweight is a real alternative to R3/PR2/RSPO. The formal Props. 1–2 are correct-looking but modest: they establish that replay *can* bias gradients, not that this bias is what empirically hurts.
- **Rigor: single-model, single-dataset, single-seed.** Every result is Qwen3-30B-A3B trained on DAPO-Math-17K and evaluated on four math/logic benchmarks. No second MoE architecture (different expert counts or K), no dense-model control, no non-reasoning domain, and — more seriously — no seed variance at all. The metric is "best validation accuracy reached during training" (peak-of-trajectory), which inflates noisy runs; the GRPO baseline's own non-monotonicity (ZebraLogic 46.3 at 2048/512 but 61.1 at 2048/128) suggests run-to-run noise of several points, i.e., the same order as some reported gaps.
- **Missing ablations.** No sensitivity study for the filtering ratio q (everything uses q = 0.08); no comparison of the TV-distance-over-usage-distribution score against simpler alternatives (per-token expert-set overlap / Jaccard, or probability-space staleness metrics); no RDC-without-TIS arm anywhere — "RDC" always means RDC+TIS, so the standalone contribution of the filter is never isolated.
- **Reproducibility: medium.** Hyperparameters are unusually well documented (Table 3: LR 3e-6/1e-6 sync/async, bucket boundaries, TP/PP/EP/ETP 4/1/4/2, TIS C=2.0, staleness 0.5, sync every 4 updates), and the drift score is implementable from the paper. But no code release is mentioned, the async scheduling details live partly in an appendix, and reproducing the GRPO-collapse baseline presumably depends on exact engine mismatch (FP8 vs BF16) conditions.
- **Significance: practical for async MoE RL pipelines.** If you run truly on-policy single-mini-batch updates, the paper's own table says RDC buys you nothing (it loses to TIS on 2 of 4 benchmarks at 2048/2048). The value proposition is specifically for mini-batch staleness and asynchronous training — a growing but still narrow regime. Filtering 8% of rollouts also silently discards compute; no sample-efficiency or wall-clock cost analysis is given for that trade.
- **Red flags.** Peak-metric reporting without seeds; headline "5.5 points over TIS" is an average over just two benchmarks (AIME24/25) in one async setting; ZebraLogic async gain is absent (53.0, below R3's 53.9); and the theory-to-evidence link is correlational — no experiment directly measures replay's gradient bias (e.g., ‖b_replay‖) or shows that filtered high-drift responses are the ones carrying biased gradients.

## Strengths

- Crisp problem decomposition: three named sources of off-policy mismatch, each mapped to whether system-level, probability-level, or routing-level corrections apply.
- The non-identifiability argument (Section 3.4) is the paper's best idea — it gives a principled reason probability-space corrections cannot substitute for routing awareness, beyond mere empiricalcompetition.
- The method is deliberately simple (a TV-distance score plus top-q filtering, no architecture changes, negligible overhead since routing assignments are already recorded) and composes orthogonally with TIS.
- Gains appear exactly where the theory predicts they should (staleness ↑ → RDC margin ↑), and the paper openly reports the low-staleness regime where RDC loses to TIS.
- Training-dynamics analysis (reward, gradient norm, KL, response length) goes beyond leaderboard numbers and connects RDC to the well-known async-RL collapse phenomenon.

## Limitations

- Evaluated on one MoE model (Qwen3-30B-A3B), one training set (DAPO-Math-17K), and math/logic benchmarks only; generalization to other MoE topologies, dense models (where RDC is moot), or code/general RL is untested.
- No variance over seeds; all conclusions rest on single runs scored by peak validation accuracy, which is noise-favoring.
- The filtering ratio q = 0.08 and the length-bucket design are unmotivated by any ablation; the choice of TV distance over expert-usage distributions (rather than exact per-token routing overlap) blurs *where* in a response routing diverged.
- No RDC-alone results, so the marginal effect of filtering versus simply adding TIS is never cleanly separated.
- The formal analysis shows replay *can* bias gradients but the paper offers no direct measurement of that bias or of the correlation between D_route(y) and per-response gradient error.
- Discarding 8% of generated responses has a compute cost that is never quantified, and no wall-clock/throughput comparison against R3 (which requires replay bookkeeping) is provided.

## Relevance

这篇论文直接命中LLM强化学习训练稳定性与MoE系统方向：它把"训练-推理引擎不一致 + 异步RL"这一真实工程条件下的off-policy退化问题转化为可测量的路由器漂移信号，并给出几乎零开销的过滤式修正，对任何用vLLM+Megatron类分离架构做GRPO/RLHF后训练的MoE流水线都有直接参考价值。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- How does RDC's benefit scale with expert granularity and routing sparsity (e.g., K, E, fine-grained experts in DeepSeek-style or Llama-4-style MoEs), and does it persist with shared experts or auxiliary router losses enabled (both excluded from the Appendix B analysis)?
- Would a token-level or segment-level drift mask outperform the response-level mask — i.e., is the whole response stale, or only the high-drift span?
- What is the empirical magnitude of the replay gradient bias b_replay(θ) during actual training, and does it correlate with D_route(y) across responses as the theory implicitly assumes?
- Can q be set adaptively (e.g., target a fixed drift mass or KL budget) instead of a fixed 8%, and how sensitive are the AIME gains to q ∈ [0.02, 0.3]?
- Does RDC help under *engine-consistent* bitwise-on-policy training with staleness only from mini-batching, isolating routing drift from numerical mismatch?
- Do the gains transfer beyond math/logic reasoning (code RL, general instruction RL), and do they survive multi-seed evaluation with mean-final rather than peak-best metrics?
