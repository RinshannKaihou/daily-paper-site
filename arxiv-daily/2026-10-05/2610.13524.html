Key finding · arXiv 2610.13524

The humblest possible model — a convex quadratic with heavy-tailed inputs — reproduces the loss spikes that plague LLM pre‑training.

Simulating Training Spikes of Deep Neural Networks by Layer-wise Random Feature Models

Ran Tian · Courtney Paquette — Google DeepMind · 2026-10-05

Key metric

5 s
CPU · one layer · 1,500 steps
vs 40 h TPUv4×32 · real 25.5B-token run

A per-layer spike simulation is roughly five orders of magnitude cheaper than training the 1.3B model it mimics (Table 1).

Why this matters

Turns LLM training spikes from folklore into a provable, simulatable mechanism — screening configs for spike risk costs one validation pass, not 40 TPU-hours.

把「训练尖峰」从经验现象变成可证明（α≤0.5 阈值）、可仿真（每层 5 秒 CPU）的机制问题，并给出只需一次验证集前向的配置筛查工具。

Critical edge

The simulation predicts spike propensity, not timing — and the paper's own figures show 1st-order dynamics fall ~20× faster than any layer-wise quadratic model predicts.

Methodology

  1. 01

    PLRF toy model. A convex quadratic random-feature loss ℓ = (c/2)(wᴺAx − b[x])² with inputs scaled i−α; its SGD dynamics are analytically solvable and reproduce neural scaling laws.

  2. 02

    Spike mechanism. Gradient noise accumulates as a random walk; concentration of measure cancels it iff Σλi → ∞, i.e. exactly α ≤ 0.5. Heavy tails let the walk align with the top-eigenvalue subspace — a spike.

  3. 03

    Layer alignment. A 2nd-order Taylor expansion maps each "independent linear unit" layer to random-feature form, factorizing the per-sample gradient norm: E‖∇W‖² ≈ ΣλiE‖∇y‖².

  4. 04

    Simulation (Alg. 3.1). One validation pass yields Hessian trace, activation spectra and gradient stats; Gaussian pseudo-gradients matching measured mean + 2nd moment feed the real optimizer on a quadratic slice.

  5. 05

    Case study. 1.3B / 24-layer Chinchilla-optimal Transformer on C4 (25.5B tokens); 6 FF input layers simulated from the step-21k checkpoint to step 22.5k, separating spiky (β₂=0.999) from normal (β₂=0.95) runs.

Table 3, 1.3B Transformer on C4. β₂ = 0.982 vs 0.992 differ by 0.01 in (1−β₂) yet 0.0097 in loss — and by spiky-vs-smooth.

  • Checkpoint screening works: simulating 6 layers from step 21k flags both Spiky runs while Normal stays smooth — matching real trajectories in propensity, though not in exact spike position.
  • Heavy-tail condition: in the PLRF grid (D=1024, V=20000), α=0.3 never spikes under SGD or Adam at any learning rate; α=0.9 spikes near the divergence threshold.
  • Theory verified on the real LM: Prediction 3.5 holds with relative error < 0.1, mostly within 5%, across layers and steps spanning orders of magnitude.
  • Gradient noise cannot be ignored: despite ~524k tokens per batch, the measured equivalent batch factor B ≈ 1 — the LM trains in a small-batch stochastic regime.
  • Spiky configs are measurably heavier-tailed: fitted spectrum exponent α = 1.1613 (Spiky) vs 0.4570 (Normal) at block 0, step 45k.
  • Calibration caveat: spike-size Q90 is underestimated at B=1 and ~10× overestimated at B=64.

Strengths

  • A convex model spiking cleanly isolates the heavy-tail mechanism from non-convex Edge-of-Stability accounts, with a provable α ≤ 0.5 threshold (App. A.6).
  • The load-bearing prediction is verified to ~5% error on a real 1.3B LM, not just on the toy model.
  • The 5-CPU-second/layer cost makes a practical screening tool; all inputs are measurable in one validation pass with standard autodiff.
  • Rare honesty: the paper catalogs its own explanatory failures in the main text, with released code (Colab, ~2 min) and transparent compute reporting.
  • Reframes the practitioner debate: the whole Hessian eigenvalue distribution matters, not sharpness λmax alone.

Limitations

  • Predicts spike propensity, not timing or location — simulated positions differed from reality and across seeds.
  • The β₂ recipe is validated on one scale (1.3B), one dataset (C4), one seed per arm, with hand-fitted constants from the very run being explained.
  • Heavy assumptions (Gaussian latent inputs, isotropic ∇y 2nd moment) are not directly testable — only their corollary is.
  • The quadratic model under-explains: the 1st-order term falls ~20× faster than predicted; the simulated 2nd-order term "jumps" rather than spikes.
  • Layer definition excludes tied-embedding LMs; the "somehow magical" B ≈ 1 cancellation may be an artifact of the tuned recipe.

Questions for further exploration

  1. Could explicitly modeling the drift of latent inputs across steps recover spike timing prediction?
  2. Does the α ≤ 0.5 threshold become a training-time dashboard — e.g. monitoring fitted activation-spectrum exponents?
  3. Does the β₂ schedule hold across scales, datasets and seeds, and against gradient clipping or QK-norm re-tuning?
  4. What mechanism explains the ~20× faster 1st-order decay — cross-layer correlations, higher-order Taylor terms, or both?
  5. Is the B ≈ 1 cancellation a consequence of batch-size tuning itself, and is "tune until B ≈ 1" a valid batch-size rule?
