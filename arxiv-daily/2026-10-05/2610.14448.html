Async vs synchronous collection · Qwen3-4B trace, iteration 300

  • Setup. Full-parameter GRPO on DAPO-Math-17k (EN); Qwen3-4B/8B-Base, 4×H200, two TP2 async rollout replicas + colocated FSDP learner; all checkpoints evaluated with a common BF16 evaluator.
  • Why KV. Concurrency amortizes weight reads but attention scales with retained context → KV-bound regime. Proposition 4.1: continuous refill keeps C_KV ≥ 1 — concentration is structural, not incidental.
  • Calibration-free INT4 KV. Online per-vector quant params; head-wise affine INT4 groups (4.25 bits/elem incl. metadata); fixed Hadamard rotation on K/Q after RoPE spreads outliers while preserving QKᵢ.
  • Bounded INT8 protection. First 16 (attention-sink) + latest 128 positions mirrored in a fixed-size INT8 side cache — overhead independent of context length; cuts shared-prefix conditional KL by 7.0×.
  • Execution. Disjoint integer and BF16 paths merged via exact softmax — no full-cache reconstruction; window eviction needs no requantization since the INT4 copy already exists.

Weight quantization does not pay in the KV-bound regime; the selective-protection default (16+128) retains ~94–98% of KV4 throughput. Speedups concentrate late in training as responses lengthen (~2k tokens at 4B, ~5.5k at 8B); the BF16 learner still owns 60.5% of late full-iteration time at 8B.

§3

Strengths

  • KV concentration is measured, not assumed — formalized (Prop 4.1) and validated on real training traces; the case for quantizing KV over weights is empirical.
  • Genuinely end-to-end: 400–600 full-parameter GRPO iterations per configuration under a common BF16 evaluator, not just inference microbenchmarks.
  • Engineering-clean design: fixed s+w overhead, exact softmax merge between disjoint regions, no requantization on window eviction.
  • Methodological discipline: three nested timing scopes, matched-workload replay, response-cluster bootstrap CIs, three KL-type quantities kept distinct.
  • Honest scope: the limitations section (dense models, math only, colocated GRPO) matches the evidence.
§4

Limitations

  • One training seed per configuration — the ≤0.5 pp equivalence is “no observed gap”, not a statistically certified bound.
  • Kernel confound: BF16 baseline runs FlashInfer while KV4-RL runs the authors’ in-house integer engine; no BF16 path on the same engine isolates precision alone.
  • Length-regime dependent: 4B full-iteration gain is only 1.17×; at late 8B the async engine nears pool capacity (91.7 of 93.9 GB) and short-response workloads would see much less.
  • Narrow scope: two dense Qwen3 models, one math dataset; no MoE, hybrid attention, or agentic multi-turn settings.
  • Task-level regression survives the average: 4B AIME25 drops 16.5 → 13.6; no comparison vs KVSink/SKVQ/PM-KVQ and no code release.
§5

Questions for Further Exploration

  1. Does the KV-concentration argument and the 1.8× speedup survive on MoE or hybrid-attention models, where weight vs KV traffic balance differs?
  2. How does fixed 144-position protection behave in agentic multi-turn rollouts with contexts far beyond 16k tokens?
  3. Is the ≤0.5 pp equivalence robust to training-seed variance, and does the 4B AIME25 regression (16.5→13.6) persist across seeds?
  4. Could the protection budget be adaptive — per-head, entropy-based, or KL-estimated online — to trade a few more INT8 positions for lower mismatch?
  5. What fraction of the measured speedup comes from the integer kernel itself rather than INT4 precision, given the FlashInfer-vs-in-house confound?
