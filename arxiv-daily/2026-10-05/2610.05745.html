Beyond In-Distribution Preservation: Recovering Generalization in Quantized VLAs via Vulnerability-Oriented Tuning

Shen Ruan, Wenchang Gao, Jin Wang, Siao Liu, et al.·Soochow Univ. / SJTU / Yootta / RMIT·Oct 5, 2026

VLA Post-Training Quantization On-Policy Distillation Robot Robustness

一句话：量化 VLA 在原始 LIBERO 上几乎不掉点（保留 ~97%），但在 LIBERO-Plus 七类扰动下平均掉 6.0–15.4 分；PIVOT-Q 每条轨迹只蒸馏 16 个高脆弱状态，即把成功率恢复到接近全精度。

Key metric — distillation budget
7.4%

of full-state distillation’s budget — 16 states per trajectory vs ~199–243 — yet PIVOT-Q beats FSD 81.3% vs 76.9% on GR00T-N1.5/QuantVLA (LIBERO-Plus).

Why this matters

In-distribution scores hide quantization damage. For anyone deploying quantized robot policies, repairing 16 targeted states is cheaper — and better — than distilling everything.

Critical edge

The 81.3% headline rests on the more favorable of two unexplained protocols (Table 2 says 78.3%); FSD margins elsewhere are +0.1–0.4 pts — within one standard deviation.

▸ Methodology — the PIVOT-Q loop

  1. On-policy rollouts, matched noise

    The quantized student drives the environment; teacher vs student actions are compared at every visited state under identical observation, instruction, and diffusion noise.

  2. Vulnerability score

    Per state: current discrepancy qt blended 50/50 with a discounted future discrepancy (H=4, γ=0.9), percentile-ranked within 4 trajectory phases.

  3. Phase-balanced top-16

    Greedy pick of 4 states per phase with a minimum temporal gap → a fixed 16-state supervision set per trajectory.

  4. Weighted distill + behavioral anchor

    Weights clipped to [0.5, 2.0]; weighted MSE to teacher actions, plus a replay-bank anchor loss (≤256 clean states, λ=0.1) against policy drift.

Why sparse works: discrepancies fit log-normal (KS 0.016–0.037); the top 5% of states carry 72–79% of total deviation. Training: LoRA r=16, 5 updates per rollout.

▸ Strengths

  • Decision-relevant finding: in-distribution retention is a misleading signal of deployment quality — quantified across 4 model-quantizer pairs × 7 shift axes.
  • Causal, not correlational: the intervention experiment dissociates high- vs low-discrepancy states crisply (+7.4 vs −0.2).
  • Honest attribution: the equal-budget ablation isolates state selection — not sparsity — as the source of improvement.
  • Evaluation hygiene: fixed 560-episode protocol, three seeds with std, full hyperparameters disclosed, code promised.
  • Practical envelope: 16 distilled states per trajectory is a realistic budget for post-deployment repair.

Limitations

  • Simulation-only: no real-robot, cross-embodiment, or wall-clock/latency evidence; claims confined to LIBERO-Plus’s seven predefined shifts.
  • Protocol ambiguity: Tables 2 and 3 differ by ~3 pts for the identical flagship setting, unexplained.
  • Asymmetric comparison: FSD gets ~199–243 states vs 16, and the one category FSD wins (camera viewpoint) is not dissected.
  • Teacher-bound: needs a frozen full-precision teacher at selection and training time; “7.4% budget” counts states, not teacher inference cost.
  • No sensitivity sweeps for H, γ, K, M, λ; the log-normal fit is descriptive — no predictive model of vulnerability before rollout.

▸ Questions for further exploration

  1. Does PIVOT-Q transfer to real robots or other suites (SimplerEnv, RoboCasa) — and does the log-normal tail persist outside LIBERO?
  2. Can the vulnerability score go teacher-free — from Hessian/activation statistics or the student’s own ensembles?
  3. Is it complementary to QAT (e.g., QVLA), or does quantization-aware training already suppress the vulnerable tail?
  4. Which protocol ships with the released code — and what explains the ~3-pt Table 2 vs Table 3 difference?
  5. Is tail concentration (top 5% of states → 72–79% of discrepancy) a usable a-priori diagnostic of recoverability? π0.5 (43.8%) and OpenVLA (34.2%) show weaker concentration and smaller gains.
