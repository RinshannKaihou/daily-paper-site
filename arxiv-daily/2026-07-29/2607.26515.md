> **一句话总结：** 该论文提出 Rollout-ResQ——一种仅对 FP4 rollout 激活值加一个 2:4 稀疏残差修正项的方法，配合三级层次化缩放的 HiFloat4 格式，在 Qwen2.5-3B/GSM8K 上把全 4 比特端到端 RL 训练与 BF16 的精度差距从 4.9% 压缩到 1.1%（MXFP4 上从 13.6% 压缩到 5.3%）。

> **TL;DR:** The paper presents the first end-to-end FP4 RL post-training (rollout + training, forward and backward, all at 4-bit) and shows the dominant error source is rollout-side activation underflow, not training quantization. Their fix, Rollout-ResQ with 2:4-sparse residual correction, paired with the HiFloat4 (HiF4) format, closes the GSM8K accuracy gap to BF16 from 4.93% (naive HiF4) to 1.06% on Qwen2.5-3B, versus 2.73% for the strongest baseline (OCC) and 8.42% for SmoothQuant; under MXFP4 the gap narrows from 13.65% to 5.31%.

## 核心贡献

- 首次实现"端到端"FP4 强化学习后训练：rollout 策略和训练策略（包括前向和反向传播）全部运行在 4-bit 精度，不依赖 LoRA 适配器或更高精度的梯度路径。
- 通过系统性诊断定位精度损失的真正来源：不是训练侧量化误差，而是 rollout 激活值量化——异常值（outlier）拉大动态范围，导致大量激活值在 FP4 下下溢为零（"zero collapse"，部分层零值比例超过 80%）；反直觉的是，仅将训练策略恢复为高精度反而比全 FP4 训练更差，说明 rollout-训练精度失配才是主要故障模式。
- 提出 Rollout-ResQ：仅对 FP4 rollout 矩阵乘法追加一个受硬件友好稀疏模式（如 2:4 半结构化稀疏）约束的残差修正项，用极小的额外计算开销恢复被异常值下溢丢失的大部分精度，同时保持真正的 FP4 GEMM 吞吐率。
- 在 Qwen2.5-3B 和 Qwen2.5-Math-7B 上验证方法有效性和格式无关性：搭配 HiFloat4（三级层次化缩放格式）时效果最佳，将 BF16 差距从 4.9% 收窄到 1.1%；搭配开放标准 MXFP4 时也能将差距从 13.6% 收窄到 5.3%，同时证明 FP4 格式本身的选择决定了可恢复精度的上限（HiF4 显著优于 MXFP4）。

## Key Contributions

- First end-to-end FP4 RL post-training in which both rollout and training policies—including forward and backward passes—operate entirely at 4-bit precision, without LoRA adapters or a higher-precision gradient path.
- A systematic diagnosis pinpointing rollout-side *activation* quantization (not training-side quantization) as the dominant source of degradation. Outliers stretch the block dynamic range so far that a large fraction of activation values underflow to zero under FP4 ("zero collapse," exceeding 80% sparsity in some layers). Counterintuitively, restoring only the training policy to higher precision while keeping rollout at FP4 performs *worse* than fully FP4 training, exposing rollout–training mismatch (not training-side noise) as the principal failure mode.
- Rollout Residual Quantization (Rollout-ResQ): a single residual correction term, quantized to FP4 and constrained to a hardware-friendly sparsity pattern (channel-wise, 2:4 semi-structured, or block sparsity), added only to the FP4 rollout matmul. This recovers most of the outlier-driven underflow loss while adding minimal compute overhead (theoretical ~2.67x speedup over FP16 for the 2:4 variant, vs. 2x for the dense residual).
- Demonstrates the recipe is format-agnostic but format-sensitive in ceiling: HiFloat4 (HiF4), with its three-level hierarchical E6M2/E1/S1P2 scaling, is empirically the more robust format for end-to-end FP4 RL than the single-scale, open-standard MXFP4.

## Methodology

The paper builds on GRPO-based RL post-training implemented in VeRL, using a hybrid-engine architecture (vLLM for rollout, FSDP for training). It studies W4A4 quantization (both weights and activations to FP4) applied to all linear layers of every transformer block, comparing two 4-bit formats: HiFloat4 (HiF4, three-level hierarchical scaling over 64-element blocks: E6M2 top-level scale, E1 second/third-level scales, S1P2 mantissa) and MXFP4 (single E8M0 shared block scale over 32-element blocks, E2M1 elements).

Diagnosis (Section 4.2): the authors run a controlled ablation matrix on Qwen2.5-3B/GSM8K crossing {BF16, FP4} training precision with {BF16, W4A16, W4A8, W16A4, FP4} rollout precision. This isolates that keeping training in FP4 while rollout stays BF16 nearly matches the full-BF16 baseline, whereas keeping training in BF16 with FP4 rollout collapses accuracy far below even the fully-FP4 setting — establishing rollout activation quantization, not training, as the bottleneck. They further inspect activation distributions (percentage of zero values across layers, percentage of blocks containing outliers) to show that outlier-driven block-scale inflation drives non-outlier values to zero.

Method (Section 4.3): for each linear layer with activation X and weight W, Rollout-ResQ computes the residual ΔX = X − Q(X) capturing what standard FP4 quantization discards, then re-quantizes this residual to FP4 and applies a sparsity function S (channel-wise top-α%, semi-structured M:N such as 2:4, or block sparsity) before adding it back: Ŷ = Q(X)Q(W)ᵀ + S(Q(ΔX))Q(W)ᵀ. This differs from prior block-level dynamic fallback methods (e.g., Zhang et al.'s dynamic block-level fallback, ref [64]) by operating at the tensor level and replacing a dense fallback with a hardware-supported sparse correction — motivated by an empirical finding (Figure 4, MSE/RMSE heatmaps) that the residual Q(ΔX) is much more robust to 2:4 sparsification than the primary activation Q(X) itself. They compare against PTQ-style outlier-mitigation baselines adapted to the RL setting: SmoothQuant (static per-iteration calibrated smoothing), RHT (rotation-based Hadamard transform), and OCC (outlier clamping-and-compensation, dynamic block-level fallback).

Experiments train Qwen2.5-3B via GRPO on GSM8K (evaluated on GSM8K-test and Math500) and Qwen2.5-Math-7B on DAPO-Math-17K (evaluated on AIME2024, AIME2025, AMC2023, Math500, using Mean@32). Compute: Qwen2.5-3B runs used ~100GB peak memory and ~10 hours; Qwen2.5-Math-7B used ~370GB and ~20 hours, all on an internal cluster, with FP4 simulated (no native FP4 hardware available).

## Results & Highlights

- **GSM8K-test, Qwen2.5-3B (Table 2):** BF16 baseline = 86.96%. Naive HiF4 (no recovery) drops to 82.03% (a 4.93-point gap). The strongest prior baseline, OCC, reduces the gap to 84.23% (−2.73 points). Rollout-ResQ-S2:4 achieves 85.90% (−1.06 points), the best result — nearly closing the gap to BF16 and clearly beating OCC and SmoothQuant (78.54%, −8.42 points).
- **Math500, Qwen2.5-3B under MXFP4 (Table 2):** BF16 = 61.2%. Naive MXFP4 collapses to 27.8% (−33.4 points). OCC only reaches 42.8% (−18.4 points). Rollout-ResQ-S2:4 lifts this to 46.4% (−14.8 points), while Rollout-ResQ-Dense reaches 48.2% (−13.0 points), both far ahead of OCC/SmoothQuant/RHT.
- **DAPO-Math-17K, Qwen2.5-Math-7B under HiF4 (Table 3, Mean@32):** On AIME2024, BF16 = 27.29%, naive HiF4 = 16.15% (−11.14), RHT = 13.43% (−13.86), OCC = 6.77% (−20.52), while Rollout-ResQ-S2:4 = 20.73% (−6.56) — the best recovery among all methods, roughly halving the naive-HiF4 gap. On Math500, BF16 = 74.59%, naive HiF4 = 59.40% (−15.19), and Rollout-ResQ-Dense achieves the best score at 69.02% (−5.57), with Rollout-ResQ-S2:4 close behind at 68.95% (−5.64) — versus OCC's 43.59% (−31.00), a substantially weaker baseline that actually underperforms naive HiF4 on this benchmark.
- **Format comparison headline claim:** with Rollout-ResQ, HiF4 closes the BF16 accuracy gap from 4.9% (naive) to 1.1% on Qwen2.5-3B/GSM8K, while the same recipe applied to open-standard MXFP4 only narrows the gap from 13.6% to 5.3% — establishing that FP4 format choice sets a hard ceiling on recoverable accuracy independent of the correction method.
- **Overhead analysis (Section 5.4):** theoretical FLOP accounting shows Rollout-ResQ-Dense achieves a 2x speedup over FP16/BF16 GEMM (assuming 4x FP4 throughput advantage), and the 2:4-sparse variant achieves up to ~2.67x speedup, at "minimal overhead" relative to plain FP4. Note this is a theoretical estimate only — the paper explicitly states no real hardware speedup was measured (see Limitations).

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty:** The core empirical finding — that rollout-side activation quantization, not training-side quantization, is the dominant failure mode in FP4 RL, and that restoring only the training policy to higher precision makes things *worse* than full FP4 — is a genuinely interesting and counterintuitive result that meaningfully advances understanding beyond prior 8-bit RL quantization work (JetRL, QeRL) and static PTQ outlier literature (SmoothQuant, QuaRot, OmniQuant). The Rollout-ResQ mechanism itself is an incremental but sensible combination of known ideas (residual/outlier decomposition from ref [64], N:M structured sparsity from prior pruning/sparse-GEMM literature) adapted specifically to the non-stationary rollout distribution problem in RL post-training, where static calibration (as used by SmoothQuant/OmniQuant) is shown empirically to underperform.

**Rigor:** The ablation in Table 1 isolating weight-only vs. activation-only quantization in the rollout is a clean and convincing diagnostic. The comparison sweeps three sparsity variants (channel-wise, 2:4, block) plus a dense variant across two model sizes, two datasets, and two FP4 formats, which is reasonably thorough for a systems paper. However, all baselines (SmoothQuant, RHT, OCC) are adaptations of methods designed for static inference settings repurposed for RL — the paper explicitly acknowledges this mismatch as their intended point, but it does raise the question of whether these baselines were tuned as carefully as Rollout-ResQ for the RL setting (e.g., SmoothQuant's calibration frequency, RHT's rotation granularity). No ablation is given on the choice of α=50% sparsity level or block size 32x64 beyond citing it as "a commonly used pruning level in prior work" — the paper itself flags in Limitations that exploring other sparsity levels remains open.

**Reproducibility:** Full GRPO hyperparameters are reported in Appendix A (learning rate, batch size, group size, clip ranges, truncated importance sampling threshold), and code is claimed to be public. However, all experiments rely on FP4 *simulation* rather than native FP4 hardware execution (the paper states simulation overhead "dominates training time, preventing direct measurement of real speedup and efficiency gains" — this is an explicit, honest limitation but means the headline "2.67x speedup" and "minimal overhead" claims in Section 5.4 are purely theoretical FLOP-counting, not measured wall-clock results.

**Significance:** If the format-choice finding generalizes, it has practical implications for hardware/format co-design decisions in the FP4 RL post-training space, an increasingly important cost bottleneck given long CoT rollout trajectories. That said, the paper only tests two relatively small models (3B and 7B) and two math-reasoning benchmarks/datasets (GSM8K, DAPO-Math-17K), which the Limitations section itself concedes may not generalize to "extremely large models or datasets." The Rollout-ResQ-S2:4 variant is not uniformly the best across every table/metric (e.g., Rollout-ResQ-Dense wins on Math500 for the 7B model and on GSM8K-test under MXFP4), so "best variant" claims require picking the right sub-variant per setting, which is a minor form of post-hoc cherry-picking across the four proposed variants (Dense, S50%, S32x64_50%, S2:4) rather than a single method winning cleanly everywhere.

**Red flags:** None severe, but note the weak baseline dynamic — OCC, the strongest prior baseline on GSM8K, actually performs *worse than naive uncorrected HiF4* on the 7B/DAPO-Math-17K Math500 benchmark (43.59% vs. 59.40% for naive HiF4, per Table 3), which the paper itself flags as a notable instability rather than hiding it — a point in favor of the paper's honesty, but it also means the "OCC is the strongest baseline" framing from the 3B section doesn't hold uniformly at 7B scale.

## Strengths

- Clear, well-designed diagnostic ablation (Table 1) that convincingly isolates the true bottleneck (rollout activation quantization) rather than assuming it a priori.
- Honest, prominent acknowledgment of the counterintuitive negative result (higher-precision training policy making things worse) rather than burying it.
- Broad empirical validation across two model scales (3B, 7B), two FP4 formats (HiF4, MXFP4), and four math reasoning benchmark suites (GSM8K, Math500, AIME24/25, AMC23), with training curves (Figure 5) showing not just final accuracy but convergence stability.
- Explicit, quantified overhead analysis with a concrete sparsity-efficiency trade-off calculation, and multiple sparsity variants offered so practitioners can choose their point on the accuracy/speed curve.
- Transparent Limitations section that flags exactly what wasn't tested (real hardware measurement, larger models/datasets, alternative sparsity levels).

## Limitations

- No native FP4 hardware was used; all results are from simulated quantization, so the claimed compute/throughput advantages (2x–2.67x speedup) are theoretical FLOP estimates, not measured wall-clock speedups — explicitly acknowledged by the authors.
- Only tested on two model sizes (3B, 7B) and math-reasoning tasks/datasets; generalization to larger models, other domains (code, agentic tasks), or non-math RLVR settings is untested.
- Only a single sparsity ratio (50%) is explored across all sparsity pattern types; the accuracy-efficiency trade-off at other sparsity levels is left as future work.
- The best-performing Rollout-ResQ variant differs across benchmarks/settings (Dense vs. S2:4 vs. S50%), meaning practitioners must tune which variant to deploy rather than having one universally dominant recipe.
- Baseline methods (OCC, RHT, SmoothQuant) show inconsistent, sometimes unstable behavior across settings (e.g., OCC underperforming naive HiF4 on 7B Math500), which slightly weakens the strength of "beats all baselines" claims since baseline instability itself is part of the story rather than a uniformly strong comparison point.

## Relevance

该论文与大模型高效训练/推理量化、强化学习后训练效率密切相关，尤其对关注 FP4/低比特 RL 训练成本优化、Ascend/华为硬件生态或格式化量化（HiFloat4/MXFP4）设计的研究者有直接参考价值。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- Would Rollout-ResQ's gains hold on native FP4 hardware (e.g., next-gen NPUs/GPUs with real FP4 tensor cores), or does the theoretical 2x–2.67x speedup evaporate once memory-bound sparse-kernel overheads are accounted for in practice?
- Does the rollout-activation-underflow bottleneck generalize to non-math RLVR domains (code generation, agentic tool use, long-horizon dialogue) where activation/token distributions may differ substantially from math CoT reasoning?
- How does Rollout-ResQ interact with much larger models (e.g., 70B+) where outlier severity and activation dynamic range are known to scale differently than in 3B/7B models?
- Could an adaptive sparsity ratio (rather than a fixed 50%) that responds to per-layer or per-step outlier severity close the remaining 1.1%/5.3% gaps further, and what would the added scheduling overhead cost?
- Since OCC's instability on the 7B/Math500 setting suggests dynamic block-level fallback methods may be less robust at scale, is there a deeper connection between model scale, outlier severity, and the choice of residual-correction granularity (tensor-level vs. block-level) that the paper doesn't fully explore?
