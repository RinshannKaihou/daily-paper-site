> **一句话总结：** 本文提出对比式错误跨度标注（cESA），让标注者在同一屏幕上同时看到 k 个模型的翻译输出并标记 major/minor 错误后打 0–100% 绝对分，在 WMT25 英→日翻译评测（12 个模型/系统）中，相较标准的逐句单独标注 ESA，k=3 时每句标注时间从 77.8 秒降到 53.7 秒（省时约 31%），同时标注稳定性（Kendall's τb）从 0.802 升至 0.813、标注者间分歧（MAE）从 20.0 降到 17.0。

> **TL;DR:** This paper introduces Contrastive Error Span Annotation (cESA), which shows k translations of the same document side by side (instead of one at a time as in standard pointwise ESA) so annotators can mark major/minor error spans and give a 0–100% score with shared context. Evaluated on the WMT25 English→Japanese task (12 MT systems/translations, including Gemini-2.5-Pro, Claude-4, DeepSeek-V3, CommandA-MT), cESA at k=3 cuts average per-segment annotation time from 77.8s (k=1) to 53.7s — up to 31% faster than pointwise ESA — while simultaneously raising ranking stability (Kendall's τb 0.802→0.813) and lowering inter-annotator MAE (20.0→17.0), and a small-scale ablation shows cESA(k=1) already beats standard ESA on inter-annotator MAE (13.4 vs. 15.9).

## 核心贡献

- 提出 cESA（对比式错误跨度标注）协议：在标准 ESA（Kocmi et al., 2024）基础上，把同一文档的 k 个模型译文并排展示在同一屏幕，标注者标记 minor/major 错误跨度后给出 5% 为步长、带明确锚点说明（Very Good/Good/Acceptable/Borderline/Not acceptable）的 0–100% 绝对分，既保留绝对分数（便于非参数化模型排名），又利用"联合评估优于分别评估"的心理测量学原理提升一致性。
- 通过两组人工评测系统回答两个实操问题：(1) 同屏展示多少个译文最优（k=1,2,3,4 对比，结论是 k=3 时间与质量兼优）；(2) 展示多译文是否引入位置偏差、相似度偏差、"陪衬效应"（decoy effect）等系统性偏差（结论：均存在但影响很小，可忽略）。
- 复用 WMT25 英→日通用翻译评测数据（97 个片段、38 篇文档，覆盖新闻/视频/社交截图等多模态输入），对 10 个真实翻译系统（含 Gemini-2.5-Pro、Claude-4、DeepSeek-V3、CommandA-MT、Mistral-Medium、人工翻译等）做大规模标注，验证了协议在生产环境下的可扩展性。
- 将该协议实现并开源到现有标注平台 Pearmut（v1.1.5），提供 18 个语言对的教程和一份最小化命令行示例，使评测机构可以直接部署使用，而非停留在论文层面的协议描述。

## Key Contributions

- **cESA protocol**: extends pointwise Error Span Annotation (ESA; Kocmi et al., 2024) by showing k translations of the same source document on one screen instead of one at a time; annotators mark minor/major error spans per translation and then assign a final 0–100% score in 5% increments, with explicit anchors (85–100% Very Good, 65–80% Good, 45–60% Acceptable, 25–40% Borderline, 0–20% Not acceptable) to keep scores interpretable and comparable across annotators.
- **Empirical answer to "how many outputs to show at once"**: two campaigns — a small-scale ablation (4 models, ESA vs. cESA(k=1)) and a large-scale study (10 models, k ∈ {1,2,3,4}) — establish that k=3 is the practical sweet spot for balancing annotation speed, inter/intra-annotator agreement, and model-ranking stability.
- **Systematic bias analysis unique to contrastive settings**: quantifies position bias (order on screen), "surrounding similarity" bias (whether nearby similar translations inflate error counts), and "surrounding quality"/decoy-effect bias (whether a strong neighboring translation depresses a weaker one's score), each measured via counterfactual within-model comparisons across screens rather than confounded raw correlations.
- **Non-parametric, absolute-score model ranking**: unlike prior contrastive/comparison-based evaluation (Chatbot Arena, K-Sort Arena, WMT ranking tasks), cESA still yields an absolute score per translation, so model rankings can be computed as simple score averages without post-hoc Bayesian/Elo-style ability modeling.
- **Production-ready release**: the protocol is implemented in the open-source Pearmut annotation tool (Zouhar and Kocmi, 2026) with an interactive tutorial and a documented minimal setup (`pip install pearmut`, JSON campaign spec), and code/data are published at github.com/zouharvi/cESA.

## Methodology

cESA modifies the ESA protocol along two axes. First, instead of one source-translation pair per screen, the annotator sees a source document (split across multiple segments) translated k times by different models/translators on the same screen; they mark minor/major error spans in each of the k translations and give each one a final score. Second, the scoring scale is discretized into 5% steps (0%, 5%, ..., 100%) with explicit anchor descriptions (Listing 2) rather than an unconstrained slider, and annotators can navigate back to previously annotated screens to revise judgments — addressing a known limitation of prior contrastive tools that don't support revision after discovering a new error pattern.

The evaluation reuses the WMT25 general translation shared task's English→Japanese subset (97 segments across 38 documents, spanning news, video, and social-screenshot domains; segments range 1–4 paragraphs, 10–120 words). Two human campaigns were run: (1) a small-scale campaign comparing standard pointwise ESA vs. cESA(k=1) on 4 translation models, with one duplicated translation per annotator to measure intra-annotator agreement; (2) a large-scale campaign comparing cESA at k ∈ {1,2,3,4} on 10 translation systems (Gemini-2.5-Pro, Alghgarb, CommandA-MT, Claude-4, DeepSeek-V3, Online-B, Mistral-Medium, Yolu, Laniqo, and human translation), with 2 duplicated translations per k-condition to control for all configurations showing all translations across 12, 6, 4, and 3 screens respectively. The design is within-subject with randomized condition order; annotators are professional English–Japanese translators (the same annotation pool used in WMT). Every annotation is run twice by different annotators to measure inter-annotator agreement (mean absolute error between two annotators) alongside intra-annotator agreement (MAE between the same annotator's repeated judgment) and "stability" (Kendall's τb ranking correlation between a random 10–90% subset of the data and the full-data model ranking, averaged over 100 runs, with 99% CI via BCa bootstrapping on 10k samples).

For the bias analysis, the paper defines counterfactual proxies rather than relying on raw correlations (which are confounded by source difficulty): (1) position bias is checked via the score distribution at each on-screen position (Table 4); (2) "surrounding similarity" bias compares a model's score when its screen-mates have high vs. low chrF-based string similarity to it (using symmetric chrF, chosen deliberately over semantic similarity to preserve sensitivity to paraphrase/error differences); (3) "surrounding quality" (decoy effect) bias compares a model's score when co-displayed with stronger vs. weaker models, using the same similarity-style counterfactual framing but keyed on score rather than similarity.

## Results & Highlights

- **cESA(k=1) beats standard ESA (small-scale ablation, Table 1)**: switching only the interface/guidelines (not the number of shown outputs) reduces inter-annotator MAE from 15.9 (ESA) to 13.4 (cESA k=1) and intra-annotator MAE from 5.9 to 5.5, while raising stability from 0.964 to 0.962 (comparable) — despite annotators being more experienced with ESA than with cESA going in. Annotators also preferred cESA on discretized (3.3 vs. 3.8) and anchored (3.5 vs. 4.2) Likert-5 scales.
- **k=3 is the optimum for speed and quality (large-scale campaign, Table 1)**: average time per segment drops from 77.8s (k=1) to 55.4s (k=2) to 53.7s (k=3) — a roughly 31% reduction from k=1 to k=3 — before rising again to 59.3s at k=4. Stability peaks at k=3 (0.813) versus 0.802 (k=1), 0.786 (k=2), and 0.797 (k=4); inter-annotator MAE is lowest at k=3 (17.0) versus 20.0 (k=1), 19.0 (k=2), and 19.0 (k=4).
- **Model ranking (Table 3, main campaign)**: across all four k-configurations, Gemini-2.5-Pro ranks highest (80.4–85.4 average score) and Laniqo ranks lowest (58.0–68.7); the ranking order (Gemini-2.5-Pro > Alghgarb > Human > CommandA-MT > Claude-4 > DeepSeek-V3 > ONLINE-B > Mistral-Medium > Yolu > Laniqo) is consistent across k=1,2,3,4, supporting the claim that cESA scores can be averaged directly for ranking without parametric correction.
- **Bias effects exist but are small**: position bias produces "very little distributional difference" across screen positions (Table 4); surrounding-similarity bias shifts scores by at most −0.2 points (Table 5); the surrounding-quality/decoy effect is the largest but still small — a stronger co-displayed model depresses another model's score by an average of −0.5 points at its largest (Table 6, cESA k=3, mean-score condition: −1.4).
- **Within-screen calibration**: the same model's score varies less when compared on the same screen than across different screens (Table 7) — e.g., at k=3, within-screen average absolute score difference is 12.9 vs. 17.8 between-screen — indicating that co-displaying translations improves scoring consistency beyond what random per-screen model assignment alone would give.
- Annotators self-reported a preferred maximum number of simultaneously-annotatable models of 2–3 (median responses: 2, 2, 3, 3, 3), corroborating the k=3 recommendation from the objective metrics.

## Authors & Credibility

The author list combines academic MT-evaluation researchers (Vilém Zouhar, ETH Zurich; Marine Carpuat, University of Maryland; Philipp Koehn, JHU, a foundational figure in statistical/neural MT and long-time WMT organizer; Rachel Bawden, Inria; Martin Popel, Charles University) with industry researchers at companies actively building translation systems (Roman Grundkiewicz, Microsoft; Parker Riley, Google; Tom Kocmi, Cohere — lead author of the original ESA protocol paper cited throughout this work; Sara Rajaee, University of Amsterdam). Several authors are recurring WMT shared-task organizers, and the paper explicitly builds on and is positioned as the successor to Kocmi et al. (2024)'s ESA protocol, giving it strong domain credibility. Code, data, and an interactive annotated demo are released at github.com/zouharvi/cESA.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty**: The core idea — showing multiple MT outputs side by side rather than one at a time — is not new in isolation (WMT ranking tasks, EASL, RankME, and Chatbot Arena all show multiple outputs), but combining it with ESA's error-span-plus-absolute-score format, systematically sweeping k, and rigorously testing for the biases this specific combination could introduce, is a genuine and useful protocol-engineering contribution rather than a modeling contribution.

**Rigor**: The experimental design is careful — within-subject, randomized condition order, duplicated items for intra/inter-annotator agreement, bootstrapped 99% confidence intervals for stability, and counterfactual (rather than raw-correlation) tests for similarity/quality bias that explicitly control for the confound of source-sentence difficulty. A concrete limitation the paper itself flags but does not fully resolve: the small-scale ablation (ESA vs. cESA k=1) and the large-scale sweep (k=1..4) used different annotator crowds and different model sets, which the authors acknowledge causes the k=1 numbers to differ between Table 1's two halves (167.3s vs. 77.8s) — this makes the two campaigns not directly comparable and slightly weakens the "cESA(k=1) < ESA" claim as a controlled comparison against the k=3 results.

**Reproducibility**: Strong — code, data, and the annotation tool (Pearmut) are open-sourced, with a minimal reproducible setup given in the paper itself (Listing 1) and 18-language-pair tutorials already built into the tool, suggesting real deployment intent beyond a one-off academic exercise.

**Significance**: If adopted, this could measurably reduce the cost of WMT-scale and industrial human MT evaluation (up to 31% time savings while improving agreement), which is a meaningful practical contribution given how expensive and noisy human evaluation currently is.

**Red flags / weaknesses**: (1) All experiments are on a single language pair (English→Japanese) and a single shared task (WMT25); the paper does not test whether the k=3 optimum, or the small observed biases, generalize to other language pairs, especially those with different reading directions or translation lengths. (2) The "optimal k=3" finding rests on a non-monotonic trend (time: 77.8→55.4→53.7→59.3s across k=1..4) from a single 10-model, 97-segment dataset — the paper does not report statistical significance tests distinguishing k=2 from k=3, only point estimates and CIs in Table 1, so the practical difference between k=2 and k=3 could plausibly be noise. (3) The decoy-effect bias (up to −1.4 points in the mean-score condition, Table 6) is dismissed as "small" without a formal significance test against zero, despite being the largest of the three measured biases and directly relevant to fairness of model comparisons when weak and strong systems are annotated together.

## Strengths

- Directly actionable protocol with open-source tooling (Pearmut) and ready-to-use tutorials, not just a paper-only proposal.
- Rigorous, well-controlled bias analysis using counterfactual comparisons rather than naive correlations, addressing a real methodological gap in prior contrastive-evaluation work.
- Reports both efficiency (time/cost) and quality (agreement, stability) metrics together, rather than trading one off silently against the other.
- Validated at meaningful scale (WMT25 shared task data, professional translator annotators, two independent annotation passes per item).

## Limitations

- Single language pair (English→Japanese) and single shared task (WMT25); generalization to other language pairs/directions and to other domains beyond news/video/social-screenshot is untested.
- Small-scale ablation and large-scale k-sweep use different annotator pools and model sets, limiting direct comparability of their k=1 numbers.
- The paper explicitly acknowledges (Limitations section) that other potential biases, such as effects from the specific order in which model outputs are presented (citing Knowles, 2021), are only partially addressed via document shuffling and revision capability, not eliminated.
- No comparison to MQM (the more detailed, categorized human evaluation gold standard) under the same contrastive setup — the comparison is limited to ESA vs. cESA, both from the same research lineage.

## Relevance

该工作直接关系到人工评测协议设计与标注效率优化，其"多输出同屏对比 + 反事实偏差检验"的方法论思路也可迁移到其他需要多模型并排人工评估的场景（如 LLM 输出质量评测、RLHF 偏好标注）。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- Does the k=3 optimum hold for language pairs with very different reading/scanning patterns (e.g., right-to-left languages, or pairs with much longer/shorter average segment lengths)?
- How does cESA perform when the k co-displayed outputs are deliberately adversarial (e.g., all near-identical or all wildly different in quality), beyond the "surrounding similarity/quality" bias tests already run?
- Would formally testing the decoy-effect bias (−0.5 to −1.4 points) for statistical significance change the paper's conclusion that biases are "negligible"?
- Could cESA be combined with MQM's categorized error taxonomy instead of ESA's simpler major/minor scheme, and would the efficiency gains still hold with the added annotation complexity?
- How sensitive is the k=3 recommendation to annotator expertise level — would non-professional or crowd-sourced annotators (as opposed to professional translators) show a different optimal k?
