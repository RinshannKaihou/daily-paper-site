> **一句话总结：** SkillBoost 把 LLM 智能体的技能自演化重新建模为带探索-利用权衡的约束搜索问题（结构化利用定位失败组件 → 先验引导探索生成 Best-of-N 候选修复 → 验证式接受只提交净收益为正且回退可控的编辑），在 23 个模型-基准组合上全面超越人工技能、LLM 生成技能、Trace2Skill 与 SkillOpt，且把测试-训练泛化差距压到接近零。

> **TL;DR:** SkillBoost reframes skill self-evolution as an MDP with an exploration-exploitation trade-off instead of pure trajectory fitting. On LiveMathematicianBench with Claude-opus-4-6, it reaches 76.0% accuracy vs. 28.6% for no-skill and 49.6% for the strongest baseline (SkillOpt), a +47.4pp gain over baseline and +26.4pp over the best prior optimization method, while keeping the test-train generalization gap near zero (vs. large negative gaps for SkillOpt/Trace2Skill).

## 核心贡献

- 将技能自演化问题重新表述为马尔可夫决策过程（MDP），指出现有方法（梯度式/零阶搜索式技能优化）的根本缺陷是"数据拟合"目标，会在小批量轨迹上过拟合，造成"技能过拟合"现象（在观测批次上涨分，但在留出测试集上出现明显泛化差距）。
- 提出 SkillBoost 三阶段框架：(1) **结构化利用**——通过工作流合规性检查、推理链回溯、根因聚类三个分析器，把观测到的失败精确归因到技能的具体可编辑组件（Metadata/Workflow/Constraints/References），而非整体重写技能；(2) **先验引导探索**——固定诊断结果，让 LLM 用不同修复策略生成 N 个候选编辑（Best-of-N），利用模型先验探索多种修复方向；(3) **验证式接受**——只有同时满足"修复的失败数 > 0"且"对已解决案例的回退（regression）小于阈值 ε"的候选才会被提交为新技能版本。
- 给出 Best-of-N 探索的轻量理论分析（Theorem 1），证明候选池增大带来的期望方向性进展只以 √(ln N) 增长，从而解释了为什么增大候选数 N 的收益迅速饱和（据此选择 N=4 作为默认设置）。
- 在 23 个模型-基准配置（5 个 backbone：Claude-opus-4-6、Qwen-3.7-max、Qwen-3.6-plus、DeepSeek-v4-pro、Kimi-k2.6；5 个 benchmark：SpreadsheetBench、BFCL-v4、LiveMathematicianBench、ALFWorld、DocVQA）上取得一致的最优或近最优表现，且是唯一在所有 pair 上都做到非负增益的方法。
- 通过跨模型迁移（把在 Claude-opus-4-6 上优化的技能搬到 DeepSeek-v4-pro / Kimi-k2.6）与跨基准迁移（LiveMath 技能迁移到 OlympiadBench）实验，证明学到的技能编码的是可迁移的通用程序性知识，而非模型或数据特定的模式。

## Key Contributions

- Identifies **skill overfitting** as the central failure mode of existing skill self-evolution methods (which treat skills as trainable parameters optimized via gradient-like or zeroth-order search over trajectory batches), and recasts self-evolution as a **constrained search problem** governed by an exploration-exploitation trade-off, formalized as an MDP $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r)$ where states are structured skill vectors and actions are targeted component edits.
- Proposes **SkillBoost**, a three-stage evolution loop: **Structured Exploitation** (attribute failed trajectories to specific skill components via workflow-compliance checks, backward reasoning-chain reconstruction, and root-cause clustering into "strategy defects" vs. "capability gaps"), **Prior-guided Exploration** (generate a Best-of-N pool of candidate edits under different repair strategies $\pi^{(n)}$, all grounded in the same failure diagnosis $g_t$), and **Verified Acceptance** (a constrained-commitment gate: accept a candidate only if $r(s') > 0 \wedge \text{Regress}(s') < \epsilon$, selecting the highest-gain candidate that passes).
- Provides a theoretical analysis of Best-of-N exploration under verified acceptance, deriving an expected lower bound on directional progress $\mathbb{E}[\delta_t] \approx \epsilon\sqrt{2\ln N / d}$ and an iteration bound $\mathbb{E}[T_\eta] \geq (\ell-\eta)\sqrt{d/(2\ln N)}$, explaining the diminishing-returns behavior of increasing the candidate pool size.
- Demonstrates SoTA performance across 23 model-benchmark configurations, outperforming human-crafted and LLM-generated skills while substantially mitigating overfitting (near-zero test-train gap).
- Shows via ablations that all three modules (workflow compliance, reasoning-chain reconstruction, root-cause clustering) and the verified-acceptance gate are individually necessary; shows via transfer experiments that optimized skills generalize across agents/backbones and across related benchmarks.

## Methodology

SkillBoost represents a skill as a structured state vector $s_t \in \mathcal{S}$ with coordinate components (Metadata, Workflow, Constraints, References), making skills directly addressable for localized editing rather than monolithic text blobs. Each evolution iteration alternates a **forward rollout** phase and a **backward optimization** phase:

1. **Forward Rollout (Skill-Conditioned Task Execution).** The frozen target LLM $\theta$ executes a batch of tasks $\mathcal{D} = \{(q_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^M$ conditioned on the current skill $s_t$, producing step-level trajectories $\tau_i = \{(o_i^{(j)}, \text{tool}_i^{(j)}, \text{out}_i^{(j)})\}_j$ and predictions $\hat{y}_i$. This yields causal evidence for both successful and failed cases.

2. **Structured Exploitation (Attribution).** Given failed cases $\{(q_i, y_i, \tau_i) \mid \hat{y}_i \neq y_i\}$, an "Analyzer" agent runs three checks on each trajectory: (a) *workflow compliance* — checks whether the step sequence follows the skill's defined workflow, mapping three trajectory patterns to three defect types (skipped steps → weak procedural constraints; followed-workflow-but-wrong-answer → ambiguous instructions; unsupported conclusions → missing evidence-grounding rule); (b) *reasoning chain reconstruction* — walks the trajectory backward from the final output to find the first step $j^*$ where the intended rule was departed from or never reached; (c) *root cause clustering* — groups failures sharing the same defect so one edit can repair the whole cluster, and splits root causes into "strategy defects" (fixable by editing the skill) vs. "capability gaps" (recurring, not fixable by rule edits). The output is a diagnosis $g_t = \{(\text{cause}_k, \text{target}_k)\}_{k=1}^K$ grounded in step-level trajectory evidence, where target$_k$ names the specific editable skill component.

3. **Prior-guided Exploration (Best-of-N Candidate Generation).** With $g_t$ held fixed, SkillBoost generates $N$ candidate edits $a_t^{(n)} = \text{Generate}(s_t, g_t, \pi^{(n)})$ under $N$ different repair strategies $\pi^{(n)}$ (e.g., conservative vs. bold edit scope), each producing a candidate skill $s_{t+1}^{(n)} = P(s_t, a_t^{(n)})$. All candidates address the same real, evidence-grounded failure but differ in repair scope/strategy. Concretely, each candidate is generated via a structured 8-module "Repair Brief" template (Metadata Header, Baseline Performance Summary, Failure Mode Cluster Analysis, Mutation Strategy, Mutation Action Mapping Table, Anti-Regression Guardrails, Back-Testing Results, Execution Plan) that pairs the diagnosis with a concrete edit plan (add/delete/replace text at specific skill locations).

4. **Verified Acceptance (Constrained Commitment).** For each candidate, define gain $r(s') = \text{Eval}(s', \mathcal{D})$ using two case-level metrics: $\text{Fix}(s')$ = fraction of previously-failed cases now repaired, and $\text{Regress}(s')$ = fraction of previously-correct cases now broken. A candidate is accepted only if $\text{Accept}(s') \iff r(s') > 0 \wedge \text{Regress}(s') < \epsilon$; the highest-gain candidate passing this gate becomes $s_{t+1}$; if none pass, the skill stays unchanged. This full-set back-testing (not just re-testing on the targeted failure subset) is what limits regressions "by design."

Baselines compared: No-Skill (frozen model, default prompt), Human Skill (expert-written), One-Shot LLM Skill (generated once, no refinement), Trace2Skill (distills trajectories into skills), and SkillOpt (a gradient-like zeroth-order skill optimization method, described as the prior SoTA).

## Results & Highlights

- **LiveMathematicianBench (Claude-opus-4-6):** SkillBoost reaches **76.0%** accuracy vs. **28.6%** for No-Skill (+47.4pp) and **49.6%** for the strongest baseline SkillOpt (+26.4pp over SkillOpt); Trace2Skill only reaches 46.4% and Human Skill only 37.8%.
- **SpreadsheetBench (Claude-opus-4-6):** SkillBoost achieves **82.5%** vs. **50.0%** No-Skill (+32.5pp) and **75.0%** for the strongest baseline (One-Shot LLM Skill), a +7.5pp margin over the best prior baseline.
- **Across 20 text-only model-benchmark pairs (Table I),** SkillBoost matches or exceeds every baseline, with per-model gains over No-Skill ranging from **+10.6 to +28.4pp**, and is the only method that yields non-negative gains on every single pair (human- and LLM-generated skills sometimes *degrade* performance, e.g., on BFCL-v4 and ALFWorld).
- **DocVQA (multimodal, Table II):** on Qwen-3.6-plus, SkillBoost hits **92.0%** vs. 86.0% No-Skill baseline (+6.0pp) and 90.0% for the strongest baseline (LLM skill, +2.0pp margin); similar gains (+4.0 to +4.6pp over No-Skill) hold on Qwen-3.7-plus and Kimi-k2.6, showing the method extends beyond text-only tasks.
- **Overfitting gap (Table III):** SkillOpt and Trace2Skill consistently show large *negative* Δ = Test − Train (e.g., Claude-opus-4-6/LiveMath: SkillOpt −4.7, Trace2Skill −10.7), i.e., they overfit the training batch, whereas SkillBoost keeps Δ near zero or positive across all backbones/benchmarks (e.g., +1.7 on the same LiveMath/Claude-opus-4-6 cell).
- **Verified-acceptance gate ablation (Table V):** removing the full-set back-testing gate drops BFCL accuracy from 48.5% to 37.7% (Claude-opus-4-6), from 20.8% to 17.0% (Kimi-k2.6), and from 44.6% to 36.3% (DeepSeek-v4-pro); on SpreadsheetBench the drop is from 82.5% to 75.7% (Claude) and 80.0% to 67.5% (DeepSeek) — a concrete quantitative demonstration that regression control, not just failure-fixing, is essential.
- **Cross-model transfer (Table VI):** a skill optimized on Claude-opus-4-6 transferred zero-shot to DeepSeek-v4-pro/Kimi-k2.6 still beats each target model's own human-written skill by 0.7–14.3pp (e.g., BFCL-v4: Claude-opus-4-6-optimized skill on Kimi-k2.6 gets 16.3% vs. 12.5% for Kimi's own human skill).
- **Cross-benchmark transfer:** a LiveMath-optimized skill applied to OlympiadBench improves DeepSeek-v4-pro from 44.0% to 50.0% (+6.0pp) and Kimi-k2.6 from 46.0% to 60.0% (+14.0pp).
- **Best-of-N saturation (Table VII, Fig. 4):** accuracy improves with N but saturates after N=4 (SpreadsheetBench: 61.1% at N=1 → 77.9% at N=4 → 80.4% at N=8, i.e., only +2.5pp from doubling N further while token cost nearly doubles), consistent with the √(ln N) theoretical prediction.
- **Deployment cost:** SkillBoost reduces per-case inference tokens by **13.9% on average** vs. SkillOpt at deployment time, because its constrained edits keep the evolved skill compact (Fig. 3) rather than monotonically growing (SkillOpt's skill length grows unbounded and is later shown to be actively pruned/regularized by SkillBoost's evolution loop, e.g. on BFCL-v4).

## Authors & Credibility

Hongqiang Lin and Chao Liu (equal contribution, †) lead a team from **Zhejiang University** and **Alibaba Group**, with Nenggan Zheng and Xipeng Cao as corresponding authors. The paper is an arXiv preprint (v1, no indication of peer-reviewed venue acceptance at the time of this summary). It builds on and directly compares against several other 2025/2026 arXiv preprints in the same "agent skill self-evolution" niche (Trace2Skill, SkillOpt, and related work like Skillgrad, SkillRL, Skill-r1, Skilladaptor, AutoRefine, Co-EvoSkills), indicating an active, crowded, and very recent research area where most cited baselines are themselves unpublished preprints — a point worth noting when weighing the strength of "state-of-the-art" claims.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty:** The core contribution — reframing skill self-evolution as an MDP/constrained-search problem with an explicit exploration-exploitation trade-off, rather than pure "fit the batch" optimization — is a genuinely useful conceptual reframing, and the diagnosis-then-Best-of-N-repair-then-verify pipeline is a sensible, well-motivated instantiation. The lightweight Best-of-N theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) is a nice touch that directly predicts and explains the observed saturation at N=4, which is a strong piece of internal consistency (theory predicts experiment, and the experiment (Table VII) confirms it).

**Rigor:** The method is evaluated on 23 model-benchmark configurations spanning 5 backbones and 5 benchmarks, with ablations isolating each of the three exploitation sub-modules (Table IV) and the verified-acceptance gate (Table V), plus cross-model and cross-benchmark transfer experiments — this is a reasonably thorough experimental design for a preprint. However, a notable data quirk: the LiveMathematicianBench train split used in the worked appendix example has only **35 questions** (per the Appendix "Repair Brief" walkthrough, "LiveMath train split (35 theorem-grounded multiple-choice questions)"), and other benchmarks' splits are also described as "orders of magnitude smaller than post-training corpora." With such small training splits, single-run accuracy deltas of a few points carry substantial sampling noise, yet the paper reports no variance/error bars or multiple-seed results anywhere in the tables — a real gap for a method whose central claim is about generalization robustness.

**Reproducibility:** No code or skill artifacts are mentioned as released; the "Repair Brief" template and the exact prompts for the Analyzer/Generate steps are described narratively but the full prompt text is not reproduced (only excerpted "exact rule text" snippets in the appendix worked example). The dependence on a frozen LLM to serve as its own diagnosis-and-repair engine makes results sensitive to prompt engineering not fully specified in the paper.

**Significance:** The overfitting-gap analysis (Table III) is the paper's most compelling evidence — showing SkillOpt and Trace2Skill both suffer large negative test-train gaps while SkillBoost stays near zero is a clean, benchmark-spanning demonstration that the method addresses the stated problem, not just a proxy metric.

**Red flags / weak points:**
- The comparison baselines (Human Skill, One-Shot LLM Skill, Trace2Skill, SkillOpt) are all relatively weak by construction — none of them include an explicit anti-regression/back-testing mechanism, so SkillBoost's win on the "verified acceptance" ablation (Table V) is somewhat expected once one designs a gate that directly optimizes the reported overfitting metric. The paper does not compare against RL-based skill-evolution methods it cites in Related Work (e.g., Skill-r1, SkillRL), which could plausibly also mitigate overfitting through a value/reward signal, leaving open whether SkillBoost's gains hold up against that class of stronger baselines.
- The evaluation cost is very high (the paper reports evaluation costs reaching "3–8M tokens per run" for the Best-of-N back-testing), which is a real practical limitation for the "lightweight self-evolution" framing the intro promises as an alternative to post-training; the paper acknowledges this cost explicitly but doesn't compare wall-clock/dollar cost against actual fine-tuning to substantiate the "lightweight" claim.
- The paper explicitly caveats in the Discussion that "weaker models may limit the quality of self-evolution" since the whole pipeline depends on the base model's own ability to attribute failures and generate meaningful edits — this is a legitimate self-identified limitation but also means the method's applicability to smaller/weaker open models is untested (all 5 backbones tested — Claude-opus-4-6, Qwen-3.7-max, Qwen-3.6-plus, DeepSeek-v4-pro, Kimi-k2.6 — are large, capable frontier-tier models).

## Strengths

- Clear, well-motivated conceptual reframing (constrained search / exploration-exploitation MDP) backed by a matching theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) whose qualitative prediction (diminishing returns in N) is empirically confirmed.
- Consistent, non-negative gains across all 23 tested model-benchmark pairs, unlike any of the baselines.
- Direct, benchmark-spanning quantification of the overfitting phenomenon it sets out to solve (Table III), not just an aggregate accuracy win.
- Transfer experiments (cross-model and cross-benchmark) provide evidence that the learned skills capture transferable procedural knowledge rather than memorized batch-specific patterns.
- Appendix qualitative analysis of "representative learned rules" (e.g., the SpreadsheetBench rule discovering that `openpyxl` doesn't evaluate formulas) gives concrete, interpretable evidence of what the method actually discovers, adding credibility beyond aggregate numbers.

## Limitations

- No variance/error-bar reporting despite small train splits (e.g., 35-item LiveMath train set) that make single-run deltas noisy.
- Very high evaluation/back-testing cost (3–8M tokens per run) undercuts the "lightweight alternative to post-training" framing.
- No comparison against RL-based skill-evolution baselines (Skill-r1, SkillRL) mentioned in Related Work, only against prompt/heuristic-based optimization methods (SkillOpt, Trace2Skill).
- Tested only on frontier-tier proprietary/large models (Claude-opus-4-6, Qwen-3.7-max/3.6-plus, DeepSeek-v4-pro, Kimi-k2.6); generalization to smaller open-weight models is unverified and self-acknowledged as a possible failure point.
- Full prompts for the Analyzer and Generate steps are not released, limiting exact reproducibility.

## Relevance

该工作与"技能库/记忆库自演化"及"LLM 智能体持续学习"方向高度相关，其"结构化失败归因 + Best-of-N 候选 + 回退门控"的设计思路对构建可靠的智能体自我改进系统、避免灾难性遗忘/过拟合具有较强参考价值，尤其是 verified-acceptance 的全量回测门控机制值得在类似的 prompt/记忆自优化系统中借鉴。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- How does SkillBoost compare against RL-based skill-evolution methods (Skill-r1, SkillRL) that it cites but does not benchmark against?
- Does the method's benefit hold on smaller/weaker open-weight backbones where self-diagnosis capability may be limited, as the authors themselves flag as a concern?
- What is the actual wall-clock/dollar cost of SkillBoost's multi-round Best-of-N back-testing compared to lightweight LoRA fine-tuning, to substantiate the "lightweight alternative to post-training" claim?
- How sensitive are results to the regression threshold ε in the verified-acceptance gate, and how is ε chosen/tuned per benchmark?
- Would the gains persist with larger, more diverse training splits (current splits are described as "orders of magnitude smaller" than typical corpora), or is part of the observed benefit an artifact of very small-batch evaluation noise?
