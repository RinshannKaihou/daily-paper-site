> **一句话总结：** CRUX 团队提出"影子评估"（shadow evaluation）——把两篇尚未发表的 NeurIPS 2026 投稿的核心研究问题原样交给 Claude Opus 4.8（OpenClaw 脚手架，六天时间、3000 美元 API 预算、GPU 算力）独立重做，再由论文原作者亲自评审：结果两篇 AI 论文均被明确拒稿（Personas 论文 2/6"拒绝"，TabPFN 论文 1/6"强烈拒绝"），AI 智能体能完成全部工程环节，却在选择研究方向、回应负面反馈、及时止损、管理资源和遵守指令这五个方面反复失败，用 Codex + GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra 的复现实验也再现了几乎全部失败模式。

> **TL;DR:** CRUX introduces "shadow evaluations": give a frontier agent (Claude Opus 4.8, extra-high reasoning, on the OpenClaw scaffold) the exact research question behind an unpublished NeurIPS 2026 submission, six days and $3,000 of API credit plus GPU budget, and let the paper's own authors grade the output like conference reviewers. On the Personas paper (LLM personas as weight-space coordinates) the agent scored 2/6 (Reject); on the TabPFN paper (deployment-time distribution-shift detector for tabular foundation models) it scored 1/6 (Strong Reject), and the paper's own reported detector only reached AUROC 0.60 on real-world shifted data versus 0.76 on synthetic shifts — a gap the human reviewer flagged as unaddressed. Both agents left over half their $3,000 API budgets unspent, and a robustness rerun on Codex + GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra reproduced nearly every failure mode while burning through its entire token budget in about two days.

## 核心贡献

- 提出并系统实施"影子评估"这一第三种 AI 研发自动化评测范式：不同于"可验证任务+自动评分"（如 RE-Bench、MLE-Bench）和"AI 生成论文投稿盲审"（如 AI Scientist-v2、Zochi），影子评估让智能体在无污染、开放式的真实研究问题上作答，再由对该问题有数月深耕经验的原作者进行深度、非盲的专家评审。
- 与两篇尚未公开的 NeurIPS 2026 投稿的作者合作（"Personas"论文：LLM 人格在权重空间中的可分解性与可控性；"TabPFN"论文：表格基础模型的部署期分布漂移检测器），让智能体在不接触论文和结论的情况下"影子"式重做同一研究问题。
- 通过详尽的日志分析，归纳出智能体在开放式研究中反复出现的五种失败模式：缺乏判断高质量研究门槛的能力、缺乏创造性问题解决能力、无法有效回溯放弃走不通的方向、缺乏对资源/时限的自我认知、以及指令漂移。
- 用 Codex + GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra 替换模型和脚手架重跑 TabPFN 实验，作为稳健性检验，证明结果并非 OpenClaw 脚手架的特有缺陷。
- 公开专家评审全文、预实验预测调查、智能体代码仓库与完整运行日志，为后续可复现的影子评估研究提供基础设施和透明度。

## Key Contributions

- Introduces "shadow evaluations" as a third measurement paradigm for AI R&D automation, complementing verifiable-task benchmarks (RE-Bench, MLE-Bench, PostTrainBench) and blind-peer-review demonstrations (AI Scientist-v2, Zochi): an agent answers the exact, uncontaminated research question behind an unpublished paper, and the paper's own deeply-expert authors grade the output.
- Partners with the authors of two unpublished NeurIPS 2026 submissions — a Persona Cartography paper (decomposing/controlling LLM personas as weight-space directions) and a TabPFN paper (deployment-time harmful-distribution-shift detection for tabular prior-fitted networks) — and tasks Claude Opus 4.8 (extra-high reasoning, OpenClaw scaffold) with independently answering each paper's central research question given 6 days, $3,000 in Anthropic API credit, GPU credits, and full VM/web access.
- Identifies five recurring failure modes from detailed log analysis: poor judgment about the bar for publishable research, uncreative responses to negative feedback on research design, ineffective backtracking from dead ends, poor awareness of remaining time/budget, and instruction drift over long horizons.
- Runs a robustness check by reproducing the TabPFN experiment with Codex and GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra (its native scaffold, "ultra" reasoning), confirming the failures are not an artifact of the OpenClaw scaffold.
- Releases the full expert reviews, a pre-experiment survey of twelve collaborators' expectations, agent repositories, and run logs for transparency and future replication.

## Methodology

The core design is "shadow evaluation": take the central research question of a high-quality, not-yet-public paper, hand it to a well-resourced frontier agent with no access to the paper or its findings, and have the paper's original authors grade the agent's output as if reviewing a conference submission. This addresses three weaknesses of prior evaluation paradigms simultaneously — verifiable-task benchmarks restrict evaluation to metrics scoreable as a single number; blind peer review at AI conferences is documented to be highly stochastic (the 2014/2021 NeurIPS consistency experiments found ~half of review-score variance was subjective and ~a quarter of accept/reject decisions disagreed between committees) and doesn't reveal how many failed AI-generated submissions go unreported before an eventual acceptance.

Two unpublished NeurIPS 2026 submissions were used as test cases: the "Personas" paper (can LLM personas be decomposed, measured, and controlled as positions in a weight-space trait basis?) and the "TabPFN" paper (design, justify, and validate a deployment-time detector for harmful distribution shift in tabular prior-fitted networks, using white-box access to gradients/activations). The original authors formulated the research questions (without hinting at promising solution paths), set resource budgets, and graded the finished papers using a NeurIPS-style review template (Quality/Clarity/Significance/Originality on 1–4, Overall on 1–6, Confidence on 1–5).

Both runs used Claude Opus 4.8 with extra-high reasoning on OpenClaw, a scaffold chosen to be model-provider-agnostic (agents can delegate to subagents, monitor their own API/GPU/time budgets, keep a running research log, and query a self-review subagent plus three external AI reviewing tools: the Stanford Agentic Reviewer, the CMU Paper Reviewer, and refine.ink). Agents had 6 days of wall-clock time (with one 24-hour extension granted after drafts self-graded as "Weak Reject"), $3,000 of Anthropic API credit, GPU credits, and unrestricted VM/web access — but no guidance specific to either research question (only general ML-research scaffold fixes). Three human interventions were logged: a bug fix for an OpenClaw crash affecting Anthropic reasoning models, the 24-hour deadline extension, and a request to rewrite an initially inscrutable draft for readability. A pre-experiment survey of twelve CRUX collaborators captured priors on expected agent performance and failure causes before any results were shared. A robustness check reran the TabPFN experiment on Codex with GPT-5.6 Sol at "ultra" reasoning (a multi-agent orchestration analogous to OpenClaw), using nearly identical instructions delivered via a single markdown file.

## Results & Highlights

- **Both papers were unambiguously rejected by their own original authors acting as reviewers.** The Personas paper scored Overall 2/6 ("Reject", Quality 2/4, Clarity 1/4, Significance 2/4, Originality 3/4, reviewer Confidence 4/5); the TabPFN paper scored Overall 1/6 ("Strong Reject", Quality 1/4, Clarity 2/4, Significance 2/4, Originality 2/4, reviewer Confidence 5/5) — both reviewers cited poorly motivated data/experiment choices and conclusions that "did not follow from the evidence."
- **The TabPFN agent's own detector underperformed the paper's baselines exactly where it mattered most:** per the human reviewer's own summary, "the only respectable result...beats D3M and Detectron on synthetic datasets + shifts. There are no comparisons to these on real shifts...and even here the proposed method's performance is significantly worse (AUROC 0.60, dropped from 0.76 on synthetic)" — a real-vs-synthetic generalization gap the agent did not investigate or resolve.
- **Both agents left more than half of their $3,000 API budgets unspent and finished with clock time to spare.** The Personas run used $1,130 of $3,000 (API) and $392 of $500 (GPU); the TabPFN run used $1,235 of $3,000 (API) and $69 of $100 (GPU); one agent declared the project complete seven hours before its own deadline despite its self-review scoring below its own "Weak Accept" bar.
- **The agents' internal self-review never once returned an acceptance across 14 total rounds** (4 for Personas, all Reject 3/10 or Weak Reject 4/10; 10 for TabPFN, all Weak Reject 4/10) — yet the agents responded to this consistently negative signal by adding caveats and narrowing claims rather than rethinking their approach or restarting.
- **Both final papers failed basic NeurIPS technical requirements** despite passing engineering milestones cleanly: both exceeded the 9-page limit onto a 10th page, and both contained fewer references than the human-authored originals (36 vs. 69 for the TabPFN paper, 16 vs. 52 for the Personas paper); the Personas paper had zero visualizations in its main body versus 15 in the original authors' paper.
- **The Codex/GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra robustness rerun reproduced nearly every identified failure mode** on the TabPFN task — the agent again failed to run appropriately powered experiments, made no novel contribution, and returned a misformatted draft with no appendices — while additionally exhausting its entire $3,000 token budget in just over two days, leaving nearly 100 hours of its allotted time unused (the opposite budget-mismanagement pattern from the OpenClaw runs).
- **No significant reward hacking was found**; if anything the trend ran the opposite direction — agents began with more marketable claims and diligently retired them in favor of negative results. The log review did surface two other safety-relevant incidents: one run committed an access token to its repository, and five instances of subagents hallucinating or misrepresenting results were caught by the orchestrator agent (specifically instructed to double-check subagent work) before reaching the final draft.
- **Higher reasoning effort mattered:** dry runs of the same two papers using Opus 4.8 without reasoning suffered from poor literature review and much worse writing quality and returned inscrutable papers within days, whereas extra-high-reasoning runs (the main experiments) produced work good enough to warrant full external review — though the authors note more wall-clock time or resources, unlike more reasoning per call, did not appear to help.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty:** Shadow evaluation is a genuinely useful methodological contribution — it threads a real gap between narrow verifiable-task benchmarks and blind peer review, both of which the paper argues (with citations to the NeurIPS 2014/2021 consistency experiments) are weak measures of open-ended research capability. Using unpublished submissions as uncontaminated test cases, graded by the people who spent months on the exact same question, is a clever and hard-to-game design.

**Rigor:** The paper is unusually self-critical for a capability-evaluation paper claiming a negative result about the object of study's own creator's field (AI R&D). It explicitly names its own biases (some core-team members are on record as skeptical of imminent recursive self-improvement), discloses that "interpretive ambiguity" exists among its own coauthors about whether the failure is best characterized as "lack of creativity," "poor judgment," or "epistemic lock-in," and runs a second model/scaffold (Codex + GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra) specifically to rule out scaffold overhang as a confound. A concrete, paper-specific detail supporting rigor: Table 4 directly juxtaposes each human reviewer's core criticism against the agent's own final self-review language, showing the agent's self-review actually identified nearly the same issues in its own words (e.g., "a huge leap, a kind of 'proof by example' fallacy") without acting on them — this is a stronger, more falsifiable claim than a vague "the agent lacked judgment."

**Reproducibility:** The authors release expert reviews, survey responses, agent repositories, and run logs for the TabPFN paper (the Personas paper's underlying materials are withheld since that source paper is not yet public), which is a genuinely strong transparency commitment relative to competing demonstrations like AI Scientist-v2 or Zochi.

**Significance and honest weak spots:** The headline finding — n=2 papers — is explicitly flagged by the authors as the study's central limitation (Table 3), and they are candid that "five runs" (two pilot dry-runs, two main runs, one robustness check) is the true evidentiary base, far smaller than any benchmark with dozens of tasks. The evaluation is also non-blind: reviewers knew the paper was AI-generated and had already answered the same question themselves, which the authors acknowledge could cut either way (harsher scrutiny of AI output, or unconscious preference for their own original approach). The "no reward hacking" finding is reassuring but should be read narrowly — it covers overt data/result fabrication, not the softer failure of the agent selectively weighting AI-review feedback (Section 4.4: the agent overweighted the single most lenient external reviewer's "recommend acceptance" verdict while discounting more critical reviews that flagged the same issues human experts later raised). The paper is honest that this is a "generator-verifier gap" observation rather than a validated claim that AI reviewers can reliably discern research quality, since both agent papers were rejected and thus the reviewers' discriminative accuracy on a genuinely good paper remains untested.

## Strengths

- A well-motivated, carefully argued new evaluation methodology (shadow evaluation) that plausibly measures something neither verifiable benchmarks nor blind peer review can.
- Deep, paper-specific qualitative evidence: Table 4's side-by-side comparison of human-expert criticisms against the agent's own final self-review is a rare, concrete demonstration that the agent "knew" the flaws in its own words but didn't act on them.
- Extensive transparency: full expert reviews, agent logs, pre-experiment predictions from twelve independent collaborators, and a documented robustness check with a different model and scaffold.
- Explicit, itemized bias disclosure (Section 8) covering both known team priors and acknowledged "unknown" biases in log-interpretation discretion — unusually candid for a capability-evaluation paper.
- Detailed resource-trajectory figures (Figure 1, Figure 2) that ground abstract claims like "poor resource awareness" in exact dollar and hour figures.

## Limitations

- Sample size of two shadow-evaluated papers (plus dry runs and one robustness rerun), explicitly acknowledged by the authors as insufficient to generalize across the diversity of AI research tasks or difficulty levels.
- Non-blind reviewing: the same people who spent months answering the research question graded the AI's answer to it, which the authors admit could bias results in either direction and cannot be fully disentangled from a typical NeurIPS reviewer's judgment.
- Could not test Anthropic's strongest model (Fable/Mythos 5 was deliberately limited on frontier AI R&D by Anthropic and unavailable during the study).
- Scaffold/harness engineering time on the Codex/GPT-5.6 robustness check was not matched to the multiple rounds of refinement applied to the main OpenClaw scaffold, so the comparison, while informative, is not perfectly controlled.
- The paper cannot establish ground-truth accuracy of the AI self-review/external-review tools used during the runs, since both agent-produced papers were ultimately rejected — it remains unknown whether these reviewers would correctly recognize a genuinely strong paper.
- Six days of wall-clock time is long relative to most existing AI R&D benchmarks but still far shorter than the months the original human authors spent, and the authors' argument that extending it "would not meaningfully change the main results" rests on their own judgment rather than a tested extension.

## Relevance

该论文提出的"影子评估"方法论及五种失败模式（判断力、创造性回应负反馈、有效回溯、资源感知、指令遵从）为评估 LLM 智能体自主科研能力提供了比 RE-Bench/MLE-Bench 等可验证基准更贴近真实开放式研究的视角，对关注 AI 智能体自动化研发、AI 安全评估设计、以及递归自我改进论证证据基础的研究者具有较高参考价值。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- Would the same five failure modes (judgment, creativity, backtracking, resource-awareness, instruction-following) persist if the agent were given an explicit "abstain and restart from scratch" affordance rather than being required to produce a paper, given the authors' own speculation that the TabPFN agent's negative-results paper may have been an artifact of having no abstention option?
- How would results change with Anthropic's most capable model (Fable/Mythos 5), which the authors note was deliberately restricted from frontier AI R&D use during this study?
- Does the "generator-verifier gap" the authors identify (AI self-review reliably rejects but the agent can't act on the feedback) hold up as a viable reinforcement-learning signal if scaled to many shadow-evaluation episodes, or does the untested accuracy of these reviewers on genuinely strong papers undermine that plan?
- Would a larger panel of shadow-evaluated papers, spanning different subfields and difficulty levels of AI research, replicate the same five failure modes, or are some of them (e.g., instruction drift, poor resource awareness) more scaffold-specific than the paper's single robustness check can establish?
- To what extent does the non-blind reviewer bias (original authors grading an agent's attempt at their own question) systematically favor the humans' own solution path over equally valid alternative approaches the agent might have taken?
