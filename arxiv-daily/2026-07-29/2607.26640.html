Why this matters

A side-by-side annotation protocol with rigorous bias checks could cut the cost of large-scale MT evaluation — and the recipe transfers to LLM-output and RLHF preference annotation.

Critical edge

The k=3 optimum comes from one 97-segment, single-language-pair dataset with a non-monotonic time trend (77.8→55.4→53.7→59.3s) and no significance test separating k=2 from k=3 — the "best k" could plausibly be noise.

01 Methodology

↑ one annotation screen at k=3: three MT outputs of the same document, error spans marked, each scored 0–100%.

  • Two changes to standard ESA. (1) Show k translations of the same source document on one screen instead of one at a time. (2) Replace the free slider with a 5%-step scale anchored to five labeled bands (Very Good / Good / Acceptable / Borderline / Not acceptable).
  • Annotators mark minor/major error spans in each of the k outputs, then assign each a final score; they can navigate back and revise earlier screens — a gap in prior contrastive tools.
  • Data: WMT25 English→Japanese task, 97 segments across 38 documents (news, video, social screenshots), 10 systems including Gemini-2.5-Pro, Claude-4, DeepSeek-V3, CommandA-MT, and human translation.
  • Design: within-subject, randomized order, professional EN→JA translators, every item annotated twice (inter-annotator agreement) plus duplicated items (intra-annotator agreement); k swept over {1,2,3,4}; stability = Kendall's τb on bootstrapped subsets vs. full ranking.
  • Bias tests are counterfactual, not raw correlation — controlling for source-sentence difficulty when checking position, "surrounding similarity" (chrF-based), and "surrounding quality" (decoy-effect) bias.

Annotators' self-reported preferred max k (median): 2, 2, 3, 3, 3 — corroborating the objective k=3 recommendation.

Strengths
  • Open-source, production-ready tooling (Pearmut) with 18-language-pair tutorials — not a paper-only proposal.
  • Counterfactual bias analysis controls for source-difficulty confound, filling a real methodological gap in prior contrastive evaluation work.
  • Reports efficiency (time/cost) and quality (agreement, stability) together, rather than trading one off silently.
  • Validated at WMT25 scale with professional translators and two independent annotation passes per item.
Limitations
  • Single language pair (English→Japanese) and single shared task (WMT25); generalization untested.
  • Small-scale ablation and large-scale k-sweep used different annotator pools and model sets — their k=1 numbers aren't directly comparable.
  • Presentation-order effects are only partially addressed (shuffling + revision), not eliminated.
  • No comparison to MQM, the more detailed categorized gold-standard human evaluation, under the same contrastive setup.

Open questions

  • Does the k=3 optimum hold for language pairs with very different reading/scanning patterns (e.g. right-to-left, or much longer/shorter segments)?
  • How does cESA perform when co-displayed outputs are deliberately adversarial — all near-identical, or all wildly different in quality?
  • Would formally testing the decoy-effect bias (−0.5 to −1.4 pts) for significance change the "biases are negligible" conclusion?
  • Could cESA combine with MQM's categorized error taxonomy without losing its efficiency gains?
  • Would non-professional or crowd-sourced annotators show a different optimal k than professional translators?
