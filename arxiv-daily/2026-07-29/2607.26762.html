Probing study · computational linguistics · short paper

Across masked, diffusion, and autoregressive LMs, no semantic relation reaches even 0.30 controlled F1 — relata regions barely exist.

Relation Geometry in Semantic Space of Language Models ↗ Zhihan Cao, Hiroaki Yamada, Simone Teufel et al. (7 authors) arXiv 2607.26762 · 29 Jul 2026
bilinear probe semantic relations hypernymy / antonymy / synonymy LM interpretability ModernBERT · LLaDA-8B · LLaMA-3.1-8B

Key metric

< 0.30

Controlled F1 (relation predictability) for every real relation, across all four model families. Best single result: LLaMA-3.1-8B on hypernymy, F=.25. The "unrelated pair" control class scores .46 — higher than any genuine relation, for every model.

Why this matters

Quantifies how poorly today's LLMs geometrically encode meaning relations — testing the limits of distributional semantics with a leakage-proof, multi-architecture probe.

Critical edge

The three LMs differ in parameter count, training corpus, and context window as well as architecture, so LLaMA's apparent edge over ModernBERT can't be cleanly attributed to its causal-LM design — a confound the authors flag themselves.

Methodology

Bilinear probe · leak-proof SemCor/WordNet dataset · three controlled metrics

SemCor + WordNet nouns → 2.7M direct + 6.88M indirect triplets → bilinear probe per relation → controlled F / D / T scores
f_r(w, v) = softmax( e_wT · W_r · e_v ) — one learned matrix W_r per relation r

Each W_r defines a hyperplane separating words that stand in relation r to a target word from those that don't — so strong probe accuracy is direct evidence of a linearly separable "relata region" per relation. Six WordNet relations are tested (hypernymy, hyponymy, holonymy, meronymy, synonymy, antonymy) plus an unrelated-pair control class.

  • Leakage controls: lemma-disjoint train/test splits, removal of intra-sentential co-occurring pairs, and direct-only training triplets (indirect pairs held out entirely for transitivity evaluation) — each fixes a specific flaw the authors document in prior probing work.
  • Three metrics, all "controlled" (raw score minus a random-probe baseline trained on same-dimension random vectors, to cancel the probe's own capacity): Relation Predictability (F1, 4-fold CV × 10 trials), Directionality (does swapping word order flip the prediction to the reverse relation), Transitivity (F1 on indirect hypernymy/hyponymy triplets at hierarchy distance 2–9).
  • Models compared under one protocol: ModernBERT-large (masked, 395M params), LLaDA-8B-Base (diffusion, 8B), LLaMA-3.1-8B (causal, 8B), plus a fastText static-embedding baseline — using final-layer, average-of-subwords token representations.
  • Bonus ablation: lexical information is removed by masking the target token; contextual information is removed by flattening attention to one-hot ("degenerate") or uniform ("egalitarian", τ=100) — isolating how much each model leans on surface form (ΔLEX) vs. context (ΔCTX).

LLaMA leads all 4 asymmetric relations: HPO .22 · HOL .27 · MER .21 Directionality ceiling: no model-relation pair exceeds D = 0.20 (best: LLaMA on hypernymy, .18) Transitivity (HYP/HPO): LLaMA +.15 / +.12 vs. static −.06 / −.16 and LLaDA −.02 / −.06
Antonymy is the one relation where the context-blind fastText baseline (F=.16, D=.12) beats every billion-parameter LM outright — the authors read this as possible counter-evidence to the claim that relation knowledge is best learned purely from context.

All models' transitivity scores decline monotonically with hypernymy distance: the static baseline and LLaDA drop below chance around distance 4–5, while LLaMA stays positive until distance 9. A lexical/contextual ablation (ΔLEX vs. ΔCTX) further shows LLaMA relies more on surface-form lexical information, while ModernBERT and LLaDA lean more on contextual information for most relations — though even they shift toward lexical reliance for long-distance transitivity pairs.

✓ Strengths

  • Rigorous leakage prevention — lemma-disjoint splits, intra-sentential removal, direct-only transitivity training — that directly fixes flaws documented in prior probing studies cited by name.
  • Broad relation coverage (6 relations) vs. prior work's near-exclusive focus on hypernymy, under one unified bilinear-probe framework.
  • Random-probe control applied to every reported metric, isolating genuine semantic geometry from probe overfitting/capacity effects.
  • Novel, principled ablation quantifying lexical vs. contextual contribution via attention-distribution manipulation, not heuristic feature removal.
  • Honest, non-cherry-picked reporting — the poor synonymy geometry and the antonymy counterexample are reported and discussed, not downplayed.

✕ Limitations

  • Confounded model comparison: the three LMs differ in scale, training data, and context window in addition to architecture, so "causal LMs capture relations better" isn't isolable from scale/data effects.
  • Only linear probes are used — the paper can't tell whether synonymy is encoded non-linearly rather than absent from the space.
  • Data is restricted to nouns from SemCor/Brown Corpus, reflecting pre-1970s English usage, limiting ecological validity today.
  • No downstream-task validation — poor probing performance is never shown to correlate with actual task failure (e.g., WSD, entailment).
  • Excludes multiword expressions, named entities, and subword-level relations, narrowing what "relation geometry" actually covers.

Questions for Further Exploration

  1. Would non-linear probes (small MLPs, kernels) recover clearer synonymy/antonymy geometry, or does the failure persist regardless of probe capacity?
  2. How would results change with same-scale model comparisons (causal, masked, diffusion LMs all at matched size and training data) to isolate architecture from scale/data confounds?
  3. Does poor relata-region separability for synonymy/antonymy actually predict downstream failure on tasks like paraphrase or contradiction detection?
  4. Would the antonymy finding replicate against contrastive/instruction-tuned embedding models (e.g., sentence-transformers) built specifically for semantic similarity?
  5. How sensitive are the reported scores to using the best intermediate layer per relation rather than only the final layer?
