> **一句话总结：** 本文确定了"单调对手"(monotone adversary) 模型下的最优学习速率：当 VC 维 $d \geq 2$ 且插入预算随问题规模增长时，极小化期望误差为 $\Theta((d/n)\log(n/d))$，比干净 i.i.d. 情形多出一个无法消除的对数因子；而在 $d=1$ 时干净的 $\Theta(1/n)$ 速率得以保留，由一个基于留一法 (one-inclusion) 论证的非本真 (improper) 学习器达到。

> **TL;DR:** Resolves the two open questions of Larsen–Pabbaraju–Shetty [LPS26] on the monotone-adversary model (an adaptive adversary appends correctly-labeled examples to a clean i.i.d. sample, breaking exchangeability). The author proves the minimax expected error is $\Theta(1/n)$ at VC dimension one (achieved by a new improper leave-one-out learner, vs. the clean PAC rate $\Theta(d/n)$ as baseline — so dimension one is unaffected) but $\Theta((d/n)\log(n/d))$ for every $d \geq 2$ — a strictly worse, inherent logarithmic gap over the i.i.d. baseline, attained by an ERM and matched by a projective-plane lower bound against all (possibly improper, randomized) learners. The same rates hold with Littlestone dimension replacing VC dimension, so the clean online-to-batch rate $O(d_L/n)$ is also unattainable.

## 核心贡献

- **完全确定了单调对手模型下的最优速率 (Theorem 3.1)：** 对于 VC 维恰为 $d$ 的二分类假设类和 $n$ 个干净样本，在所有有限已知插入预算上取上确界后的极小化期望误差为
  - $d = 0$: $R(n,m,0) = 0$；
  - $d = 1$: $R(n,m,1) = \Theta(1/n)$，且对任意预算 $m$ 成立，由一个简单的非本真 (improper) 共识学习器达成（上界 $1/(n+1)$，下界 $1/(2e(n+1))$）；
  - $d \geq 2$: $\sup_m R(n,m,d) = \Theta\!\left(\frac{\min\{d,n\}}{n}\log\frac{en}{\min\{d,n\}}\right)$。
- **回答了 LPS26 的第一个公开问题：** 这个多出来的 $\log(n/d)$ 因子并非 ERM 这类特定算法的产物，而是非可交换性 (lack of exchangeability) 的内在代价。结合 LPS26 已有的 ERM 上界，ERM 在此模型中反而是 $d \geq 2$ 时的最优学习器——这反转了经典 PAC 理论中 ERM 比最优学习器多一个对数因子的图景。
- **回答了 LPS26 的第二个公开问题 (Theorem 3.3)：** 即使把 VC 维替换为 Littlestone 维 $d_L$，最优速率仍为 $\Theta((d_L/n)\log(n/d_L))$，因此干净的在线到批处理 (online-to-batch) 速率 $O(d_L/n)$ 在单调插入下不可达——即便对于 $d_L = 2$ 的单一固定类也是如此。
- **$d=1$ 的上界用了新的技术：** 提出一个基于 Ben-David $f$-表示 + 留一法 (leave-one-out) / one-inclusion graph 论证的共识规则；关键引理 3.13 证明在 VC 维 1 下，无论对手如何自适应地填充每个留一样本，总留一误差不超过 1。
- **所有下界都来自单一构造：** 基于有限射影平面 (projective plane) 的显式假设类 + 一个"困难先验"，使两个在非平凡质量点上取值不同的目标假设 $u_p$ 与 $v_L$ 能被对手填充成完全相同的最终多重集 $U_f$，从而任何学习器都无法从输入中区分二者；对 $d \geq 3$ 通过对不相交副本取笛卡尔积 (Cartesian product) 放大。

## Key Contributions

- **Complete characterization of optimal rates (Theorem 3.1).** For binary hypothesis classes of exact VC dimension $d$ with $n$ clean examples, the worst-case (over finite known insertion budgets) minimax expected error is $\Theta(1/n)$ at $d=1$ for every budget $m$, and $\Theta((\min\{d,n\}/n)\log(en/\min\{d,n\}))$ for $d \geq 2$. The $d=0$ case has zero risk.
- **Resolves Open Question 1 of Larsen–Pabbaraju–Shetty [LPS26].** The extra $\log(n/d)$ factor is an inherent consequence of losing exchangeability, not an artifact of ERM-type analyses. Combined with the LPS26 ERM upper bound, ERM is in fact an *optimal* learner at $d \geq 2$, inverting the classical picture where ERM loses a log factor to more elaborate PAC learners (one-inclusion graph, majority vote, bagging, majority-of-three).
- **Resolves Open Question 2 of [LPS26] for Littlestone dimension (Theorem 3.3).** Replacing $d$ by Littlestone dimension $d_L$, the optimal rate remains $\Theta((d_L/n)\log(n/d_L))$, so the clean online-to-batch rate $O(d_L/n)$ is unattainable — already for a single fixed class with $d_L = 2$.
- **New improper learner at $d=1$.** A simple consensus rule (predict 1 only when all version-space hypotheses predict 1, after Ben-David's $f$-relabeling) achieves error $\leq 1/(n+1)$ against *every* adaptive monotone adversary. Its analysis rests on Lemma 3.13: at VC dimension one, the total number of leave-one-out errors is at most one, *even though the adversary may augment each leave-one-out sample differently*, so the augmented samples need not be exchangeable.
- **A single elementary lower-bound construction.** All lower bounds flow from one idea: build a finite projective-plane-based class $H_q$ of VC dimension exactly 2, a hard prior over target/distribution pairs $(u_p, D^{\text{row}}_p)$ and $(v_L, D^{\text{col}}_L)$, and an adversary that pads the clean sample to one of two indistinguishable multisets $U_f$ (version space $\{u_p, v_L\}$) or $W_{h^\star}$ (singleton version space). Because the posterior stays balanced at $1/2$ on $U_f$, every learner errs with probability $\geq 1/(2k)$ at the contested point. Higher dimensions are handled by taking the Cartesian product of $\lfloor d/2 \rfloor$ disjoint blocks (Theorem 3.10).

## Methodology

The paper is purely theoretical (no experiments). It works in the monotone-adversary model formalized in Definition 1: an adversary $A: \mathcal{X}^n \to \mathcal{X}^m$ observes the clean sample, appends $m$ correctly-labeled points of its choice, and the learner receives a uniform shuffle of the $n+m$ examples. Crucially, the adversary is *adaptive* (it sees the clean sample) but must label every inserted point by the true target $h^\star$; it does not see the test point, the shuffle, or the learner's coins.

The minimax risk $R(n,m,d)$ is defined as the worst case (over classes of VC dimension exactly $d$, targets, distributions, and deterministic adversaries with exact budget $m$) of the best achievable expected test error (Eq. 1), and $R(n,d) = \sup_m R(n,m,d)$.

The upper bounds are proved as follows. The $d=0$ case is trivial (all hypotheses coincide). For $d=1$, the author adapts the classical leave-one-out / one-inclusion-graph argument of Haussler–Littlestone–Warmuth: draw $n+1$ i.i.d. points, apply the adversary *independently* to each leave-one-out sample, and use the permutation-invariance of the consensus rule plus Lemma 3.13 (which bounds the total leave-one-out error by 1 at VC dimension 1) to get risk $\le 1/(n+1)$. For $d \geq 2$, the upper bound is the existing ERM result of [LPS26] (Theorem 3.15), which the present paper shows is tight.

The lower bounds all instantiate a single indistinguishability template. For $d=2$ (Theorem 3.5), the class $H_q$ is built over the points/lines of a projective plane of prime order $q$ (with $K = q^2+q+1$ points, $k = q+1$ lines through each point): a point-concept $u_p$ and a line-concept $v_L$ are defined on "checker" coordinates $c_{rM}$ and "selector" coordinates $s_{rM}$. A hard prior picks $(u_p, D^{\text{row}}_p)$ or $(v_L, D^{\text{col}}_L)$ each with probability $1/2$. The adversary, given a sample missing some support checker, completes it to the multiset $U_f$ whose version space is exactly $\{u_p, v_L\}$ — so the learner cannot tell which target produced its input. Because the two hypotheses disagree on the missing checker $c_{pL}$ (which has mass $1/k$), the Bayes-balanced posterior forces error $\geq \rho/(2k)$, where $\rho$ is the probability a support checker is missing. A coupon-collector bound (Lemma 3.9) gives $\rho \geq 2/3$ for the chosen $q \asymp n/\log(en)$, yielding risk $\geq \log(en)/(30n)$. For $d \geq 3$ (Theorem 3.10), the construction takes $\lfloor d/2 \rfloor$ disjoint tagged copies of $H_q$ as a Cartesian product, the marginal distribution picks a block uniformly, and each "successful" block contributes $1/(2rk)$ to the risk; choosing $q$ at scale $n/r$ produces the $\log(en/d)$ factor. The Littlestone-dimension result (Theorem 3.3) reuses a checker-only countable variant $H_{\text{prime}}$ (Lemma 3.16 shows $\text{Ldim}(H_{\text{prime}}) = 2$) and the same amplification. All randomized adversaries are derandomized at the end via the probabilistic method, so the lower bounds hold against deterministic adversaries.

## Results & Highlights

This is a theory paper; its quantitative results are minimax rate theorems rather than empirical benchmark numbers. The "baseline" throughout is the classical i.i.d. (clean) PAC rate $\Theta(\min\{1, d/n\})$ of Ehrenfeucht–Haussler–Kearns–Valiant and Haussler–Littlestone–Warmuth.

- **Metric: minimax expected test error, $d=1$ regime, against any adaptive monotone adversary (Proposition 3.4 lower + Proposition 3.14 upper).** The paper proves $R(n,m,1) = \Theta(1/n)$ for every budget $m$, with the explicit bounds $1/(2e(n+1)) \leq R(n,m,1) \leq 1/(n+1)$. Compared with the clean i.i.d. baseline $\Theta(1/n)$, the delta is *zero*: at VC dimension one, monotone insertions cause no loss. This is the only regime where the clean rate survives.
- **Metric: minimax expected test error, $d \geq 2$ regime, worst case over known finite budgets (Theorem 3.1 part 3).** The paper proves $\sup_m R(n,m,d) \asymp \frac{\min\{d,n\}}{n}\log\frac{en}{\min\{d,n\}}$, with an explicit $d=2$ lower bound of $\log(en)/(30n)$ (Theorem 3.5) and a $d \geq 3$ lower bound (Theorem 3.10) matching the LPS26 ERM upper bound (Theorem 3.15) up to constants. Versus the clean i.i.d. baseline $\Theta(d/n)$, the delta is a multiplicative $\log(n/d)$ factor that is proven inherent against *every* (possibly improper, randomized) learner — closing the gap left open by LPS26, who could only show $\Omega((d/n)\log(n/d))$ for *specific* algorithm families.
- **Metric: minimax expected test error indexed by Littlestone dimension $d_L$, worst case over budgets (Theorem 3.3).** The paper proves $R_L(n,d_L) \asymp \frac{\min\{d_L,n\}}{n}\log\frac{en}{\min\{d_L,n\}}$ for $d_L \geq 2$, and $\Theta(1/n)$ for $d_L = 1$. Compared with the clean online-to-batch baseline $O(d_L/n)$, the delta is again a $\log(n/d_L)$ factor, witnessed already by a *single fixed class* with $d_L = 2$. This answers LPS26's Open Question 2 in the negative.
- **Bounded-budget regime (Remark 3.2).** When the insertion budget is $m = O(1)$ (with $n \geq 8(m+1)$), a majority vote over optimal clean learners trained on all $t = \lfloor n/(4(m+1)) \rfloor$-element subsets achieves risk $O(d/t) = O(d(m+1)/n)$, recovering $\Theta(\min\{1,d/n\})$ for fixed $m$. Hence the logarithmic separation provably requires the budget to *grow* with $n, d$ — the paper pins down exactly where the gap appears and where it does not.

## Authors & Credibility

The sole author, Anay Mehrotra, is affiliated with Stanford University and works in learning theory; the paper builds directly on (and cites) the recent ALT 2026 paper of Larsen, Pabbaraju, and Shetty that introduced the model. The arguments are mathematically self-contained: all lower bounds rest on a single, checkable projective-plane construction, and the VC-dimension and Littlestone-dimension calculations for the constructed classes are proved explicitly (Lemma 3.7, Lemma 3.16) rather than asserted. The acknowledgments candidly disclose that "GPT 5.6 Sol and Claude Fable were routinely used in the development of ideas used in the paper" — a transparency that is unusual and commendable, though it means a reader should weight the proofs (which are written out) rather than any informal intuition. The paper is a single-author work resolving two stated open questions of a strong prior result, which is a credible venue for such a contribution.

## Critical Assessment

- **Novelty:** High and well-scoped. Prior to this paper, LPS26 proved an $O((d/n)\log(n/d))$ ERM upper bound and $\Omega((d/n)\log(n/d))$ lower bounds *only for specific algorithm families* (one-inclusion with uniform tie-breaking, majority-of-$t$). The genuinely new contribution is an *algorithm-independent* lower bound against every (improper, randomized) learner, which requires a fundamentally different indistinguishability argument (two targets producing the same multiset) rather than exploiting a particular algorithm's tie-breaking. The $d=1$ improper learner is also new and non-obvious.
- **Rigor:** Proofs are written in full and are elementary (coupon-collector + Bayes posterior + a VC shattering argument). The one place I would scrutinize is the posterior-balance argument in the $d \geq 3$ product construction (Theorem 3.10): the conditioning on the hidden count vector $(N_1,\dots,N_r)$ is handled by a multinomial-with-equal-cells symmetry, and the claim that swapping one block's target leaves other blocks' likelihoods unchanged relies on the blocks being on disjoint domains; this is correct but is exactly the step where a subtle bug could hide, and the reader must verify the "for every complete ordered input with positive probability … posterior $1/2$" claim carefully.
- **Reproducibility / checkability:** Strong. The lower-bound construction is finite and explicit (a projective plane of a prime order $q$ chosen via Bertrand's postulate), so the constants ($\rho \geq 2/3$, the $1/30$ and $1/90$ prefactors) are independently verifiable. There are no experiments to reproduce.
- **Significance:** This is a clean resolution of two explicitly stated open questions. The conceptual punchline — *adding correctly-labeled examples can make learning harder by a log factor* — is memorable and refines the usual intuition that clean-label data augmentation is harmless. The practical implications for applied ML are limited (the model is worst-case and the separating budget $m$ grows with $n$), which the paper is honest about.
- **Red flags / caveats worth flagging:**
  - **The logarithmic separation needs a growing budget.** Remark 3.2 shows that for $m = O(1)$ the clean rate $\Theta(d/n)$ is still achievable. A casual reader of the headline "$\log(n/d)$ gap" could miss that the worst case is taken *over* $m$ and that the witnessing $m = m(n,d)$ is large (order $n|\mathcal{X}_q|$). The optimal risk as a function of $m$ in between the bounded and worst-case regimes is explicitly left open — so the result is sharp only at the two endpoints, not throughout the $(n, m, d)$ space.
  - **High-probability (PAC, $(\varepsilon,\delta)$) sample complexity is not addressed.** The paper states only expected-error rates; the optimal $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ rate under monotone insertions remains open (listed as a further direction). Since the most-cited prior results (Hanneke, Larsen, Aden-Ali et al.) are high-probability, this is a meaningful gap relative to what practitioners of PAC theory usually want.
  - **No computational/efficiency story.** The $d=1$ upper-bound learner and the lower-bound constructions are information-theoretic; whether the optimal $d \geq 2$ rate is achievable *efficiently* is open, as is efficient learning when VC bounds are vacuous (e.g., convex bodies with Gaussian marginals). The author flags this honestly rather than overclaiming.
  - **Single-author, AI-assisted development.** As noted, the paper candidly reports routine use of LLMs. The proofs as written are human-checkable and theorems are precisely stated, so this does not undermine correctness — but it does mean the "ideas" layer was co-developed with models, and a skeptical reader should treat the informal motivation as such.
  - **The lower-bound class is a deliberately pathological combinatorial object** (projective planes), not a "natural" hypothesis class. This is standard and acceptable for an information-theoretic lower bound, but it means the result says nothing about whether natural classes (e.g., halfspaces, neural nets) suffer the same gap; the "structural properties of a fixed class that determine its rate" is explicitly left open.

## Strengths

- Resolves both open questions of [LPS26] completely and with matching upper/lower bounds at every VC and Littlestone dimension.
- The lower-bound technique is unified and elegant: a single projective-plane + indistinguishability construction scales from $d=2$ to all $d \geq 2$ and to the Littlestone setting, with a clean coupon-collector analysis governing the probability $\rho$ of the "missing checker" event.
- The $d=1$ positive result is a nice, simple improper learner whose analysis cleanly separates where the one-inclusion argument *does* survive adaptivity (dimension one) from where it *cannot* (dimension two), giving a crisp conceptual explanation for the phase transition at $d=2$.
- Honest about the regime where the result bites (growing budget) and where it does not (bounded budget), and explicit about the many remaining open problems (rates as a function of $m$, high-probability complexity, partial/multiclass classes, efficient algorithms).
- Transparent disclosure of AI assistance in the acknowledgments.

## Limitations

- **Worst case is over the budget $m$.** Theorem 3.1 part 3 takes $\sup_m$, and the witnessing budget $m(n,d)$ is large (order $n|\mathcal{X}_q|$). For any *fixed* budget $m = O(1)$, Remark 3.2 shows the clean rate survives, so the $\log(n/d)$ gap is not a statement about bounded data augmentation. The optimal rate as a function of $(n,m,d)$ jointly is open.
- **Expected error only.** No $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ high-probability bounds are given, which is the more standard PAC currency.
- **Information-theoretic, not algorithmic.** The paper does not establish that the optimal $d \geq 2$ rate is achievable by an efficient algorithm; the lower bounds are against all learners but the matching upper bound is just ERM, whose *computational* efficiency in the monotone model (especially when VC bounds are vacuous, e.g., convex bodies with Gaussian marginals) is open.
- **Binary classification only.** Extension to partial/multiclass concept classes (where uniform convergence is unavailable) is left open; the binary lower bound already rules out $O(d/n)$ there, but the exact rate is unknown.
- **Lower-bound classes are combinatorial/unnatural** (projective planes), so the result is tight in the worst case but silent about structured classes of practical interest (halfspaces, DNFs, neural nets).
- **No empirical validation**, which is appropriate for the venue (pure learning theory) but means there is no demonstration that the gap manifests on realistic data distributions.

## Relevance

Directly relevant to the learning-theory thread of the user's interests (statistical learning under distribution shift / non-i.i.d. data, robustness of PAC guarantees). The monotone-adversary model isolates *loss of exchangeability* from other deficiencies, and the result sharpens our understanding of when correctly-labeled data augmentation (a routine practice) can provably hurt generalization. It is most useful as a theoretical reference for anyone studying sample-permutation invariance, one-inclusion-graph learners, or clean-label data poisoning.

## Questions for Further Exploration

- What is the optimal expected error as an explicit function of the triple $(n, m, d)$ — interpolating between the $m = O(1)$ clean-rate regime (Remark 3.2) and the worst-case $m \to \infty$ regime (Theorem 3.1)? In particular, is there a sharp transition at some $m^\star(n,d)$, and how does it scale?
- What is the optimal *high-probability* (PAC, $(\varepsilon,\delta)$) sample complexity under monotone insertions? The expected-error results here, combined with Markov, only give the loose $O(d/(\varepsilon\delta))$-style translation; is the classical optimal $O((d+\log 1/\delta)/\varepsilon)$ recoverable, perhaps with a log factor, or is there a separate $\delta$-dependence?
- Can the projective-plane lower-bound class be replaced by a "natural" class (e.g., halfspaces in $\mathbb{R}^d$, or a small neural network) to show the $\log(n/d)$ gap manifests for hypothesis classes used in practice, not just combinatorial constructions?
- Is the optimal $d \geq 2$ rate achievable by a *computationally efficient* learner, and when VC-based bounds are vacuous (e.g., convex bodies under Gaussian marginals, as raised by LPS26), can distributional assumptions still yield efficient monotone-robust learners?
- Do the results extend to partial/multiclass concept classes, where uniform convergence is unavailable? The binary lower bound already rules out $O(d/n)$, but the exact minimax rate is open — does a similar $\log$ factor appear, and does the DS-dimension / analogous online dimension govern it?
- The $d=1$ upper bound uses a leave-one-out consensus rule adapted to *independently re-augmented* leave-one-out samples. Does this technique transfer to other settings where exchangeability of the *full* sample fails but exchangeability of clean sub-blocks survives (e.g., data deduplication, reweighting, or offline clean-label poisoning)?
