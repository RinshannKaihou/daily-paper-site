“Why Ranking Anomaly Detection Algorithms Isn't as Reliable as You May Think”

Rankings are so unstable that any 5 of 7 methods can be crowned “best.”

02 · Methodology

One factor at a time

Seven lightweight AD algorithms (KNN, LOF, IFOR, HBOS, PCA, CBLOF, SEAN) are run across the 690 OddBench tabular datasets — deliberately shallow so the study can afford thousands of repeated benchmark runs infeasible with deep models. Each dataset is capped at 5,000 train / 5,000 test instances.

σ_rank = meanalgo [ stdsetting ( Rank(algo, setting) ) ]

Low = stable rankings; high = rankings hinge on experimental design. Under a Gaussian reading, 0.34 means only ~68% of ranks sit within ±0.34 of their expectation — large, given the 1.51-position mean gap.

Isolated sources of variability
  • Hyperparameter selection10 random values / algo, combined into 100 settingsHigh
  • Dataset count & choice25 / 100 / 400 subsets; selection of which datasetsHigh
  • Random seed100 draws, other factors fixedLow

03 · Results

What the numbers say

  • Instability is large in absolute terms: σrank ≈ 0.34 vs. a 1.51-position mean rank gap — uncertainty spans a big share of the whole spread (KNN 3.37 → CBLOF 4.88).
  • Almost any method can be SOTA: KNN wins 44.9% of settings, but IFOR (17.4%), PCA (13.2%), LOF (12.3%) and SEAN (12.2%) each top the ranking somewhere.
  • Gaming has limits: CBLOF never wins; HBOS tops only 1 of 1,016 settings — persistently weak methods stay weak.
  • Easy to fabricate a win: ≤ 8 random settings are enough to find a configuration where a competitive method is best overall.
  • Source attribution: hyperparameters and dataset count drive σrank far more than seeds or the ROC-AUC/AUCPR choice.
  • Saturation: σrank ≈ 1.63·x⁻⁰·⁴⁰ — ~200 datasets push it to a reliable ≈0.2, then the curve flattens.

Strengths +

  • Clean factor-isolating design that decomposes instability into interpretable sources, not a single aggregate.
  • σrank is a simple, reusable metric other benchmarks can self-report to flag their own fragility.
  • Large 690-dataset pool gives the saturation curve real statistical leverage.
  • Honest, self-critical framing — active AD researchers auditing their own community's SOTA culture.
  • Concrete, adoptable guidance: ≥200 datasets, report instability scores, prioritize diversity over more seeds.
  • Public code release at github.com/psorus/reproduce.

Limitations !

  • Only 7 lightweight classical methods — claims about modern deep / foundation-model AD are extrapolations.
  • Hyperparameter search spaces and the “fixed” defaults aren't fully specified, weakening per-factor reproducibility.
  • The 5,000-instance cap is a confound that may favor/penalize algorithm families differently.
  • No significance test between factors — only point estimates of σrank are shown.
  • The Gaussian reading of rank dispersion ignores that ranks are bounded and ordinal.
  • No analysis of which dataset properties flip rankings, limiting constructive guidance.
04 · Open Questions

Where the audit goes next

1Does σrank ≈ 0.34 hold once diffusion-based and zero-shot tabular foundation models — with their large hyperparameter spaces — join the pool?
2Which dataset properties (contamination rate, dimensionality, categorical fraction, cluster structure) most strongly predict the winner — and can they be used to construct favorable settings?
3How does σrank change under per-dataset aggregation (average rank / critical-difference diagrams) vs. the current “best overall” rule behind the “8 settings suffice” claim?
4Would a proper multiple-comparison correction (Wilcoxon + Holm, or Bayesian hierarchical ranking) change each algorithm's win share?
5Can σrank be re-cast as a normalized instability score that respects rank bounds, replacing the heuristic Gaussian reading?
6Is the ≥200-dataset threshold stable across domains (fraud, intrusion detection, predictive maintenance), or does it scale with domain heterogeneity?
