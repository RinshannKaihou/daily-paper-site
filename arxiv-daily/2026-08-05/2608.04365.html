Why this matters

Cryptographically raises the cost of gaming fairness audits — directly relevant to EU AI Act / DSA regulatory compliance and trustworthy ML deployment.

Critical edge

All results are simulated on a fixed split with a benign baseline — no adaptive adversary tested, and the protocol fails on imbalanced groups (HateDay: <3% detection at k=50).

01

How the oblivious audit works

The paper targets the black-box demographic-parity audit game: an auditor estimates the parity gap d_S on an audit set S and passes the provider iff |d_S| ≤ ε. Proposition 1 shows this is brittle — a provider who knows S needs as few as 2 targeted flips to hide a 0.01 gap on a balanced n=400 set.

R²esPIR closes the leak by forcing the provider to label a much larger candidate set C and commit to it cryptographically, while the auditor secretly samples S ⊂ C via PIR. The provider never learns which indices were queried, so it cannot tailor responses to the known audit set.

No change to the model, its training, or its inference pipeline — the PIR layer treats the fairness computation as a modular black box.
1

Agree on candidate set C

Provider and auditor jointly fix a large, representative C that covers the protected groups.

2

Provider labels & commits via VeriSimplePIR

Provider labels all of C into database D and posts a verifiability proof π, so labels cannot be silently changed mid-audit.

3

Auditor retrieves h(S) through PIR

Uniformly samples S ⊂ C, reads h(S) via PIR (provider learns nothing), and inserts k canary queries with known labels to detect tampering.

03

Strengths & limitations

Strengths pro

  • Modular crypto foundation. Built on VeriSimplePIR, so the trust assumption is well-understood and independently auditable.
  • Metric-agnostic. Same flip-count lower bounds extend to equal opportunity, equalized odds, multi-class attributes, and (in expectation) regression.
  • Negligible overhead. No retraining, no model modification; sub-second to few-second PIR cost for GiB-scale databases.
  • Honest framing. Explicitly states PIR "hides the audit set but does not prevent all forms of manipulation," and delineates degradation regimes.
  • Reproducible. Public oblivious_audit code, public datasets (CCD, COMPAS, HateDay), specified model class & hyperparameter protocol.

Limitations con

  • Imbalanced groups break detectability. HateDay's 8.3% minority yields <3% detection at k=50; the recommended stratified-sampling fix is proposed but not validated.
  • Single |C| per dataset. The central N_min ≫ n_min scaling claim is never stress-tested by varying candidate-set size.
  • No adaptive adversary. Experiments simulate the minimum-flip manipulation, not a smarter adversary that exploits candidate-set or canary knowledge.
  • Fixed ε = |d_{C,true}|/2; the problematic regime where ε < γ is acknowledged but not charted.
  • Side-channel leakage unaddressed. PIR hides indices, but request volume / timing / IP could re-identify audit activity — not discussed.
  • Candidate-set agreement hand-waved. No mechanism for negotiating C when the provider controls the operational data.
04

Open questions

Q1

Does stratified sampling of S recover detectability on HateDay-like imbalanced settings?

Q2

Can the protocol defend against an adaptive adversary using timing / volume / IP side channels to re-identify audit queries?

Q3

Does empirical flip-count scaling match the N_min prediction of Theorem 1 as |C| grows at fixed |S|?

Q4

Can R²esPIR combine with iterative candidate-set release without leaking information that breaks non-adaptivity?

Q5

How do guarantees degrade for equalized odds / calibration, where one flip can move multiple rates at once?

Q6

Is there a principled mechanism to negotiate C when the provider holds most of the operational data?
