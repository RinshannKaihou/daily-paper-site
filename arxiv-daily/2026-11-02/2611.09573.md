> **一句话总结：** 作者在单张 16 GB 消费级 GPU 上，用 2024 年 52 个初始场把 Aurora、Pangu-Weather、FourCastNet、SFNO、FastNet 五个 AI 天气模型的网络权重与计算转为 fp16（物理状态、归一化与反归一化仍留 fp32），全球 RMSE 相对 fp32 最多只变 1.4%（五个模型中四个 ≤0.5%，折合 8 天以内不到 1 小时、10 天最多 2 小时时效损失），而 10 天预报提速 1.3–3.5 倍、能耗降低 1.4–3.7 倍；同时证明 bf16 速度相同但精度更差、简单 8-bit 量化代价大得多。

> **TL;DR:** Running five released AI weather models (Aurora 1.5, Pangu-Weather, FourCastNet, SFNO, FastNet) with their networks in fp16 but the physical state, normalisation and denormalisation in fp32 changes global RMSE against ERA5 by at most +1.4% (≤0.5% for four of five) over 52 initial dates from 2024 — less than one hour of lead-time equivalent up to day 8 and at most 2 h at day 10 — while making a 10-day forecast 1.3–3.5× faster (e.g. FastNet 0.294 s → 0.105 s per step, 2.8×; Aurora 5.378 s → 1.517 s, 3.5×) and 1.4–3.7× more energy-efficient. Converting the whole model in one line fails: in fp16 mean sea-level pressure (max 1.05e5 Pa) overflows to infinity for 100% of values, and in bf16 rounding the state costs up to +167% RMSE for Aurora. With the state kept in fp32, bf16 is as fast as fp16 but worse for every model (FourCastNet bf16 2t day 10: +271% vs fp16 −0.6%), and int8 weight quantisation costs 1–16% RMSE at day 1 — 10–100× the fp16 change.

## 核心贡献

- **指出"一行代码转半精度"是个陷阱**：整模型转换会把物理状态也舍入到 16 位。在 fp16 下，海平面气压（最大 1.05×10⁵ Pa）100% 的数值超出 65,504 上界变成 inf，400 hPa 以上位势全部溢出；在 bf16 下不溢出但精度太粗，把 ERA5 初始场舍入到 bf16 会使 2t/t850 变化 0.57 K、msl 变化 148 Pa、z500 变化 74 m² s⁻²，与模式第 1 天的 fp32 误差同量级。Aurora 整模型转 fp16 后第一个 6 小时步起全部输出非有限值；转 bf16 则 RMSE 最高上升 167%。
- **给出可行配方并量化其代价**：物理状态、归一化/反归一化（含归一化统计量）留 fp32，只有网络转 fp16，则 5 个模型全球 RMSE 变化 ≤1.4%（4 个 ≤0.5%），折合时效损失 8 天内 <1 小时、10 天最多 1.7 小时（FastNet 10 m 风速）。
- **系统刻画"差异出现在哪里"**：按纬度带、10°×10° 网格、纬向功率谱、4 个具名风暴（Isha、Kathleen、Beryl、Milton）以及 Aurora 集合（13 日期 × 8 成员）检验，除 FourCastNet 的热带 z500 惩罚（第 10 天 +12%）外无系统性退化；集合 CRPS 变化 ≤0.8%、spread 变化 <0.5%、离散度校准不变。
- **证明 fp16 的数值范围不是实际风险**：用 forward hook 记录每个模块返回张量的最大有限幅值，52 个日期内无任何模块产生 inf/NaN；最大幅值分别为 Pangu 120、SFNO 208、FastNet 225、FourCastNet 559、Aurora 1,733，距 65,504 上界留有 544/315/291/117/38 倍余量。
- **检验"首尾层/归一化层要保高精度"这类经验法则是否迁移**：只部分成立。Pangu-Weather 与 Aurora 确实由首尾组件主导（patch embedding、stage 0、patch recovery 各占 43–69%；Aurora 的 decoder 占 z500 差异 72%），FastNet 由 decoder 独占 89%，FourCastNet 敏感性随深度单调下降（57%→14%），而 SFNO 每个组件都差不多（23–39%）——没有任何一类层单独敏感。
- **横向比较 bf16 与 8-bit**：在状态留 fp32 的前提下 bf16 与 fp16 同速（时间/显存/能耗差 ≤2%/1%/3.4%），但每个模型都更差（Aurora 2t 第 5 天 +25.2% vs fp16 +0.2%）；简单 int8/fp8 权重量化第 1 天 z500/2t RMSE 上升 1–16%（最温和配置）到 7–82%，是 fp16 的 10–100 倍。

## Key Contributions

- **Identifies the failure mode of naive whole-model conversion.** Because Aurora and Pangu-Weather convert their inputs before normalising, a one-line `model.to(torch.float16)` rounds the physical state in physical units: all msl values (max 1.05e5 Pa) exceed fp16's 65,504 ceiling and become infinite, as do 97% of surface pressure and all geopotential above 400 hPa. In bf16 nothing overflows but the rounding error (0.57 K for 2t, 148 Pa for msl, 74 m² s⁻² for z500) is comparable to day-1 forecast error and recurs at every step, raising Aurora's RMSE by up to 167%.
- **Establishes a cheap, portable recipe: network in fp16, state + normalisation + denormalisation in fp32.** Cost is one fp32 copy of the state (0.7 GB, 6% of Aurora's fp16 peak memory) plus a few element-wise ops; benefit is ≤1.4% global RMSE change and 1.3–3.5× speed-up, 1.4–3.7× energy saving.
- **Converts precision loss into an interpretable unit (equivalent lead time)** rather than a percentage, finding <1 h lost up to day 8 for every model and variable, and at most 1.7 h (FastNet, 10 m wind) at day 10.
- **Separates "the forecast changed" from "the forecast got worse."** The fp16 forecasts drift from fp32 by up to 40% of the fp32 error at day 10 (FourCastNet 21–40%, FastNet 19–32%, Aurora 13–26%) yet keep the same RMSE — behaving like two equally skilful ensemble members rather than a forecast and its degraded copy, which requires the departure to be weakly negatively correlated with error (r ≈ −0.2 at 40% departure).
- **Locates precision sensitivity architecturally with a one-at-a-time component ablation** (13 dates, 24 h, departure relative to the whole-network fp16 departure), and shows the components add roughly in quadrature (√ΣD_c² ≈ 0.9–1.5), i.e. like independent noise sources.
- **Rules out fp16 range as a practical risk with direct instrumentation**, and shows bf16's wider range buys nothing once the state is in fp32 (no overflow, ≥38× margin), while costing accuracy.
- **Benchmarks the next step down (8-bit) honestly**, including a mechanistic diagnosis of FastNet's int8 collapse: a single input of its grid-to-mesh edge embedder carries weights ~75× larger than typical, so per-output-channel int8 scaling rounds about a third of that layer's weights to zero.

## Methodology

**Models (5, three architecture families, all public fp32 checkpoints, all runnable in PyTorch on one 16 GB GPU):** FastNet v1.1 (graph network, O96 ~1° grid, 62.2M params, 6 h step), FourCastNet v1 (adaptive Fourier neural operator on a flat lat–lon grid, 0.25°, 75.3M, 6 h), Pangu-Weather 24 h model (3D transformer, 0.25°, 276.7M, 24 h step, run through a third-party PyTorch reimplementation with official weights because the release is ONNX-only), SFNO v0.1.0 (spherical Fourier operator, 0.25°, 289.4M, 6 h), Aurora 1.5 (3D Swin transformer, 0.25°, 1.26B, 6 h, both deterministic and ensemble checkpoints). AIFS and GraphCast were excluded for concrete reasons: AIFS needs six ocean-wave inputs ERA5 does not archive, GraphCast's official weights are JAX-only.

**Precision protocol.** The fp32 reference is each model's default PyTorch execution (including PyTorch's default TF32 for convolutions, deliberately retained). In the 16-bit runs every weight and every computation *inside the network* is 16-bit; the state, its normalisation and denormalisation (including normalisation statistics) stay fp32, normalised fields are rounded on entry to the network and outputs converted back to fp32 before denormalisation. Per-model adaptations were needed: normalisation moved out of Pangu-Weather's first layer (verified to reproduce the original bit-for-bit with fp32 weights), FastNet's normalised state kept in fp32 between steps, SFNO's solar zenith angle and static fields added in fp32. This is *persistent* conversion (fp32 weights discarded), the harsher option than automatic mixed precision — and the only one that fits: Aurora 1.5 in its default AMP mode runs out of memory on 16 GB in the first step, whereas persistent fp16 peaks at 11.6 GB. FourCastNet's complex Fourier filters are stored as separate real/imaginary real tensors so they can be converted; SFNO's are applied the same way. A few ops stay fp32 on 16-bit inputs (FourCastNet's FFTs, SFNO's spherical harmonic transforms).

**Verification.** ERA5 on its 0.25° regular grid (721×1440); FastNet initialised by linear interpolation to O96 and its forecasts interpolated back via Delaunay triangulation (≈1.6% of points, a meridian gap and polar rows, unscored). Variables: 2t, msl, ws10, z500, t850 at daily lead times 1–10, cosine-latitude-weighted RMSE as the primary score, plus bias and MAE. Initial dates: 52, stratified random sampling of 2024 — one date per calendar week, 00/06/12/18 UTC used 13 times each — chosen because 2024 lies after the documented training periods of all models and is Aurora's held-out test year.

**Statistics.** Paired design: fp32 and fp16 forecasts share initial conditions, so the per-date RMSE difference isolates precision from synoptic variability; reported with 95% CIs and two-sided paired t-tests, plus a minimum-detectable-effect calculation (median 0.03–0.33% of fp32 RMSE globally, 0.08–1.1% regionally). No multiple-testing correction across 50 variable×lead-time combinations, stated explicitly, with isolated location results flagged as suspect. Practical significance expressed as equivalent lead time τ ≈ Δ/(dRMSE/dt).

**Diagnostics.** (i) 10°×10° error cells → exact recomputation of RMSE/bias/departure over any union (latitude bands, maps); (ii) zonal power spectra per 10° band up to each model's resolved scale (wavenumber 45 FourCastNet, 179 Aurora/Pangu, 200 FastNet, 239 SFNO); (iii) four named 2024 storms tracked by 700 km (extratropical) / 400 km (tropical) MSLP minimum, comparing track, central pressure and max 10 m wind; (iv) Aurora ensemble, 13 dates × 8 members with seeds 0–7 in both precisions so member *m* is the same stochastic draw, scored by fair CRPS (Ferro 2014), spread-vs-error, and outside-range fraction (target 2/(M+1)); (v) forward hooks on every module to record max finite magnitude, count of inf/NaN, and count of subnormal-range values; (vi) one-at-a-time component ablation, plus layer-type ablations (all normalisation / attention / spectral filters / MLPs in fp32), channel-outlier analysis, and per-module relative output difference; (vii) cost measured on an RTX 5080 16 GB with ≥60 s and ≥8 timed steps, first step excluded, board power sampled every 50 ms; (viii) 8-bit simulation inside the fp16 network (int8 symmetric 127 levels, fp8 e4m3, per-output-channel or per-token/per-tensor scales), explicitly not measuring 8-bit speed or memory.

## Results & Highlights

- **Global RMSE change with the network in fp16 (state in fp32), 52 dates of 2024, ERA5 truth:** FastNet −0.5% to +0.25%; Pangu-Weather up to +0.2% (significant in 45 of 50 variable×lead-time combinations); FourCastNet −0.7% to +1.4% (worst: z500 at day 1); SFNO −0.03% to +0.07% (significant in 32/50 despite the tiny size); Aurora −0.16% to +0.48%. The worst case anywhere is FourCastNet's +1.4%, and four of five models stay within ~0.5% — versus the same models' fp32 day-1 errors of 0.70–1.03 K for 2t, which the bf16 state rounding alone (0.57 K) already rivals.
- **Equivalent lead-time loss (the paper's practical yardstick):** under 1 hour for every model and variable up to day 8, and at most 1.7 h (FastNet, 10 m wind) and 1.5 h (Aurora, 10 m wind) at day 10; SFNO never loses more than 0.3 h. For scale, operational global NWP has gained roughly a day of medium-range lead time per decade, i.e. a few hours per year — so this is well below the level at which progress is measured.
- **Cost on one RTX 5080 (fp32 → fp16):** FastNet 0.294 → 0.105 s/step (2.8×), 12 → 4 s per 10-day forecast, 1.07 → 0.29 Wh; Pangu-Weather 0.785 → 0.371 s/step (2.1×), 0.69 → 0.28 Wh; FourCastNet 0.115 → 0.057 s/step (2.0×), 0.38 → 0.14 Wh; SFNO 0.531 → 0.400 s/step (only 1.3×), 1.53 → 1.07 Wh; Aurora 5.378 → 1.517 s/step (3.5×), 215 → 61 s per 10-day forecast, 18.27 → 5.50 Wh. Energy falls 2.5–3.7× for the four smaller models and 1.4× for SFNO; peak memory roughly halves for Pangu-Weather (9.5 → 5.2 GB) and FastNet (2.2 → 1.2 GB), falls 29% for FourCastNet, a fifth for Aurora and a sixth for SFNO (15.4 → 12.9 GB).
- **Naive whole-model conversion, quantified:** fp16 Aurora returns no finite value from the first 6-hour step on (three dates tested). bf16 whole-model Aurora stays finite but RMSE rises by up to 167%, against at most 25% with the network in bf16 and the state in fp32. Rounding an ERA5 state to fp16 in physical units is impossible for msl (100% of values > 65,504), sp (97%) and z400/z300/z50 (81–100%); after normalisation the same rounding error shrinks 16–80× (0.026 K for 2t, 1.9 Pa for msl, 4.6 m² s⁻² for z500 in bf16).
- **Where fp16 hurts: FourCastNet's tropics.** Its near-zero global change hides opposite regional signs: between 20°S and 20°N its fp16 z500 RMSE is +2.8% at day 1 and +12% at day 10, with tropical 10° cells 11% worse on the median and up to 18%, while at high latitudes fp16 is up to 2% *more* accurate; over South Asia the RMSE rises up to +5% (z500 day 5 +4.2%). Absolute tropical z500 error is small (fp32 RMSE 44 and 155 m² s⁻² at days 1 and 10 vs 66 and 907 globally), so the global score barely moves. Zonal spectra confirm the mechanism: fp16 adds variance from wavenumber ~25 to FourCastNet's limit of 45 (RMS amplitude 0.73 → 0.81 gpm vs 1.12 in ERA5), consistent with flat-spectrum rounding noise.
- **Range headroom:** no module of any model returned a non-finite value over 52 dates; largest magnitudes 120 (Pangu-Weather), 208 (SFNO), 225 (FastNet), 559 (FourCastNet), 1,733 (Aurora) — 544, 315, 291, 117 and 38 times below 65,504. FourCastNet's largest values grow from 116 to 559 after day 6 in its input layer, plausibly because its normalised state drifts outside the training range late in the forecast.
- **Ensemble (Aurora, 13 dates × 8 members):** global CRPS changes at most +0.8% (z500, day 1) and mostly 0.0–0.3%; spread changes <0.5%; the outside-range fraction stays 0.21–0.38 within ±0.01 of fp32, i.e. calibration is untouched. Each fp16 member departs from its same-seed fp32 twin by 3–11% of the ensemble spread up to day 5 and 31–43% at day 10 — a perturbation much smaller than the spread the ensemble is built to sample. The ensemble's CRPS is 0.56–0.76× the MAE of the deterministic checkpoint on the same dates, and fp16 preserves that advantage.
- **Storms:** Pangu-Weather's fp16 tracks match fp32 to within 1 km; Aurora and SFNO mean position errors differ by ≤3 km; FastNet and FourCastNet change by −29 to +34 km per storm (largest: FastNet's Milton, 163 → 197 km, diverging after landfall). Mean central-pressure error changes by ≤0.7 hPa everywhere — against inter-model position errors of 60–430 km. At London through Storms Isha and Jocelyn, fp16 and fp32 differ by ≤0.5 hPa / 0.3 m s⁻¹ / 0.3 K up to day 5 (≤0.15 hPa / 0.03 m s⁻¹ / 0.05 K for Pangu-Weather, SFNO and Aurora) against fp32 errors of 0.7–2.3 hPa / 0.3–0.8 m s⁻¹ / 0.4–1.0 K.
- **Component sensitivity:** Pangu-Weather's patch embedding, first stage and patch recovery each produce 43–69% of the whole-network fp16 departure, middle stages 11–22%; Aurora's decoder 72% (z500) and first backbone stage 73% (2t); FastNet's decoder alone 89% while its 16 processor rounds contribute ≤8% each; FourCastNet falls from 57% (patch embedding) to 14% (output layer); SFNO is flat at 23–39% per component. Keeping all normalisation, attention or spectral-filter layers in fp32 changes the departure by ≤6% for four models (≤16% for some variables) and 5–10% for FastNet's normalisation — i.e. the standard rules of thumb do not buy much.
- **bf16 vs fp16 (state fp32 both):** same speed/memory/energy (≤2%, ≤1%, ≤3.4% apart), but bf16 is significantly worse in 10–20 of 20 variable×lead-time combinations per model and significantly better in only 2 of 100 overall. Concretely, Aurora 2t day 5: bf16 +25.2% vs fp16 +0.2%; FourCastNet 2t day 10: bf16 +271.4% vs fp16 −0.6%, with 2t error more than doubling fp32 on 21 of 52 dates; FastNet z500 day 5: bf16 +3.0% vs fp16 +0.1%.
- **8-bit is not a drop-in (13 dates, simulated):** int8 weights — the mildest setting — raise day-1 z500/2t RMSE by 1–16% for Aurora, Pangu-Weather, FourCastNet and SFNO (10–100× the fp16 change) and up to +55% (FourCastNet 2t) at day 10; fp8 weights cost 7–82% at day 1; also quantising layer inputs costs 8–430% at day 1 and destabilises FourCastNet by day 10 (z500 ×19, 2t ×125). FastNet is the outlier where int8 weights more than double z500 error (+171% at day 1) while fp8 does less damage (+82.4%) — traced to one encoder input whose weights are ~75× larger than typical, causing about a third of that layer's weights to round to zero under per-output-channel scaling.

## Authors & Credibility

Single-author paper by Stefan Siegert, Department of Mathematics and Statistics, University of Exeter, explicitly marked "has not yet been peer-reviewed" on the title page. Siegert is a well-established researcher in statistical post-processing and ensemble forecast verification (his earlier work includes the "fair" Brier/CRPSS decomposition literature and ensemble calibration diagnostics; the paper itself cites Siegert et al. 2011 for the outside-range calibration statistic). That background shows: the verification protocol is the paper's strongest asset — paired fp32/fp16 forecasts on identical initial conditions, cosine-latitude weighting, fair CRPS for the ensemble, minimum-detectable-effect reporting, and an explicit refusal to over-read statistical significance. The paper is unusually transparent about method: a public code repository (`sieste/ai-nwp-fp16`, Zenodo-archived) with the exact software environments, a reproduction check for the stored results, and all figure/table data archived separately at Zenodo. It also discloses generative-AI assistance (Claude Sonnet/Opus 5.5) for coding, experiments and drafting, with the author taking responsibility. Licence handling for each checkpoint is documented in unusual detail, including the observation that Pangu-Weather's re-hosted weights are Apache-2.0-tagged but derived from CC BY-NC-SA originals and therefore treated as non-commercial.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty is modest but the gap is real.** This is an engineering-characterisation paper, not a methods paper: the recipe (fp16 compute, fp32 master state and normalisation) is exactly what mixed-precision training has prescribed since Micikevicius et al. 2018, and the paper says so. Its contribution is that nobody had measured what reduced precision costs *forecast skill* in AI weather models — the paper documents that Aurora, GraphCast, AIFS and FourCastNet all ship 16-bit inference paths while "none of these sources reports how much forecast skill reduced precision costs." Filling that gap for five models with a paired design is worthwhile and, for a field that now runs AIFS operationally, timely.

**The experimental design is genuinely careful, and the honesty is above average.** Three things stand out. First, the paired design plus the minimum-detectable-effect table (median 0.03% of RMSE for SFNO, 0.33% for FastNet) pre-empts the obvious misreading of "significant in 45 of 50 combinations": the paper states plainly that "a significant result here shows that fp16 changes the forecast, not that the change is important," and separately converts changes into lead-time equivalents. Second, the distinction between departure and degradation is handled properly rather than rhetorically — the observation that fp16 forecasts drift up to 40% of the error at day 10 while keeping the same RMSE is explained with the correlation condition (r ≈ −half the relative departure), which is the right way to say "these behave like two ensemble members." Third, the Aurora footnote (Table 6) admits that its 3.5× speed-up is partly an artefact of fp32 requiring weight streaming from CPU, that its memory saving is therefore understated and its energy comparison possibly affected. Many papers would have quietly reported 3.5× as the headline.

**Weak points, concretely.** (1) *The baseline for "no loss of skill" is the model's own fp32 run, not the state of the art.* Everything is verified against ERA5, not observations, and ERA5 is also the training target for all five models; the paper acknowledges this, but it means "negligible loss of skill" is measured against a reference that is itself optimistic, particularly for extremes. (2) *One year, one season cycle, one GPU.* 52 dates spread over 2024 gives 13 dates per synoptic season and cannot say anything about, e.g., a strongly negative-NAO winter or a record hurricane season; the four named storms are four cases and the paper says so, but the storm analysis is nonetheless the weakest-evidence section — its conclusion ("fp16 does not visibly degrade these storms") rests on track and central pressure, not on wind structure, precipitation or extremes, which are precisely where AI weather models are known to be weak (the paper cites Rasp et al. 2024 and Ben Bouallègue et al. 2024 on exactly this, then does not test it). (3) *The sensitivity experiments are 24-hour and 13-date.* They rank components but cannot show how a perturbation introduced in, say, FastNet's decoder propagates to day-10 skill; the one-at-a-time design also cannot detect interactions, which the author concedes and partially addresses via the quadrature check (√ΣD_c² ≈ 0.9–1.5) — an argument that is suggestive rather than conclusive, since SFNO's separate-conversion departure is only 48% of its whole-network departure for z500, showing the decomposition does break down for at least one model. (4) *The 8-bit comparison is deliberately naive and the paper knows it.* Round-to-nearest with one scale per output channel, no calibration, no outlier handling, no quantisation-aware fine-tuning — this is a strawman relative to the LLM quantisation literature it cites (Dettmers et al. 2022; Xiao et al. 2022; Lin et al. 2023), and the FastNet diagnosis (one input with ~75× larger weights) is essentially an argument that the failure is fixable by per-input or block scaling. The conclusion "simple 8-bit quantisation is not a substitute" is defensible as stated; it would not survive being paraphrased as "8-bit does not work for weather models." (5) *Some numbers need care.* Pangu-Weather is run through a third-party PyTorch reimplementation with official ONNX-derived weights, and while the paper verifies bit-identical reproduction with fp32 weights after moving normalisation, the reimplementation is not the reference implementation. FastNet is scored on 98.4% of the grid after Delaunay interpolation, and its fp32 skill is depressed at day 1 by resolution mismatch (O96 native, verified at 0.25°) — which the paper attributes plausibly but does not test, and which matters because FastNet is one of the two models with the largest fp16 departure. Aurora's fp32 reference uses TF32 for convolutions (PyTorch default), so the "fp32" baseline is not strictly fp32; the paper discloses this, but it slightly narrows the measured fp16 benefit for the conv-containing models. (6) *Generality.* Five models, all chosen because they fit in 16 GB and run in PyTorch; GraphCast (JAX) and AIFS (missing ocean-wave inputs in ERA5) are excluded for defensible but consequential reasons, and the discussion of TPUs — where bf16 is native and the paper's recommendation inverts — is one paragraph. The claim "fp16 should be the default for research use on a single GPU" is well supported; broader claims about operational inference are not made, which is to the author's credit.

**Red flags: few.** No cherry-picked benchmark set (all five variables, all 10 lead times, all 52 dates reported); no hidden test-set leakage (2024 is out-of-sample for all models with documented training periods, and the SFNO training window is reconstructed from two secondary sources with the reasoning shown); no overclaiming in the abstract relative to the results. The main structural weakness is that a single-author, non-peer-reviewed characterisation study cannot independently validate its own hardware measurements — but the code, environments and archived results make that reproducible by others, which is the right substitute.

## Strengths

- **A genuinely useful, immediately actionable result.** The recipe is one code change plus one caveat (keep the state in fp32), and it makes a 1.26B-parameter model fit and run 3.5× faster on a $1,000-class 16 GB consumer GPU. For academic groups priced out of data-centre hardware, this is the difference between running 52 initial dates and running 13.
- **Exceptional statistical hygiene for a systems paper.** Paired forecasts, confidence intervals, paired t-tests, minimum detectable effect, explicit no-multiple-testing-correction disclaimer, and a separate practical-significance metric (equivalent lead time). Very few ML-systems papers do any of this.
- **The "state must stay in fp32" finding is a real trap, documented with mechanism.** The paper shows *why* it fails per model (Aurora/Pangu convert before normalising; FourCastNet/FastNet normalise outside; SFNO loses the imaginary parts of its complex filters on whole-model conversion — a silent failure mode that would be maddening to debug).
- **Honest cost accounting.** Energy is measured (board power × duration, 50 ms sampling), not estimated; the Aurora streaming artefact is disclosed; the SFNO 1.3× speed-up is explained (fp32 spherical harmonic transforms, complex filters as four real products) and the author admits not having profiled it.
- **Breadth of diagnostics without bloat.** Latitude bands, 10° maps, zonal spectra, four storms, an 8-member ensemble, module-level hooks, and component ablations — each answering a specific objection a reviewer would raise, and each reported with its own caveat.
- **Reproducibility infrastructure.** Public repo with pinned environments, a reproduction check, Zenodo-archived scores, and per-checkpoint licence notes.

## Limitations

- **Single year (2024), single verification dataset (ERA5), five variables, daily lead times to 10 days.** No season-stratified analysis, no extremes-specific metric (the paper measures RMSE, bias, MAE, CRPS — not, e.g., tail-weighted scores or precipitation), and ERA5 is the training target for all five models.
- **Five models chosen by a hardware constraint.** All fit in 16 GB and run in PyTorch; GraphCast and AIFS are excluded. Sensitivity to fp16 is found to follow neither size nor skill, so extrapolation to other architectures is explicitly not supported.
- **Sensitivity experiments are short-horizon (24 h) and use 13 dates.** They rank components but do not connect a component's rounding to day-10 skill; one-at-a-time designs cannot detect interactions, and the quadrature check does not hold cleanly for SFNO (48% for z500).
- **Hardware and software specificity.** One RTX 5080, one PyTorch version; speed-ups depend on kernel selection and would not transfer to CPUs (no fast fp16) or to TPUs (bf16 native, where the recommendation inverts).
- **8-bit results are accuracy-only.** Speed and memory of 8-bit inference are explicitly not measured, and the quantisation scheme is the simplest possible (no calibration, no outlier treatment, no QAT), so the negative result is about naive 8-bit, not about 8-bit in general.
- **Storm analysis is four cases.** Track and central pressure only; wind structure, precipitation and extremes are untested, and the paper is careful to say the cases show absence of visible degradation, not absence of degradation.
- **Aurora's fp32 baseline is confounded** by weight streaming and by TF32 convolutions; its 3.5× and its energy ratio are the least clean numbers in Table 6, as the footnote concedes.

## Relevance

与"AI 天气/气候模型的推理效率与业务化部署"这一方向高度相关：如果你关心在单卡（尤其消费级 16 GB）上跑 AI 天气模式、做多初始场/集合/敏感性实验，这篇给出了可直接照做的半精度配方（网络 fp16、状态与归一化留 fp32）以及"精度换成本"的定量账本——1.4% RMSE 换 1.3–3.5 倍加速和 1.4–3.7 倍节能。对做模型压缩/量化的人来说，它还是一份难得的负面结果清单：bf16 在状态已留 fp32 时毫无优势，朴素 8-bit 会掉一到几个数量级的技巧，而"首尾层保高精度"的经验法则在天气模式上只部分成立。

## Questions for Further Exploration

1. Does the fp16 penalty stay ≤0.5% in an extreme-season sample (e.g. winter 2023/24 North Atlantic, or the 2024 Atlantic hurricane season as a whole rather than four storms), and does it grow for tail metrics (99th-percentile wind speed, 24 h precipitation extremes) that RMSE and CRPS dilute?
2. The paper shows fp16 departure reaches 40% of the error at day 10 while RMSE is unchanged. What happens beyond day 10 — in a 15-day or monthly-mean configuration, does the drift become a systematic bias rather than a zero-mean perturbation?
3. Why is FourCastNet the outlier (tropical z500 +12% at day 10)? The flat-grid adaptive Fourier filter is the natural suspect, but a controlled test — SFNO with its spherical transform replaced by a flat FFT, or FourCastNet with per-latitude scaling — would settle whether the cause is the transform's geometry or the specific filter implementation in 16 bits.
4. Would block-scaled formats (MXFP8/MXFP4, or per-input-scale int8) close the FastNet gap that the paper diagnoses as a single 75× outlier input? The hook-based paired design described here could answer this directly and cheaply.
5. Can the "keep the sensitive component in fp32" result be turned into an automatic mixed-precision *policy* — e.g. fp16 for everything except the encoder/decoder of Pangu-Weather and Aurora, decoder of FastNet — and does that recover the residual 0.5% at no measurable cost in speed?
6. How do these findings transfer to the operational-scale members of the family (AIFS single/ensemble, GraphCast, Aurora at full resolution) where the model does not fit in 16 GB even in fp16, and where the binding constraint is intermediate activations rather than weights?
