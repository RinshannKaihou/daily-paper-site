> **一句话总结：** 作者推导出 KL 正则化 pass@k / max@k 目标的闭式最优策略，证明 @k 的本质是"按题目难度自适应地重新分配优化压力"（对已会的简单题加大有效正则、对难题加大奖励权重），并据此提出 KAPPO——只需一次性参考校准，就能把任意现成 @1 优化器（DPO、A⋆PO、GRPO）改造成 @k 优化器；在 MATH Level 5 上 @2-A⋆PO 用 12.8 万条 rollout（PKPO 的 50%）达到 pass@1 65.34%，仅比 PKPO 的 66.08% 低 0.74 个点。

> **TL;DR:** This paper derives closed-form optimal policies for KL-regularized pass@k and max@k, showing that @k optimization is exactly equivalent to ordinary @1 optimization with a prompt-adaptive KL coefficient β_eff(x) = β/(k(1−p*(x))^{k−1}) (binary) or a prompt-adaptive reward transform (continuous). That equivalence yields KAPPO, a wrapper that turns any @1 optimizer into an @k optimizer using a one-time bank of M=30 reference completions per prompt. On MATH Level 5, @2-A⋆PO reaches 65.34% pass@1 on OLMo-3-7B vs 66.08% for PKPO (the dedicated multi-completion pass@k method) while generating 128k instead of 256k on-policy completions (−50%, and −75% at k=4); on Qwen3-4B the pass@1 gap to PKPO widens to −2.61 pts (57.27 vs 59.88), so "matching PKPO" holds at the matched sampling budget but not uniformly. The main empirical claims about reduced sharpening on AIME 2026 and LiveCodeBench v6 are shown only as figures, with no tabulated numbers.

## 核心贡献

- **给出 pass@k 的闭式最优策略。** 对二值奖励，KL 正则化 pass@k 的最优策略仍是"参考策略 × 指数倾斜"形式，但常数 β 被换成了随 prompt 变化的有效正则强度 β_eff(x) = β / (k(1−p*(x))^{k−1})，其中 p*(x) 由一条一维不动点方程唯一确定（且必有 p* > p_ref）。
- **给出 max@k 的闭式最优策略**，形式为对参考奖励 CDF 的 (k−1) 次幂做积分后的指数倾斜；并证明连续奖励下"自适应正则"只是解释性工具而非严格等价（因为 β_eff 还依赖具体 completion 的奖励值），真正的目标级等价要通过奖励变换实现。
- **证明了 @k 与 @1 的等价性（本文最有用的一条）。** pass@k 可以完全等价地写成"普通 @1 目标 + prompt 自适应正则 β_eff(x)"或"普通 @1 目标 + prompt 自适应奖励缩放 k(1−p*)^{k−1}·R"。这解释了 @k 为什么能抑制 sharpening：它在简单题上施加更强的有效正则，把优化压力推向难题——且这个解释与具体优化器无关。
- **提出 KAPPO 框架**，把上述等价性工程化：一次性"参考校准"（每个 prompt 采样 M 条参考回答，算出 p_ref / F_ref，解出 β_eff 或构造奖励变换查表），之后训练过程完全不变。作者实例化了三个版本：@k-DPO（正则侧）、@k-A⋆PO（奖励侧，单条在线 completion）、@k-GRPO（奖励侧，无 KL 项）。
- **实证：单 completion 训练即可获得 @k 压力。** @k-A⋆PO 只用每条 prompt 一条在线 rollout，就在 MATH Level 5 上追平了需要 n=k 条 rollout 的 PKPO，rollout 成本降低 50%（k=2）到 75%（k=4）。

## Key Contributions

- **Closed-form optimal policy for pass@k.** For binary rewards the KL-regularized pass@k optimum keeps the exponential-tilt form π*(y|x) ∝ π_ref(y|x)·exp(R(y,x)/β_eff(x)) but replaces the global β with the prompt-dependent β_eff(x) = β/(k(1−p*(x))^{k−1}); p*(x) is pinned down by the scalar fixed point k(1−p*)^{k−1} = β·log[p*(1−p_ref)/(p_ref(1−p*))], which is shown to have a unique root in (0,1) via strict monotonicity of the associated function Φ_q.
- **Closed-form optimal policy for max@k**, plus the honest observation that the continuous-reward case does *not* admit a clean regularization-side equivalence: β_eff becomes β_eff(r,x) = β/(r∫F_{π*}^{k−1}), which depends on the completion's own reward, so plugging it into an @1 objective would yield a different optimum.
- **An optimizer-independent explanation of why @k mitigates sharpening.** The mechanism is a difficulty-dependent redistribution of optimization pressure: easy prompts (high p_ref) get large β_eff, i.e. strong regularization and small reward multipliers; hard prompts get weak regularization and large multipliers. This is the paper's conceptual core and it is genuinely explanatory rather than descriptive.
- **KAPPO**, a drop-in wrapper that converts any @1 policy-optimization method into an @k method after a one-time reference calibration, with no change to the base algorithm and no online k-completion groups. Instantiated as @k-DPO, @k-A⋆PO, @k-GRPO.
- **An explicit, computable max@k solution for win-rate rewards.** By replacing the raw reward with the reference quantile F_ref(R(y,x)|x), the implicit CDF dependence disappears and the transformed reward becomes S_x^q(r) = β·log[C + Ψ^{-1}(F_ref(r|x))^k/β] with Ψ(z)=∫_0^z du/(C+u^k/β) and C set by Ψ(1)=1 — precomputable as a lookup table over the M+1 empirical quantiles.
- **A rigorous finite-bank error analysis** (Appendix K) with DKW-based uniform consistency bounds, O(M^{-1/2}) noise and O(M^{-1}) bias, and explicit leading constants — including a candid demonstration that the win-rate transform is badly behaved in the upper tail.

## Methodology

**Setup and notation.** Prompts x ~ D, completions y ~ π(·|x), reward R(y,x), reference policy π_ref, KL coefficient β. The @k objective is J_k(π) = E_x[V_k(π;x) − β·KL(π(·|x)‖π_ref(·|x))], where V_k is pass@k for binary rewards and max@k for continuous ones. Both reduce to the standard @1 objective at k=1.

**Derivation route.** Appendix A proves a general variational optimality lemma: for any objective of the form U_x(π) − β·KL(π‖π_ref) with U_x concave and directionally differentiable, any maximizer has the Gibbs form π*(y|x) ∝ π_ref(y|x)·exp(A_{π*}(y,x)/β), where A is the score appearing in the directional derivative. Uniqueness follows from a Bregman-type decomposition J_x(π) − J_x(π*) ≤ −β·KL(π‖π*). The authors also argue that an optimum must have full support on supp(π_ref), since transferring mass ε to an unsupported y costs βε·log(1/ε), which dominates the O(ε) reward change.

**pass@k.** With U_x(π) = 1 − (1 − p_π(x))^k, the score is A_π(y,x) = k(1−p_π(x))^{k−1}·R(y,x). Substituting into the lemma and normalizing gives Proposition 4.1, and summing over successful completions gives the scalar equation of Proposition 4.2. Two corollaries follow: optimizing the plain @1 objective with β_eff(x) reproduces the pass@k optimum exactly (Cor. 4.3), as does optimizing with constant β and reward R̃ = k(1−p*)^{k−1}R (Cor. 4.4). Both hold because the objectives decompose over prompts and β_eff is constant in y for fixed x.

**max@k.** With real-valued rewards the score involves ∫_{-∞}^{R(y,x)} F_{π*}(u|x)^{k−1}du, giving an implicit optimal policy that requires the reward CDF under the *optimal* policy. The paper provides a numerical reconstruction procedure (Appendix D) but does not use it empirically. The tractable route is Corollary 4.6 (max@k = @1 with reward transform S_x(r) = k∫_{-∞}^r F_{π*}(u|x)^{k−1}du) and, for win-rate rewards, Proposition 4.7's fully explicit transform.

**KAPPO.** One-time reference calibration: draw M=30 reference completions per training prompt, store their rewards, estimate p̂_ref(x) (binary) or F̂_ref(r|x) (continuous), then solve the fixed point / build the lookup table once per (k, β) pair. The bank is reused across all runs sharing the same base model and dataset; changing k, β, learning rate or seed requires no new generation. Training then proceeds with the base algorithm untouched except for the substitution.

**Three instantiations.** @k-DPO swaps DPO's global β for β_eff(x) inside the sigmoid (regularization side; chosen because DPO never optimizes a scalar reward). @k-A⋆PO substitutes the adaptive reward into A⋆PO's squared-regression loss — binary: βR/β_eff(x) with the calibrated partition term ℓ(x)=β·log Ẑ(x); continuous: the win-rate transform with Z(x)=1. @k-GRPO replaces raw rewards with S_x^q(R_i) before standard group-relative normalization, with no KL penalty at all.

**Experimental design.** Math: train on the MATH training split, validate on MATH test (Level 5 subset for checkpoint selection), final-test on AIME 2026 — chosen because it postdates both checkpoints and is markedly harder. Code: train on LeetCodeDataset train split, validate on its test split, final-test on the hard subset of LiveCodeBench v6 (Jan–Apr 2025). Models: OLMo-3-7B-Instruct-SFT (fully open, explicit decontamination) and Qwen3-4B-Base. Baselines: k=1 (same optimizer, isolating the @k effect) and PKPO at minimum rollout multiplicity n=k. Full-parameter bf16 training, AdamW, cosine schedule with 3% warmup, 4096-token max length, ≤1000 steps (math) / ≤500 steps (code), 128 prompts per step, 32 GH200 GPUs.

## Results & Highlights

- **KAPPO matches PKPO at the matched sampling budget on MATH Level 5 (Table 1).** OLMo-3-7B: @2-A⋆PO reaches 76.98% pass@2 vs 77.38% for PKPO (k_train=2) — a 0.40 pt gap; Qwen3-4B: 70.48% vs 70.77% — a 0.29 pt gap. Both are within the paper's stated "within 0.5 pp at matching budget" claim, and both are far above the starting checkpoints (50.94% and 38.32% pass@2 respectively).
- **But the pass@1 gap is not negligible on Qwen3-4B.** @2-A⋆PO gets 57.27% pass@1 vs 59.88% for PKPO (−2.61 pts), and @4-A⋆PO gets 57.22% vs 57.94% (−0.72). On OLMo the same comparison is 65.34% vs 66.08% (−0.74). So the "matching a SoTA multi-completion optimizer" headline is accurate at the training-matched budget and optimistic at pass@1 — the abstract's framing does not mention this asymmetry.
- **Rollout cost drops by 50% / 75%.** With 128 prompts per step over 1000 steps, @k-A⋆PO generates 128,000 on-policy completions; PKPO at n=k needs 256,000 (k=2) or 512,000 (k=4). Calibration adds 0.5 h against a 10–12 h training run (4.2–5.0%), falling below 1% once amortized over six runs — for MATH that is 30 × 7,500 = 225,000 one-time reference completions.
- **k saturates almost immediately in Table 1.** OLMo @2-A⋆PO 65.34% vs @4-A⋆PO 64.46% pass@1; Qwen 57.27% vs 57.22%. Going from k=2 to k=4 changes pass@1 by less than 1 pt in both models, so k is not a fine-grained dial in this regime even though the paper describes it as one that "directly controls sharpening."
- **In-distribution gains are enormous.** OLMo-3-7B goes from 37.38% to 65.34% pass@1 (+27.96 pts) and Qwen3-4B-Base from 23.93% to 57.27% (+33.34 pts) on MATH Level 5 — large enough that they mostly reflect fitting the training distribution rather than a capability jump, which is why the AIME/LiveCodeBench transfer results matter more than these numbers.
- **The sharpening control itself is figure-only.** Figures 2 and 4 show pass@k_eval / max@k_eval curves on AIME 2026 and LiveCodeBench v6 hard subset, and the text states that k>1 training "retains less of the pass@1 gain but consistently overtakes the single-reward baselines at larger evaluation budgets." No numeric values, no seed error bars, no table accompany these claims anywhere in the main text or appendix.
- **Calibration error is quantified and lopsided.** At k=4, M=30, the win-rate-transformed reward has standard deviation 2.62% and bias 0.66% of its range at q=0.5, rising to 18.27% and 4.43% at q=0.9; at q=0.01 there is a 0.740 probability of observing zero successes in the bank, and at k=4 the map jumps from g(0)=0 to g(1/30)≈0.553.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty.** The core mathematical machinery — the variational lemma in Appendix A — is a standard KL-regularized control result (Gibbs form plus a Bregman gap), and the pass@k derivation is three lines once you write A_π(y,x) = k(1−p_π)^{k−1}R(y,x). What is genuinely new is not the difficulty of the derivation but the *identification*: nobody had written down that the @k optimum is exactly the @1 optimum under a prompt-dependent β, and that observation is what makes @k deployable on DPO/A⋆PO/GRPO without new estimators. That is a real contribution, but it is a contribution of framing and engineering rather than of hard theory, and the paper's prose occasionally oversells it ("optimizer-independent explanation" is fair; the implicit suggestion of deep new mathematics is not).

**Rigor of the empirical claims.** This is where the paper is weakest, and the weakness is structural rather than sloppy. The central empirical claim — that KAPPO lets you trade pass@1 for coverage by turning k — rests entirely on Figures 2 and 4, which have no numbers attached, no confidence intervals, and no seed replication. AIME 2026 contains 30 problems, so a single problem is worth 3.33 percentage points at pass@1; a claim like "k>1 retains less of the pass@1 gain" is therefore well inside binomial noise at that budget, and pass@k_eval curves at k_eval=512 have their own estimator variance that the paper does not characterize. The one place with real numbers, Table 1, is the in-distribution MATH Level 5 validation set where both models improve by 28–33 points — a regime where almost any competent optimizer looks good. So the paper has solid numbers for a claim it does not need to defend (KAPPO fits MATH) and no numbers for the claim it does need to defend (KAPPO controls sharpening).

**The k-dial saturates, and the paper does not discuss it.** In Table 1, @4 and @2 are within 0.9 pts of each other on both models and both metrics. If k is supposed to be a monotone control knob for the sharpening/coverage trade-off, the near-identity of k=2 and k=4 is a result that needs explanation — perhaps β_eff saturates, perhaps the fixed point compresses, perhaps the win-rate tail noise dominates at larger k. The text simply reports both rows and moves on. A reader trying to use k operationally gets no guidance on the range where it actually does something.

**Implementation choices that quietly undo the theory.** @k-DPO caps β_eff(x) at 2 and adds a failure pseudo-count α=0.5 (Appendix H, J.2). Since β_eff = β/(k(1−p*)^{k−1}) diverges as p* → 1, the cap binds precisely on the *easy* prompts — which is exactly the region where the paper's mechanism (strong regularization on already-solved tasks) is supposed to be doing the anti-sharpening work. The cap is defensible as numerical hygiene, but it means the DPO instantiation is not the theory's β_eff on the prompts that matter most, and no ablation isolates how much of @k-DPO's behavior comes from the cap versus the adaptive pressure. Similarly, @k-GRPO is run without a KL penalty, which the authors admit is outside the theory; the honest analysis in Appendix G shows GRPO's group normalization interacts with the nonlinear transform rather than cancelling it, which means the clean equivalence does not transfer to the most widely used optimizer in practice.

**Weak or unrepresentative baselines.** The "DPO" curve in Figure 2b is not standard DPO: preference pairs are constructed online from eight samples of the *current* policy, taking highest- versus lowest-reward candidates with ties zero-weighted, 64 prompts per step. That is a bespoke online-DPO variant, and it is not obvious it is a strong baseline for sharpening — an entropy-regularized or temperature-adjusted DPO would be the more informative comparison. More broadly, the paper never runs the obvious cheap control for sharpening: an entropy bonus or a temperature sweep. Prior work is cited as showing these are "limited or sensitive," but citing is not measuring, and without that baseline there is no way to know whether KAPPO's coverage gains are large relative to a one-line intervention.

**Missing ablation on the mechanism.** The paper's explanation is that difficulty-dependent pressure redistribution drives the effect. A direct test would stratify prompts by p_ref and show that the gain concentrates on high-difficulty prompts, or ablate the difficulty mapping (e.g. shuffle β_eff across prompts, or use a constant β_eff equal to the mean). Neither is done. The theory predicts a specific, falsifiable pattern and the experiments only check the aggregate curve, which weakens the link between the two halves of the paper.

**Surrogate substitution in the continuous case.** Proposition 4.5 gives the exact max@k policy but requires F_{π*}, which is intractable; Appendix D sketches a numerical reconstruction and the paper explicitly leaves its empirical evaluation "to future work." All code-generation experiments therefore optimize the win-rate surrogate of Proposition 4.7, not raw max@k. That is a reasonable engineering choice and the authors say so, but it means the code results validate a different objective than the one in the title of Section 4.2, and the gap between them is unmeasured.

**Scale and setting heterogeneity.** Two models, 4B and 7B. Qwen3-4B-**Base** is a pretrained checkpoint with no post-training, so for that model "RL post-training" is really RL applied directly to a base model — a different regime from the SFT-then-RL pipeline the paper motivates with OLMo. Whether any of this survives at 30B+ with long chain-of-thought reasoning models, where sharpening is most acute and where the reward distribution is far from binary, is untested. The paper's framing ("frontier models," "post-training") outruns its evidence base.

**Red flags, ranked.** (1) Headline sharpening claims are figure-only on a 30-problem benchmark. (2) The k knob saturates at k=2 with no comment. (3) The β_eff cap neutralizes the mechanism exactly where it is supposed to act, unablated. (4) No entropy-regularization baseline for the central claim. (5) Table 1's 28–33 point gains are in-distribution and flatter the method relative to the harder transfer results. None of these are fatal — the theory stands on its own and the efficiency result is real — but together they mean the paper is a strong theoretical contribution with an under-instrumented empirical section, not the definitive demonstration the abstract implies.

## Strengths

- The equivalence result is the kind of thing that changes how people build things. "pass@k = @1 with prompt-adaptive β" means you do not need a new policy-gradient estimator, a new loss, or k-completion groups — you need a lookup table computed once. That is a genuinely low-friction path from a theory paper to practice, and the three instantiations (DPO, A⋆PO, GRPO) demonstrate it rather than asserting it.
- The theory is clean and, as far as I can check, correct. The uniqueness argument in Proposition 4.2 (Φ_q strictly increasing from −∞ to +∞, with Φ_q(q) < 0 implying p* > p_ref) is tight, and the boundary-case treatment (p_ref ∈ {0,1} ⇒ optimum is π_ref itself) is handled rather than waved away.
- The continuous-reward section resists the temptation to overclaim. The authors explicitly state that the regularization-side equivalence fails for max@k because β_eff depends on the completion's reward, and they redirect to the reward-transform route. Many papers would have papered over that.
- Appendix K is exemplary. Quantifying the calibration error as O(M^{-1/2}) noise with DKW uniform consistency, then showing the bias is +18.27% at q=0.9, is the sort of self-critique that makes a method usable — a practitioner knows to enlarge the bank for hard prompts.
- The efficiency result is real and fairly measured: PKPO is compared at matched prompt batches with its minimum rollout multiplicity, not against a crippled configuration, and the 50%/75% rollout reduction is arithmetic that follows from the setup.
- Evaluation design shows contamination awareness that most RL papers lack: AIME 2026 postdates both checkpoints, LiveCodeBench v6 problems are dated Jan–Apr 2025 against an April 28 model release, OLMo is chosen specifically because its decontamination is documented, and the paper states plainly that Qwen's pretraining cutoff is undocumented so contamination "cannot be ruled out completely."
- Code released, fully open base model for one of the two families, hyperparameter grids published for every method including the baselines.

## Limitations

- **No statistical support for the main empirical claim.** AIME 2026 (30 problems) and LiveCodeBench v6 hard subset results are figure-only, single-seed, no confidence intervals. At 3.33 pts per AIME problem, the pass@1 trade-off claims are not resolvable.
- **k saturates.** k=2 and k=4 are nearly indistinguishable in Table 1 and the paper offers no analysis of why, which undercuts the "k directly controls sharpening" framing.
- **Theory–practice gap on the most important optimizer.** The analysis assumes KL regularization; @k-GRPO is run without any KL term, and Appendix G concedes that GRPO's group normalization reshapes the adaptive pressure rather than preserving it.
- **The DPO instantiation deviates from its own theory** via a β_eff cap at 2 and a pseudo-count α=0.5, with no ablation of either.
- **Continuous-reward results use a surrogate.** Raw-reward max@k requires F_{π*}; only the win-rate variant is trained, and the numerical reconstruction in Appendix D is never evaluated.
- **Static, small calibration bank.** M=30 per prompt with no adaptive budget allocation, which the authors name as a limitation; the upper-tail sensitivity (18.27% std at q=0.9) is exactly where hard-prompt rewards live.
- **Narrow model coverage.** Two models at 4B and 7B, one of them a base (non-post-trained) checkpoint; no scaling study, no long-CoT reasoning model, no MoE.
- **No cheap control baselines** for sharpening (entropy bonus, temperature, KL sweep), so the incremental value of KAPPO's machinery over a one-line regularizer is unmeasured.
- **pass@k remains a proxy.** The paper acknowledges that pass@k gains do not prove discovery of strategies outside the base distribution, but offers no direct measurement of strategy diversity (e.g. clustering of solution traces) to close the gap.
- **No compute-matched comparison to PKPO.** Matching prompt batches while using fewer rollouts per prompt means KAPPO is compared at lower total compute; the more informative comparison — PKPO given the same total rollout budget — is absent.

## Relevance

直击"RL Post-Training Dynamics & Failures"这一方向的核心病灶：RLVR 的 sharpening（熵坍缩、行为覆盖收缩、难题上停滞）正是该领域最典型的训练失效模式，而本文给出了它的解析解释（按难度重分配优化压力）和一个可直接挂在现有优化器上的控制旋钮（KAPPO，k 调覆盖/精度的权衡）。对关心 GRPO 类方法训练动力学、熵坍缩缓解手段、以及"@1 目标 + 自适应正则"这类低侵入改造的读者，理论与工程两条线都值得细读。

## Questions for Further Exploration

1. Why does k saturate between 2 and 4? Is it because β_eff's dynamic range compresses at moderate p*, because the fixed point p* flattens, or because the win-rate transform's upper-tail noise (18.27% std at q=0.9, M=30) swamps the additional pressure? A plot of β_eff(x) distributions at k=2 vs k=4 over the actual MATH prompt mix would settle much of this.
2. What happens if the β_eff cap at 2 is removed or raised in @k-DPO? Since the cap binds on easy prompts — the region the mechanism targets — an ablation with cap ∈ {2, 5, 20, ∞} would reveal how much of the anti-sharpening effect is theory versus clamp.
3. How does KAPPO compare to an entropy bonus or a temperature sweep at matched compute? Without this, the field cannot tell whether prompt-adaptive pressure is worth the calibration machinery.
4. Does the predicted difficulty stratification actually appear? Stratify prompts into p_ref deciles and report the pass@k gain per decile — the theory says the gain should concentrate on low-p_ref prompts, and a flat profile would falsify the mechanism story.
5. Can a compute-matched PKPO (same total rollouts, more prompt batches at n=k) close the residual pass@1 gap on Qwen3-4B (57.27 vs 59.88)? If yes, KAPPO's advantage is purely wall-clock/engineering; if no, there is something the single-completion signal genuinely misses.
6. Does the equivalence survive at 30B+ with long chain-of-thought models, where p_ref is near zero on most prompts (making β_eff ≈ β/k, i.e. nearly no adaptive effect) and where the calibration bank would need to be far larger to resolve the low-p_ref region?
7. What does the method do to *strategy* diversity rather than pass@k — e.g. the number of distinct solution clusters among successful samples? The paper's own framing says pass@k is only a proxy, and nobody has yet measured the thing directly.
8. How sensitive is @k-GRPO to the interaction between S_x^q and group-standard-deviation normalization late in training, when per-prompt win-rate distributions diverge across prompts (Appendix G flags this but does not measure it)?
