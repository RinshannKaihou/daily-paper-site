Same accuracy. Different probe.

Why this matters

If you cite probe accuracy as evidence that a direction represents a concept, that evidence may be silent about what the probe actually tracks.

Stakes: every probe-accuracy claim

Critical edge

The recommended fix rests on CONFLICT, a criterion the author calls “not a validity measure”: at full reversal, s = −N also ranks T perfectly.

Selection heuristic, not a proof

01

Methodology

Population study,
not a probe duel

Every extractor is a scalar linear readout of mean-pooled hidden states, so the comparison is like-for-like: same models, same data, same edit pairs — only the training correlation moves.

  1. 01

    Freeze

    Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Gemma-2-9B-it, four target / nuisance settings.

  2. 02

    Populate

    70 standard extractors per setting (7 depths × 10 configs), plus 35 nuisance-erased variants.

  3. 03

    Impose ρ

    Resample training data to ρtr ∈ {.5, .7, .9}; hold one test pool and reweight its four cells.

  4. 04

    Edit nuisance only

    Human counterfactuals, verified pronoun swaps, phrase replacement. Synonym placebos set the floor.

  5. 05

    Compare

    Test DEC (AUROC) against CFN (score change ÷ target gap), with |LEAK| from labels alone.

The protocol needs nuisance labels on development data, but no counterfactuals at deployment — which is what makes it portable to a new construct.

The counterintuitive part

The nuisance that is not correlated in the world is absorbed more. First person has no natural tie to sentiment in these reviews (.66 vs .68), yet under imposed ρ its median CFN is .12–.17 — against .05–.06 for negation, which really does co-occur with negative sentiment (.64 vs .30). What gets absorbed is the correlation the training set manufactured.

03

Strengths
& limitations

Read together,
not separately

Strengths

  • The negative control is the best experiment: same reviews, one naturally correlated nuisance (negation) and one that is not (first person).
  • A population, not a pair. 70 extractors per setting means the claim is about the probes you could plausibly have shipped.
  • Placebo edits give a noise floor (median |CFN| .01–.06) next to every effect that is reported.
  • The selection experiment has a cost curve and reports the criterion that backfires, not just the one that wins.
  • Reproducibility discipline: independent metric reimplementation to <10⁻⁸, disjointness audit, every table regenerated by script.

Limitations

  • Correlated regimes are synthetic — ρ is imposed by resampling; the Amazon-1P ρ=.9 condition cannot occur in natural review data.
  • One nuisance per setting. Selectivity against unexamined nuisances, and nuisance interactions, are unknown.
  • Linear readouts only, mean-pooled states, two 7–9B models, English. SAE features and nonlinear probes are untested.
  • Judge agreement is mediocre (κ .06–.55); CEBaB keeps 41% of judged edits at recall .50, a selective subset.
  • The magnitude is filter-sensitive (.41–1.15 vs .26–.89), and the 4.7× upper end may include badly regularized probes.
04

Open questions

What the paper
leaves untested

  1. Does CONFLICT selection damage probes when the nuisance is constitutive of the target — negation as compositional sentiment, “not good” meaning “bad”?
  2. At matched DEC, is it cheaper to fix the data or fix the readout — and do the two compose?
  3. How much of the 4.7× spread survives if you restrict to the top-k probes by development DEC?
