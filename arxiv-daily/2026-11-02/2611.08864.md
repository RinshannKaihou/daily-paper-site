> **一句话总结：** 作者提出 ASP——一个在 rollout 开始前跑一次的三步离线调度器（Qwen2.5-1.5B 预测输出长度 → 动态规划在 b..b+κ 的尺寸约束下切出长度同质的 minibatch → 模拟退火把 minibatch 重排成负载均衡的 batch group），插进 TU/GU/CU 分离的异步 GRPO 管线后，在 DeepScaleR 上把 Qwen3-30B-A3B 的平均迭代时间从 MindSpeed-RL 的 927 s 压到 215.2 s（−76.8%）、从 verl 的 536.7 s 压到 215.2 s（−59.9%）；但真正归功于调度器本身（架构固定、只开关调度）的只有 238 → 212 s 的 10.9%，其余大头来自"把 rollout 和训练拆开"这件事本身。

> **TL;DR:** On DeepScaleR with matched 64 Ascend 910B3 NPUs, ASP cuts mean end-to-end iteration time to 215.2 s for Qwen3-30B-A3B (vs 536.7 s for a verl 0.7.1 disaggregated baseline, −59.9%; vs 927 s for the MindSpeed-RL hybrid, −76.8%) and reaches 58 tokens/s/NPU vs MindSpeed-RL's 18 (3.22×). The number that actually belongs to the scheduler is much smaller: holding the ASP architecture fixed and toggling scheduling on/off over 200 iterations of Qwen3-30B-A3B on DeepScaleR moves mean iteration time from 238 s to 212 s (10.9%) and end-to-end throughput from 47 to 58 tokens/s/NPU (+23.4%). The most interesting finding is negative-flavored: naive length-sorted dispatch improves intra-minibatch padding (0.322 → 0.226) but triples batch-group load CV (0.103 → 0.403), producing a monotonic iteration-time drift (Spearman +0.670) that ASP's reordering removes (−0.075). The DP step, however, buys almost nothing (padding 0.226 → 0.224), and all comparisons are Ascend-only, single-seed, and against no disaggregated system other than verl.

## 核心贡献

- **提出"sorting bubbles"这一概念，并把局部效率与全局供给稳定性显式拆开。** 作者指出：按预测长度排序能让每个 minibatch 内部长度同质（局部更快），但会让短 minibatch 先发、长 minibatch 后发，训练队列先积压后抽干——局部优化制造了全局时间上的不平衡。这是全文最有价值的一句观察，也是对 StreamRL 只做 inter-instance 路由的一个真实补充。
- **一个零在线开销的三步离线调度器。** 长度预测（微调 Qwen2.5-1.5B，固定不变）、O(κn) 动态规划切分 minibatch、模拟退火重排 batch group。三步全在训练前跑完，训练期间不引入任何调度决策；SA 在 N_B=10^7 个 minibatch 上耗时 0.31 s，10^9 次迭代 35.5 s。
- **把调度器嵌进有界 lag 的异步架构并给出可观测的 lag 估计。** TU/GU/CU 三角色 + 单一受控 Training Queue + 延迟重载权重（actor 解完当前 minibatch 才换权重）。用实测 timing 推出 d_off ≈ Q̄_arrival + (T_save+T_convert+T_load+T_gen)/T_train ≈ 3.3 个 trainer step，而不是靠"我们觉得 lag 很小"。
- **逐级消融（C0–C4）把调度收益从架构收益里剥出来。** C0 原始顺序 / C1 长度排序 / C2 加 DP / C3 随机打乱 / C4 均衡排序，五档在同一架构、同一批 prompt、同一预测器下对比，能干净地看出每一步各自值多少。
- **预测器质量的敏感性扫描。** τ 从 1.0 扫到 0.0（6 档 × 2 个模型 × 3 seed），诚实地报告排序带来的 padding 收益在 τ=0.25 时已经转为微弱负值——这是很多系统论文会藏起来的结果。

## Key Contributions

- **Names and isolates a third bubble class.** Beyond StreamRL's padding and skewness bubbles, the paper identifies *sorting bubbles*: the temporal imbalance in trainer arrivals caused by dispatching length-sorted minibatches in sorted order. This reframes rollout scheduling as a two-level problem (local minibatch composition vs. global dispatch ordering) rather than a single bin-packing problem, and it is the one genuinely new conceptual contribution.
- **A three-stage offline scheduler with no online cost.** Length prediction (a fine-tuned Qwen2.5-1.5B kept frozen), an O(κn)-time dynamic program that partitions the sorted prompt list into minibatches of size b..b+κ maximizing summed utilization, and simulated annealing that permutes minibatches into batch groups of near-equal predicted load. All three run once before training; nothing is solved during rollout.
- **A bounded-lag integration.** TU (reward, log-prob, GRPO update), one or more GUs (vLLM actors), and a CU co-located with the TU that dispatches scheduled minibatches and enforces Training-Queue backpressure. Weights refresh every training step via export → layout conversion → deferred actor reload, so an actor never aborts an in-flight minibatch.
- **A quantitative lag account.** Measured breakdown T_save=23.5 s, T_convert=84.6 s, T_load=73.9 s, T_gen=763.3 s, T_train=287.6 s yields d_off ≈ 3.3 trainer steps and a generation duty cycle η ≈ 0.912, making "the lag is small" a measured claim rather than an assertion.
- **A staged ablation that separates scheduler from architecture.** C0–C4 hold the ASP architecture fixed, so the 10.9% scheduling-only gain can be compared honestly against the 76.8% headline that mostly reflects disaggregation.

## Methodology

**Problem framing.** The system is a producer–consumer pipeline: generation actors produce prompt–response samples, a Training Queue buffers them, a trainer consumes global batches. Each prompt is sampled several times, and all its samples stay in the same minibatch and the same global batch, so batch sizes are counted in prompts. Because an actor returns a minibatch only when its *longest* sequence finishes, three inefficiencies appear: padding bubbles (short sequences idle while the longest decodes), skewness bubbles (heavy-tailed lengths collapse the effective active batch near the end of decoding), and sorting bubbles (sorted dispatch makes arrival rate decay over time, so the queue first fills then drains).

**Step 1 — length prediction.** Run the target model on a subset of prompts to collect prompt–length pairs, then fine-tune Qwen2.5-1.5B on 4,000 pairs to regress output length. Only rank order matters, so quality is reported as Kendall's τ: 0.64 for Qwen3-30B-A3B (8,192-token cap; 0.55 at 16,384), 0.44 for Qwen3-32B, 0.58 for Qwen3-8B. τ=0.64 corresponds to ≈82% of prompt pairs being ordered consistently. The predictor is frozen for the whole run, justified post hoc by a measured 1.6% drift in mean response length across 103 policy versions (R²=0.013).

**Step 2 — DP minibatch construction.** Sort prompts by predicted length l₀≤…≤l_{n−1}. For a candidate minibatch spanning indices j..i−1, define utilization R(j,i) = (S[i]−S[j]) / ((i−j)·l_{i−1}), i.e. predicted useful tokens over the token slots implied by batch size and longest predicted output. Maximize the sum of R over a partition subject to b ≤ (i−j) ≤ b+κ. Because each state only examines κ+1 candidate widths, the DP is O(κn) time and O(n) space. Defaults: b=32, κ=2, so minibatch sizes vary only between 32 and 34.

**Step 3 — balanced batch-group ordering.** With minibatch load B_i = sum of predicted lengths, group minibatches into groups of k and minimize the variance of group load about the mean M. Solved by simulated annealing: propose a random swap of two minibatches, accept if improving or with probability exp((V(σ)−V(σ'))/T_n), with T_n = C/log(n+2), C=1, n_max=2×10⁶, k=6. Each swap touches only two group loads, so the objective updates in constant time; measured throughput is ~30M iterations/s, and 2.7×10⁵ iterations already reach >95% variance reduction in <0.01 s on one CPU core.

**System integration.** The CU is a CPU-side process next to the TU with three parts: a Generation Sender (dispatches batch groups as units, maintains per-GU input queues of 5–10 minibatches), a Database (stores unsent prompts, tracks issued minibatches, retries failed sends), and an Ingestor (receives completed minibatches off the trainer's critical path). Backpressure pauses *new* decoding starts when queue occupancy exceeds a threshold; actors already decoding finish. The Training Queue is a separate Ray actor holding samples until a global batch is assembled.

**Weight flow.** Three stages: trainer-side export to a vLLM-compatible shard dict on shared storage; GU-side conversion that merges tensor-parallel ranks and expert shards and repartitions to the inference layout (done once per snapshot per GU, not per actor); deferred per-actor reload. The naive paths — vLLM's `load_weights` endpoint and full process restart — both cost ~200 s for Qwen3-30B-A3B, which is why the authors wrote a parallelized host-to-device loader using a ThreadPoolExecutor over multiple device streams (small tensors grouped, large tensors on separate streams to avoid head-of-line blocking), cutting reload to 60–75 s (measured T_load=73.9 s).

**Setup.** MindSpeed-RL + GRPO, vLLM v0.9.1 as generation backend, Ascend 910B3 nodes of 8 NPUs each. Models: Qwen3-30B-A3B (MoE) and Qwen3-32B (dense) for end-to-end, Qwen3-8B for ablations. Datasets: DeepScaleR (~41K math problems, 8,192-token cap) for throughput/scheduling/scaling; Maze-Reasoning-GRPO-v0.1 (>180K maze problems, 16,384-token cap) for convergence, chosen because math is likely already saturated by the base model. Baselines: the MindSpeed-RL hybrid pipeline (same hardware, no disaggregation) and verl v0.7.1 with FSDP training and vLLM generation, using its one-step off-policy pipeline.

## Results & Highlights

- **Headline end-to-end (Table 3, DeepScaleR, matched 8 nodes / 64 NPUs).** Qwen3-30B-A3B: ASP 215.2 s mean iteration vs verl 536.7 s (−59.9%) and MindSpeed-RL 927 s (−76.8%); Qwen3-32B: 230.7 s vs 615.1 s (−62.5%) and 928 s (−75.1%). On the MoE model this corresponds to 58 tokens/s/NPU vs 18 for MindSpeed-RL, i.e. the claimed 3.22× end-to-end throughput.
- **Scheduling-only gain, architecture held fixed (Appendix F.6.1, Qwen3-30B-A3B + DeepScaleR, 200 iterations each).** Toggling scheduling on moves mean iteration time 238 → 212 s (−10.9%), end-to-end throughput 47 → 58 tokens/s/NPU (+23.4%), minibatch generation-time STD 68 → 48 s (−29.4%), Training Queue occupancy STD −15.0%, and first-iteration time 1042 → 724 s (−30.5%). Generation-side vLLM throughput rises 106 → 126 non-padding tokens/s/NPU. This 10.9% is the honest size of the paper's core contribution.
- **Local schedule quality (Table 4a, 39,000 measured Qwen3-32B responses, controlled τ=0.64, 3 seeds).** Intra-minibatch length STD drops 2138 → 1163 tokens and mean padding rate 0.322 → 0.224 going from C0 to sorted dispatch; the DP step then buys essentially nothing (0.226 → 0.224). The ordering step is where the real swing is: batch-group load CV goes 0.103 (C0) → 0.403 (C1) → 0.401 (C2) → 0.162 (C3, random shuffle) → 0.070 (C4), and group-load spread 1.68 → 9.88 → 9.81 → 2.39 → 1.39.
- **Sorting bubbles are real but need simulated training to show up cleanly (Table 4b, real Qwen3-8B generation + fixed simulated training times, τ=0.58).** C4 has the lowest mean iteration time, 178 s vs 188 s for C0 (−5.3%), and near-zero drift (iteration-index/time Spearman −0.075), while sorted dispatch drifts badly (C1 +0.670, C2 +0.510). Random shuffling reaches C4's queue STD (36 samples) and a slightly lower queue max (104 vs 110) but a worse mean (190 s) and a positive trend (+0.190) — so shuffling is not a substitute for explicit balancing.
- **Prediction quality sets a floor on the sorting benefit (Tables 9–10).** At τ=0.25 sorting is already marginally *negative* on both models (Qwen3-32B padding 0.330 vs C0's 0.322; Qwen3-8B 0.296 vs 0.294), and at τ=0 padding reaches 0.348 vs 0.322. Balanced ordering (C4) nevertheless keeps load CV at or below C0 across the entire oracle-to-random sweep — 0.070 vs 0.103 at τ=0.64, 0.092 vs 0.103 at τ=0.25.
- **Scaling (Figure 2, Qwen3-30B-A3B, fixed 128 trajectories/iteration).** Going from 16 to 32 NPUs (one GU node → three GU nodes on the same TU) cuts iteration time 776 → 288 s, a 2.69× speedup for 3× generation resources; 64 NPUs gives 215.2 s and 128 NPUs 147 s, at which point training rather than rollout becomes the bottleneck. ASP is below both baselines at every tested scale.
- **Convergence under lag (Figure 3, Maze-Reasoning-GRPO-v0.1, Qwen3-30B-A3B, 30 GRPO iterations, global batch 16 prompts).** Both ASP and MindSpeed-RL reach a similar maze-integrity plateau; ASP shows only a short warmup delay. Maze correctness follows the same qualitative pattern. Estimated off-policy distance is 3.3 trainer steps, with mean queue depth at arrival 0.39 global batches (95th percentile 0.75, max 1.25).
- **Scheduler runtime is negligible (Table 6).** SA on N_B=10⁵ minibatches with n_max=2.4×10⁶ reaches 99.4% variance reduction in 0.09 s; at N_B=10⁷ and n_max=2×10⁶ wall time is 0.31 s; even 10⁹ iterations finish in 35.5 s. The DP is O(κn) with κ∈{1,2,3}. Total offline cost is far below one training iteration.

## Authors & Credibility

Salah Chikhi and Luis Zuin (equal contribution) were interns in Huawei's Global Technology Service team in Dongguan; Entong Li and Li Zeng were full-time Huawei employees, with Zeng (zengli@sribd.cn) as corresponding author and affiliated with the Shenzhen Research Institute for Big Data. The work is therefore an industry systems paper from the team that builds and operates MindSpeed-RL — the framework ASP is implemented on top of.

That provenance cuts both ways. Positively, the authors know the Ascend/MindSpeed stack at a level of detail (NPUWorker internals, missing IPC zero-copy, expert-shard merging) that is hard to fake, the engineering is real, and there is an actual internal telecom deployment behind the anecdote. Negatively, ASP is built by the MindSpeed-RL team and evaluated primarily against MindSpeed-RL, so the "hybrid baseline" is a comparison the authors control. The paper is unusually forthcoming about this: it states outright in Appendix G that no direct comparison with StreamRL, AsyncFlow, AReaL, or Laminar was attempted, and that a verl-on-Ascend comparison "could confound system differences with implementation quality." That kind of disclosure is rare and should be credited. The writing is clear, the appendices (A–H) are substantive rather than padding, and Section F.8 documents exactly which knobs a replicator would need.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty: moderate, with one genuinely good idea.** The three-bubble taxonomy and the two-level decomposition (local composition, then global ordering) are a real contribution to how the systems community talks about rollout scheduling. But every individual ingredient is known: predicted-length sorting is StreamRL's, the DP is a small bin-packing variant, and simulated annealing for load balancing is textbook. The paper's own Table 5 is telling — ASP's differentiators against StreamRL/AsyncFlow/AReaL/Laminar are "predicted-length minibatch construction" and "balanced batch-group ordering," both of which are refinements of existing length-aware routing rather than a new mechanism. The framing is stronger than the machinery.

**The 10.9% is the real result, and it is a single unreplicated pair of runs.** Appendix F.6.1 compares two 200-iteration runs that "differ only in whether scheduling is enabled," with no seeds and no confidence interval. The authors' own variance measurement in F.6.3 puts end-to-end iteration-time CV at 6.8% (training-step CV 5.0%). A 10.9% difference is roughly 1.6 CV — not obviously outside run-to-run noise. Three seeds would have cost little and would have made this the paper's most defensible number. As written, the headline 76.8% and 3.22× are architecture-level (disaggregation), the 23.4% throughput gain is a second single-pair measurement, and the ablation numbers that *are* three-seeded (Tables 9–10) are schedule-quality proxies, not wall-clock.

**The DP step barely does anything, yet it is one of three advertised stages.** C1→C2 moves mean padding from 0.226 to 0.224, and the paper admits it "changes mean padding by at most 0.002 relative to C1 across these sweeps, and does not improve every condition." Its justification is a tail argument — misplaced boundaries can delay one minibatch by up to 12 minutes on Qwen3-30B-A3B — which is plausible but unquantified at the distribution level. The abstract's claim of "jointly reduc[ing] three sources of rollout underutilization" through a three-step pipeline is therefore generous: step 2 is a marginal refinement, and the actual work is done by steps 1 and 3.

**Baseline asymmetries that flatter the headline.** Three are checkable in the paper itself. (i) The measurement windows differ sharply (Table 7): ASP 120 iterations, verl 50, MindSpeed-RL 20 — and all means include startup. With a first iteration of ~810 s for ASP and plausibly ~1000 s for the hybrid, a 20-iteration window inflates MindSpeed-RL's mean by roughly 45 s/iteration relative to ASP's 120-iteration window. That is only ~5% of the gap, so it does not overturn the conclusion, but it is not nothing and it is not disclosed. (ii) In the scalability sweep, Figure 2's caption states verl runs *without* dynamic batch sizing — a known verl optimization that is enabled in Table 3. So the scaling comparison uses a deliberately weakened verl while the matched-resource comparison does not, and the two verl numbers at 64 NPUs differ (536.7 s in Table 3 vs a larger value in Figure 2) for exactly this reason. (iii) verl is an FSDP+vLLM stack running on Ascend NPUs, where it is not the native framework; the paper concedes the confound but the 62.5% number is still presented without that caveat.

**The closest competitors are never run.** StreamRL is the paper whose length ranker ASP extends, AsyncFlow and AReaL are the other disaggregated systems, and none is compared against. Appendix G explains why (integration cost on the Ascend stack) and that explanation is reasonable, but it means the central empirical claim — that balanced batch-group ordering is worth adding on top of existing disaggregation — is tested only against verl, which does not do any generation-side batch composition. A reviewer should read the 59.9% vs verl as "disaggregation plus scheduling beats disaggregation without scheduling," not as "ASP beats the state of the art."

**The sorting-bubble problem may not manifest at the operating point where they measure it.** In the real 120-iteration Qwen3-30B-A3B run, mean queue depth at arrival is 0.39 global batches with a 95th percentile of 0.75 and a maximum of 1.25, and the authors state the queue "remains close to empty" because the system is rollout-bound. A queue that never holds more than ~1 batch cannot exhibit the fill-then-drain oscillation that motivates step 3. The drift evidence for sorting bubbles (+0.670 Spearman) comes exclusively from Table 4b, where training time is *simulated* with fixed durations. So the third bubble is demonstrated in a synthetic setting and then argued to matter in a real setting where the queue is empty. The mechanism is sound; the empirical case that it bites in production is thinner than the framing suggests.

**Convergence evidence is 30 iterations of a formatting-heavy maze task, single seed.** Figure 3 shows two reward curves over 30 GRPO iterations at global batch 16. "No visible degradation" from two curves is a weak claim: maze *integrity* measures well-formedness and saturates quickly, and maze *correctness* over 30 iterations at batch 16 has substantial variance. There is no comparison against an on-policy synchronous run at matched compute, no seed spread, and no downstream benchmark. The paper is careful in wording ("no visible degradation in the measured bounded-lag regime") but the abstract's confidence outruns the evidence.

**Platform-specific overhead inflates the architectural win.** The weight-reload path costs 73.9 s per snapshot, and the authors attribute the original 200 s to Ascend-specific gaps — "our environment did not provide a fast safetensors loading path or IPC-handle-based zero-copy sharing between processes." On a CUDA/vLLM stack, weight reload is typically a small fraction of that, which means ASP's duty cycle (η≈0.912) and its T_convert=84.6 s layout conversion are partly artifacts of the NPU software stack. Nothing here is tested on GPUs, so the generality of the throughput claim to the hardware most practitioners use is unknown. The paper says this in the limitations ("implemented in the MindSpeed-RL ecosystem on Ascend 910B3 NPUs") but does not connect it to the size of the reported gains.

**Smaller issues.** The Qwen3-32B predictor reaches only τ=0.44, below the τ=0.50 point where Table 9 shows the padding benefit already eroded (0.322 → 0.271) — so on the dense model the scheduler's local contribution is modest and the 62.5% is essentially disaggregation. The telecom deployment claim (>3 months → ~2 months) appears in the abstract but is explicitly unverifiable ("confidential... qualitative evidence"), which is a questionable place for it. The offline assumption (prompt set fixed before rollout) is acknowledged but is a real limit for online/curriculum data generation. And the appendix discloses generative-AI assistance for writing and code debugging, which is now common but worth noting when weighing the polish of the prose.

## Strengths

- **The sorting-bubble observation is genuinely useful and, as far as I can tell, new in this exact form.** The insight that length-sorted dispatch converts a local win into a global temporal loss — and that the fix is to separate composition from ordering rather than to abandon sorting — is the kind of reframing that changes how other people build schedulers. The C1 vs C4 contrast (load CV 0.403 vs 0.070 on identical minibatches) makes the point cleanly.
- **Zero online overhead is the right engineering call.** By solving everything offline with an O(κn) DP and a simulated annealer that runs in 0.31 s at 10⁷ minibatches, ASP avoids the failure mode of online schedulers, which is that the scheduler itself becomes a latency source under load. This is a design choice that will survive contact with production better than a cleverer online policy would.
- **The ablation is properly staged.** C0/C1/C2/C3/C4 with shared minibatch composition between C2–C4 is exactly the right control structure, and it lets the reader see that random shuffling captures most but not all of the balancing benefit (CV 0.162 vs 0.070) — a distinction that a lazier paper would have elided.
- **Honest reporting of the negative results.** The τ=0.25 crossover where sorting hurts, the "does not improve every condition" admission for the DP, and the explicit note that C3 beats C4 at τ=0 on the 32B model are all results a less careful team would have buried in an appendix table without comment. They are in the appendix tables *with* comment.
- **The lag analysis is quantitative rather than gestural.** Reporting T_save=23.5 s, T_convert=84.6 s, T_load=73.9 s, T_gen=763.3 s, T_train=287.6 s and deriving d_off≈3.3 and η≈0.912 gives a replicator something to check against. Most asynchronous-RL papers assert bounded staleness; this one computes it.
- **The engineering detail on weight flow is unusually concrete.** The 200 s → 60–75 s reload improvement via ThreadPoolExecutor-driven parallel H2D streams, with small tensors grouped to reduce launch overhead and large tensors on separate streams to avoid head-of-line blocking, is the sort of detail that saves other teams weeks.

## Limitations

- **Ascend-only, single-platform.** All experiments run on Ascend 910B3 NPUs with MindSpeed-RL and vLLM v0.9.1. No GPU results, and the weight-reload overhead that shapes the duty-cycle analysis is explicitly attributed to NPU software gaps (no fast safetensors path, no IPC zero-copy), so the magnitude of the gains on CUDA is unknown.
- **No comparison to the systems ASP most resembles.** StreamRL, AsyncFlow, AReaL, and Laminar are discussed at length in Appendix A but never executed. The only disaggregated baseline is verl, which does no generation-side batch composition — so the paper cannot show that ASP's ordering beats StreamRL's routing, only that it beats having no scheduler.
- **Single-seed wall-clock comparisons.** The 10.9% scheduling gain and the 23.4% throughput gain come from one pair of 200-iteration runs. Given the authors' own 6.8% end-to-end CV, this is the weakest link in the empirical chain, and it is the number the paper's thesis actually rests on.
- **Ablations on a smaller model with simulated training.** Table 4b uses real Qwen3-8B generation but fixed simulated training-step durations and weight-refresh delays. The justification (training-step CV of 5.0% over 19 post-warmup iterations) is reasonable but the substitution means the queue dynamics under real, variable training load are untested.
- **Convergence evidence is thin.** 30 GRPO iterations, one seed, one task, global batch 16 prompts, no matched-compute on-policy control, and no held-out evaluation of the resulting model. The claim of "no visible convergence degradation" is defensible as stated but should not be read as "asynchronous ASP trains as well as synchronous training."
- **Offline scheduling requires the prompt set up front.** ASP assumes all prompts are known before rollout, which fits fixed-dataset RL post-training but not streaming or online data generation. The paper flags this; the extension to streaming prompts (online length-aware minibatching) is left open and is non-trivial, since the DP and SA both operate on the full sorted list.
- **Fixed predictor.** The length predictor is frozen for the whole run, validated by a 1.6% mean-length drift over 103 policy versions in one 120-iteration run. Longer runs, curricula, or domain shifts would need re-profiling, and there is no mechanism in the system to detect when the predictor has gone stale.
- **Scale ceiling at 128 NPUs.** The scalability study stops where training becomes the bottleneck, so the regime where generation dominates at large scale — the regime where scheduling should matter most — is extrapolated rather than measured.

## Relevance

与「Compute Infrastructure for Large-Scale ML」直接相关：它讨论的是集群调度层的 straggler/负载不均问题（padding、长尾、队列抖动），并给出了一个可落地的离线调度器实现与 Ascend NPU 上的实测数据，对关注 rollout 吞吐与资源利用率的读者有参考价值。对「RL Post-Training Dynamics & Failures」也有间接意义——它把异步 RL 的 staleness 从口号变成了可观测的 d_off≈3.3 step，但收敛证据只有 30 步单 seed，不足以支撑"异步不掉点"的强结论。

## Questions for Further Exploration

1. **How much of the 10.9% survives three seeds?** The authors measured a 6.8% end-to-end CV themselves; a three-seed replication of the scheduling-on/off pair is the single experiment that would most change how much weight this paper deserves.
2. **Does balanced ordering still help when the Training Queue is allowed to be non-empty?** The real 120-iteration run never exceeded 1.25 global batches in queue depth. If the operating point were shifted toward a training-bound configuration (more GU nodes than needed), would the sorting-bubble effect grow enough to justify step 3 on wall-clock rather than on CV?
3. **What is the DP actually buying in the tail?** The paper mentions boundaries that delay a minibatch by up to 12 minutes on Qwen3-30B-A3B but reports only mean padding. A p95/p99 iteration-time comparison between C1 and C2 would settle whether step 2 earns its place.
4. **How does ASP compose with StreamRL's routing or Laminar's repacking?** The paper argues these are complementary but never tests the combination. Routing long-tail prompts to dedicated instances and then balancing groups *within* each routing class is a natural next experiment, and would test whether the two mechanisms interfere.
5. **What happens on GPUs where reload is cheap?** If T_load drops from 73.9 s to a few seconds on CUDA, η rises toward 1 and the relative benefit of ASP's scheduling versus its architecture changes.
