Why this matters

On-device fine-tuning is bottlenecked by training state, not compute. Rounding noise costs as much as the update gains — smaller learning rates don't help.

A typical optimizer step moves a 4-bit weight about 3% of a single code step. Almost every update is a sub-step.

Critical edge

The 0.629 headline is statistically indistinguishable from a 0.627 same-budget baseline — parity at lower state, not the superiority the abstract implies.

Declared equivalent by the authors' own TOST, ±0.5 pt

01

Method, in four moves

The deployed 4/8-bit codes are the only copy of the weights. There is no master anywhere.

STEP 01

Measure the update in code steps

Groups of 64 weights sit on a 4- or 8-bit grid, W = s·q + β — 3.99 bits/weight once 1% outliers are stored aside. The median 4-bit code step is 0.011; a typical optimizer step moves a weight ~3×10⁻⁴. That gap is the whole problem.

STEP 02

Cache the remainder, not the weight

Each cached group holds 64 eight-bit accumulators in units of 1/128 code step. Codes move only in whole steps: q ← q + trunc(a), remainder stays in the line. Uncached groups freeze their codes entirely — they wait.

STEP 03

Evict with an unbiased flush

A line is 64 accumulators plus a tag; B = 2 bits/weight covers ~23% of groups. On eviction, q ← q + ⌊a⌋ + ξ with ξ ~ Bernoulli(a − ⌊a⌋). Reassignment therefore adds no bias — Prop. 1, checked over 400 forced-eviction trials.

STEP 04

Admit by Fisher-weighted code step

Every 25 steps each set keeps the A groups with the largest π_g = n_g·F_g·s_g². That ordering minimizes the ⅛π_g rounding-noise bound; greedy per-set choice is optimal under a partition matroid (Cor. 1).

State ledger: 390 KB for the 383k-parameter net versus 4.6 MB for a master copy plus Adam; 161 KB versus 1.9 MB for the 157k finder. Both fit an ESP32-S3's 512 KB on-chip SRAM — the master copies do not. Traffic runs 6.6 bits/weight/step against 192 for full precision. A compass gradient folds the six-branch block back into one 3×3 conv and costs 0.0 points over 400 steps.

03

Where it holds, where it doesn't

The paper is unusually candid about its own losses. Both columns below are the authors' evidence, read against their claims.

Strengths

  • A reframing, not a trick. Residual precision becomes a resource-allocation problem with an explicit optimality argument for the assignment rule.
  • The proof is load-bearing. Unbiasedness is what makes cache reassignment free — and it is checked numerically, not just asserted.
  • Scores the deployed format. Catches the 5.8-point merge penalty that training-time benchmarks hide.
  • Honest statistics. Paired seeds and CIs, TOST with a pre-declared ±0.5 margin, and the two places where they lose are reported.

Limitations

  • One architecture family. Small CNNs with folded six-branch blocks; no transformer, no LLM scale, no layers whose code steps span orders of magnitude.
  • The margin is 0.2 points over the closest same-budget baseline — and the authors' own test calls that equivalent.
  • Associativity does no work. Direct-mapped, four-way and fully associative caches land within 0.1 points; the cache metaphor is presentational at 6,163 groups.
  • Theory stops short. The summed one-step model still favors rounding after 400 steps, contradicting the 1.4-point ablation. No bridging argument is offered.
04

Open questions

Three threads a follow-up would have to pull.

Q1

Why do 400 steps of individually-favored rounding lose 1.4 points to 400 steps of waiting? Is the missing term trajectory variance, or drift away from a good optimum?

Q2

Does πg-ranked admission still pick the right 23% when per-channel scales make code steps vary by 2–3 orders of magnitude?

Q3

Would label-free test-time adaptation beat a two-minute guided calibration plus SGAC? The corruption benchmark motivates TTA but never runs it.
