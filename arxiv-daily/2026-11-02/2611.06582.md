> **一句话总结：** 把两个输入之间的距离对数看成宽度 N 网络中的随机游走，作者算出其漂移与方差到 1/N 阶，证明"混沌边缘"向更大权重方差平移 Δσ²w ≈ 8/N（tanh、σ²b=0.05），并用此给出免搜索的初始化规则 Crinit——在 MNIST 宽 64、深 96 的全连接网上达 0.903 测试精度，而 LeCun 规则只有 0.713、He 规则只有 0.567。

> **TL;DR:** Treats ln of the squared distance between two inputs as a random walk across layers and derives its drift (ln χ₁ + a₁/N) and variance (b₁/N) to first order in 1/N, showing the edge of chaos of a *typical* width-N tanh network sits at a larger weight variance by ≈7.9/N at σ²b = 0.05 (0.12 at N=64, measured 0.14), while the *network-averaged* critical point moves the other way — which resolves a standing theory-vs-simulation dispute. The same walk governs backpropagated gradients. The payoff is Crinit, an initialisation that lands on the finite-width edge of chaos analytically: on MNIST with N=64, L=96 it reaches 0.903(6) test accuracy after two epochs versus 0.713(21) for LeCun and 0.567(16) for He, and 91/100 of its networks reach 85% training accuracy versus 10/100 for LeCun. Gains over the *same* operating point without the 1/N correction are only 0.009(12) — i.e. essentially zero — so most of the practical benefit comes from the paper's second result (where to sit on the line, set by a one-nat compression budget) and from the first-layer scaling, not from the headline 1/N shift.

## 核心贡献

- **给出有限宽"混沌边缘"的解析位置。** 将 ln D_ℓ（两输入平方距离的对数）视为逐层随机游走，漂移 μ_d = ln χ₁ + a₁/N、长期方差 σ²_d = b₁/N，其中 a₁ < 0 由激活函数的高斯矩显式给出（式 9）。漂移为零即定义有限宽混沌边缘，它位于比平均场边缘**更大**的权重方差处。
- **化解文献中"临界点到底移没移"的争论。** 关键区分：a₁ 来自 E[ln(1+ε)] ≈ −½Var(ε) 的 Jensen 效应，因此**典型网络**（大多数网络、也是从业者实际训练的那一个）会收缩距离，必须 χ₁ > 1 才能补偿；而**对网络取平均**时，均值被极少数距离爆炸的网络拉高，临界点反而向小 σ²w 移动。此前解析工作（Grosvenor & Jefferson 2022；Terin 2026）算的是平均量，仿真跟踪的是单个网络，两者本就不该一致。
- **反向传播遵循同一随机游走。** 权重矩阵与其转置对随机向量的拉伸因子相同，故输出端随机方向梯度的平方范数与两输入平方距离同分布；因此"梯度既不消失也不爆炸"与"距离保持"是同一个条件，一个判据同时管前向与反向。
- **确定"沿线上该坐哪里"。** 沿混沌边缘 q* 仍自由。作者用高斯信道容量作为输入相似度信息量的代理，发现信息在压缩预算 γ ≈ 1 nat（κL ≈ 2）之前不随 q* 下降，据此定出 γ(q*, L) = 1，得 q* = 0.31 (L=32)、0.14 (L=96)，大深度下 q* ∝ L^{−1/2}。
- **产出可直接用的初始化规则 Crinit**（含 pip 包与代码）：给定激活、深度 L、宽度 N，直接算 σ²w = e^{−a₁/N}/P(q*)、σ²b = q* − σ²w V(q*)，并把第一层直接置到稳态信号 q*（而非 σ²w/d_in）。
- **大规模训练验证**：MNIST / Fashion-MNIST，N=64（及 32），L=32/96，每格 100 个独立网络、学习率经 pilot 选择；相对标准规则在"其常用设置"下 12/12 全胜，相对**调过增益**的标准规则 8 胜 4 平 0 负。

## Key Contributions

- **A closed-form finite-width critical point.** Expanding the exact finite-width recursion to O(1/N) yields drift μ_d = ln χ₁ + a₁/N with a₁ = −½(3P₄/P² − 1) + (signal-alignment and signal-fluctuation terms), and variance σ²_d = b₁/N. Setting μ_d = 0 gives Δσ²w = −a₁/(N ∂_{σ²w} ln χ₁), i.e. ≈7.9/N for tanh at σ²b = 0.05 and ≈0.12 in σ²w at N = 64.
- **The typical/mean distinction as the resolution of a dispute.** Because ln of a fluctuating neuron average is below the log of its mean, the typical network contracts the distance while the ensemble mean is inflated by rare expanding networks. The paper states explicitly that the two critical points sit on opposite sides of the mean-field line, at order 1/N, and shows both numerically (Fig. 3).
- **A single condition for forward and backward propagation.** The transpose argument makes the squared gradient's distribution over networks identical to that of the squared distance, so the finite-width edge of chaos is simultaneously the point where typical gradients neither vanish nor explode. Concretely: at N=64, σ²b=0.05, the median squared gradient 96 layers down is 0.08 of its output value on the mean-field edge versus 0.83 on the finite-width edge.
- **An information-theoretic criterion for the operating point.** Measures how much information about input similarity survives L layers, defines a compression budget γ = 1 nat, and shows the plateau of that information depends on N and L only through N/L, fitting ½log₂(1 + 1.2 N/L) — the capacity of a Gaussian channel with SNR 1.2 N/L.
- **Crinit, a parameter-free (one-knob) initialiser plus an open-source package**, with separate handling of the first layer and of scale-free (ReLU-type) activations, where the leaky slope α is chosen so that γ = 1.
- **Avalanche statistics as a corollary.** On the finite-width edge the walk is driftless, so "avalanches" of the distance are random-walk excursions with survival probability n^{−1/2} and duration/size exponents 3/2 and 4/3, matching the universality class reported in earlier simulation work.

## Methodology

The setup is a fully connected network with L hidden layers of width N, Gaussian weights W_ij ~ N(0, σ²w/N) and biases b_i ~ N(0, σ²b), activation φ with φ(0) = 0 (tanh in the figures, erf as a check, plus ReLU/leaky ReLU in the ReLU sections). The mean-field reference is the standard variance map q_{ℓ+1} = σ²w V(q_ℓ) + σ²b with V(q) = E[φ(√q ζ)²], and the mean-field edge of chaos is χ₁ = σ²w P(q*) = 1 with P(q) = E[φ′(√q ζ)²], giving σ²w = 1/P(q*), σ²b = q* − V(q*)/P(q*) parameterised by the steady signal q*.

The finite-width analysis starts from an exact one-layer statement: given layer ℓ, each pre-activation of layer ℓ+1 is a sum of N independent Gaussians, so the pair (z_i, δ_i) is exactly bivariate Gaussian with variances/covariance given by the empirical averages over the N neurons (Eq. 6). Replacing those averages by expectations recovers mean field; keeping them gives an exact layer-by-layer sampler that the paper uses for all its "simulations" (no weight matrices needed, so N up to 1024 is cheap).

For a small distance, φ(z_j) − φ(z′_j) ≈ φ′(z_j)δ_j, so the per-layer log-increment is s_ℓ = ln σ²w + ln[(1/N)Σ_j φ′(z_j)² η_j²], a sum of random steps. Three sources of O(N^{−1/2}) fluctuation enter: (i) the finite neuron average fluctuates about P(q*), and because the log of a fluctuating average is depressed by half its variance, this contributes the dominant, universally negative term −½(3P₄/P² − 1)/N; (ii) the perturbation partly aligns with the signal (r_ℓ = K_ℓ/√(Q_ℓ D_ℓ) fluctuates about zero); (iii) the signal size itself fluctuates about q* (x_ℓ = Q_ℓ − q*, with both E[x] and E[x²] contributing). Collecting terms gives a₁ (Eq. 9) and b₁. The expansion requires the signal to settle in far fewer than N layers, N(1 − F′) ≫ 1 — for tanh, q* ≫ 1/(2N) — which is exactly why N = 16 fails at small q* in Fig. 2(a) (shaded region, N(1−F′) < 3).

Two structural consequences are then derived rather than assumed. First, the mean distance is exp(μ_d ℓ + ½σ²_d ℓ), so on the mean-field edge (μ_d = a₁/N < 0) the typical distance decays as e^{a₁ℓ/N} while the mean grows at an estimated rate (a₁ + ½b₁)/N — the log-normal median/mean split that the paper identifies as the source of the literature disagreement. Second, the transpose argument transfers the whole walk to gradients. The zero-bias end of the line is treated separately because ∂_{σ²w} ln χ₁ vanishes at (1, 0), so the displacement there scales as N^{−1/2} rather than 1/N: solving ln χ₁ = −a₁/N without linearising gives σ²w = 1.31 at N=64 and 1.13 at N=256.

The operating point comes from an information measurement. 128 input pairs with decorrelations ε₀ spread logarithmically over [10⁻³, 1] are pushed through one finite network; the output decorrelation ε_L is fitted by a cubic in ln ε₀ (the "signal"), the residual scatter is treated as Gaussian noise, and I = ½log₂(1 + Var f / Var(ln ε_L − f)) is reported as bits per pair. This is explicitly a proxy, not a bound. Compression γ = ln(ε₀^max/ε₀^min) − ln(ε_L^max/ε_L^min) is measured in nats and collapses onto the mean-field prediction as a function of κ(q*)L for all depths. The budget γ = 1 (κL ≈ 2) is read off where I starts to fall, and the paper notes that training scans put the optimum between γ = 0.5 and 1 — so the constant is empirical, not derived.

Training tests: N = 64 (and 32 in one section), L = 32 and 96, tanh with Gaussian and orthogonal weights plus a ReLU/leaky-ReLU family; MNIST and Fashion-MNIST; no normalisation, no skip connections, linear read-out with variance 1/N; SGD with momentum 0.9, batch 128, two epochs on MNIST (936 steps) and 2/10 epochs on Fashion-MNIST. Learning rate η = η₀·32/L with η₀ chosen from {1.5, 5, 15}×10⁻³ by a 12-network pilot using final *training* loss (a rate at which more than half diverge is ineligible), then 100 fresh-seed networks per rule and depth. Fashion-MNIST adds a full gain/compression search on a held-out 10k validation split (~2000 pilot networks per depth). Standard errors are over networks; ties are defined as differences below twice the standard error of the difference.

## Results & Highlights

- **Drift and variance confirmed to 1–2%.** Along the mean-field edge of the tanh network, the measured Nμ_d agrees with a₁ (Eq. 9) within one to two per cent for q* from 0.02 to 1 at N = 64 and N = 256, and the long-run variance Nσ²_d agrees with b₁ within a few per cent, degrading to 11% at the smallest q*; the same holds for erf. Concretely at σ²b = 0.05 the theory gives a₁ = −1.68 and b₁ = 3.99, and the exact-chain simulation gives Nσ²_d = 4.23 (N=32) and 4.27 (N=64) against 3.99 predicted — a ~6–7% overprediction of the variance, not of the drift.
- **The predicted shift is measured, with a known finite-N bias.** Eq. (10) gives Δσ²w = 7.9/N at σ²b = 0.05, i.e. 0.12 in σ²w at N = 64. Locating the zero-crossing of the measured drift gives 0.14 at N = 64 — 13% larger, shrinking as 1/N as the next order predicts — and agrees with Eq. (10) within statistical error for N ≥ 256 (Fig. 2c). At fixed steady signal instead of fixed bias, the all-orders correction is 2–9% smaller than the first-order one for N ≥ 64.
- **Zero-bias end scales as N^{−1/2}, not 1/N.** The finite-width edge of chaos meets σ²b = 0 at σ²w = 1.31 for N = 64 and 1.13 for N = 256, versus 1.00 for mean field; the all-orders value at N = 64 is 1.30.
- **Orthogonal weights halve the effect, and the theory overshoots.** With orthogonal hidden weights only the nonlinearity drives fluctuations, so the predicted a₁ drops from −1.68 to −0.51 and the measured shift is about four times smaller. But here the measured drift and shift lie **13–18% below** the theory — a discrepancy of opposite sign to the Gaussian case that the paper says it has not traced.
- **MNIST, N=64, L=96, two epochs (100 networks per rule):** Crinit 0.903(6) (median 0.919), mean-field edge of chaos at the same operating point 0.893(10), typical-preserving σ²c(N) 0.744(19), LeCun 0.713(21), He 0.567(16), PyTorch default 0.1135. Crinit also gets 91/100 networks past 85% training accuracy versus 10/100 for LeCun and 18/100 for typical-preserving; 84 of its 100 networks lie within 0.03 of their median, against 35 of LeCun's.
- **MNIST, L=32 is where the honest reading bites:** Crinit 0.9517(4) vs LeCun 0.9438(5) and He 0.9392(5) — a margin resolved at eight-plus standard errors — but vs the mean-field edge of chaos at the *same* operating point it is 0.9513(4), a difference of 0.0004. A 1000-pair same-seed rerun gives 0.95146 vs 0.95140, paired difference 0.00006 ± 0.00016, with Crinit ahead in 494 pairs and behind in 497. Training speed is the real L=32 win: 159(1) steps to 85% training accuracy vs 257 for LeCun, 234 for He, 164 for mean-field-edge.
- **ReLU family, L=96:** He ReLU reaches 0.491(11) and never gets 85% training accuracy; Crinit's leaky ReLU reaches 0.9250(8) over the 79 of 100 networks that did not diverge at its pilot's rate, and 0.9112(7) over all 100 at a third of that rate. At N=32, L=96, where the zero-bias point compresses twice the budget, Crinit's leaky ReLU still reaches 0.894(2) against 0.423(12) for He.
- **Fashion-MNIST, twelve comparisons (three families × two depths × 2/10 epochs):** Crinit beats every standard rule at its usual variance in 12/12, by up to 0.355 at L=96 (0.8600(7) vs 0.770(18) after ten epochs for tanh; 0.8503 vs 0.716 after ten epochs for the ReLU family); against the best *tuned* gain it wins 8, ties 4, loses 0 (largest margins 0.798 vs 0.766 and 0.860 vs 0.840 at L=96), and it is fastest to 80% training accuracy in all six groups, using 9–40% fewer steps than tuned gain and 31–84% fewer than usual variance.
- **Information budget quantified:** the plateau of I is ½log₂(1 + 1.2 N/L) bits per pair — e.g. 2.93 ± 0.01 bits at L=10, N=512 falling to 1.15 ± 0.01 at L=160, N=512 — and ReLU on its single critical point transmits 1.3–1.4× fewer bits at L=10, 1.6–2.3× at L=40 and 3.9–4.6× at L=160.
- **Gradient propagation, the practical corollary:** on the mean-field edge a width-64 network contracts the distance between two inputs, and the squared gradient, by a factor 14 over a hundred layers; on the finite-width edge the median squared gradient 96 layers below the output is 0.83 of its output value instead of 0.08.

## Authors & Credibility

Single author, Arn Caesar, Blackett Laboratory and Centre for Complexity Science, Imperial College London. This is a physics-style paper (cond-mat.dis-nn primary, cross-listed cs.LG and stat.ML) in the statistical-physics-of-learning tradition of Poole et al. 2016, Schoenholz et al. 2017 and Hayou et al. 2019, and the reference list is the right one: it cites the mean-field edge-of-chaos lineage, the 1/N single-input results (Hanin & Nica; Sussillo & Abbott; Cohen & Newman; Kogler et al.), the disputed finite-width calculations (Grosvenor & Jefferson, SciPost 2022; Terin, Sci. Rep. 2026), the simulation-side works (Tamai et al., PRR 2025; D'Inverno et al., Neural Networks 2026; Ghavasieh et al. 2025), and the random-matrix/Lyapunov literature it borrows the typical-vs-mean argument from (Crisanti, Paladin & Vulpiani).

Verification aids are unusually good for a preprint: an exact finite-width recursion used as the simulation engine (so "simulation" means the true chain, not an approximation of it), 100 networks per training cell with reported standard errors, a 1000-pair same-seed paired comparison for the one comparison that matters most, an all-orders numerical cross-check for the shift, and Gauss–Hermite quadrature with 160 nodes for the Gaussian averages. Code, data and the `crinit` package are promised at github.com/Aureliuxs/finite-width-edge-of-chaos.

Two credibility caveats. First, the acknowledgements disclose that Claude Opus 5.5 was used to write and debug code, draft the article and the Supplemental Material from the author's notes, edit the text and check derivations — the author states he directed the use, revised the text and verified all output. That is commendably explicit, but it shifts a real verification burden onto the reader for a paper whose value is dense analytics; the SM is 30+ pages of algebra. Second, the paper is a single-author, non-peer-reviewed preprint making a claim ("explains why calculations and simulations have disagreed") that is strong enough to deserve independent replication of the O(1/N) coefficient.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty.** Genuinely new and well-targeted. The individual ingredients are old — the log of a product of random factors is depressed by Jensen, and the largest-Lyapunov-exponent vs mean-growth-rate split is textbook random-matrix theory (Crisanti–Paladin–Vulpiani) — but applying that split to *this specific* dispute, and noticing that the two camps were computing different objects (ensemble-averaged kernels vs single-network trajectories), is the actual contribution, and it is a good one. The resulting formula Δσ²w = −a₁/(N ∂_{σ²w} ln χ₁) is closed-form, checkable, and correctly degenerates to the known single-input results: a₁ = −1 for a linear network, a₁ = −½C_α with C_α = (5 − 2α² + 5α⁴)/(1 + α²)² for leaky ReLU (5 for ReLU), and the typical-preserving variance σ²c(N) = φ′(0)² 2² e^{−ψ(N/2)} as the q* → 0 limit. That the gradient statement falls out of a transpose argument for free is elegant rather than laboured.

**Rigor.** High in the analytics, and the honesty is above average. The paper states the validity condition N(1 − F′) ≫ 1 explicitly and shades the region in Fig. 2(a) where it fails at N = 16, rather than quietly dropping the N = 16 points. It reports that the measured shift is 13% *larger* than the first-order prediction at N = 64 and attributes this to the next order rather than hiding it. Most importantly, it volunteers the result that undercuts its own headline: at L=32 the mean-field edge of chaos at the same operating point is statistically indistinguishable from Crinit (0.9513 vs 0.9517; the 1000-pair paired test gives 0.00006 ± 0.00016, with Crinit behind in 497 of 1000 pairs), and with orthogonal weights the 1/N correction is 0.006 in σ²w and the two rules are equal in accuracy and speed at both depths. The paper says so in plain words: "Most of Crinit's gain over the standard rules therefore comes from the operating point and the first layer, not from the 1/N correction." A weaker paper would have buried that.

**Weak points that are specific to this paper.** (i) The orthogonal-weight branch has an unexplained 13–18% theory–simulation discrepancy of *opposite sign* to the Gaussian branch; the paper admits "we have not traced" it. Since the orthogonal case is presented as the mechanistic control that isolates the nonlinearity's contribution, an unexplained residual there weakens the claim that the mechanism is fully understood, not just the Gaussian case. (ii) The central operating-point parameter γ = 1 is measured, not derived, and the measured optimum is a plateau from γ = 0.5 to 1 — so "one parameter, default 1" is really "one parameter chosen from a scan on the same two datasets." (iii) The information proxy I is a Gaussian-channel capacity fitted to a cubic in ln ε₀ with the residual treated as noise; the paper is explicit that this is "a proxy for the information about input similarity, not a bound on it," and shows degree 2–5 polynomials move I by less than the statistical error, but the whole γ = 1 criterion rests on this proxy's shape, not on a first-principles argument. (iv) Table I mixes protocols: each row uses the learning rate its own pilot picked by *training* loss, diverged networks are dropped from the mean, and the counts appear as superscripts — at L=96 Crinit's tanh row is a mean over 100 networks of which three fall below 0.65, giving 0.903, while at η₀ = 1.5×10⁻³ none fail and the mean is 0.918. The reported 0.903 is the less flattering of the two numbers, which is to the author's credit, but the row-by-row protocol variation makes cross-row comparison slightly apples-to-oranges.
(v) Baseline breadth is the real soft spot. LeCun, He, Xavier, PyTorch default, typical-preserving σ²c(N) and the mean-field edge of chaos are all reasonable, and adding a *tuned-gain* arm is the right control — but "tuned gain" is a gain scan on σ²w only, at zero bias, with the first layer left standard. Table S10/S11 shows that giving the tuned zero-bias rule Crinit's first layer closes the gap entirely (0.8409/0.8703 at L=32, 0.800/0.859 at L=96 vs Crinit's 0.8400/0.8693/0.798/0.860). So the strongest honest statement is "the same first-layer trick plus a scanned gain matches Crinit," which is a weaker claim than "a rule computed without a search beats a tuned baseline." The paper does report this, but the abstract's framing ("matches or beats the standard rules with a tuned gain") reads more decisively than Table S10 supports.
(vi) Data quirks that matter: MNIST at two epochs with a 64-wide MLP is a 1998-era benchmark where 0.95 is near the ceiling, so the L=32 comparison has almost no headroom; the interesting regime is L=96 (L/N = 1.5) where standard rules collapse, and there the win is real but partly a *failure* of the baselines rather than a triumph of Crinit. Note also the ReLU-family accounting: Crinit's leaky ReLU is reported over 79 of 100 networks because 21 diverged at the pilot's learning rate, and the "0.9250" headline is that subsample; over all 100 at a third of the rate it is 0.9112(7), which is still far ahead of He's 0.491(11) but is not the same number.
(vii) Zero treatment of compute or wall-clock: "fewer steps" is measured in SGD steps to a training-accuracy threshold, with no wall-clock, no FLOPs, and no comparison to adaptive optimisers. A practitioner choosing between Crinit and, say, just using LayerNorm will not find an answer here.

**Significance.** The theory result is the durable part: it is a correct, closed-form, testable statement about where the finite-width critical point is, it reconciles two literatures that were measuring different quantities, and it costs nothing to evaluate (Gauss–Hermite quadrature plus a few Gaussian moments). The practical rule is a smaller contribution than the abstract implies, because the ablation shows the 1/N term is worth ~0.009 accuracy at L=96/N=64 and ~0 at L=32, and because the paper's own tests are small MLPs without normalisation. But the *diagnostic* value is high for anyone debugging a deep network that will not train: "your width-64 mean-field-critical init is 14× contracting over 100 layers" is a useful, quantitative statement, and σ²w = 1.31 instead of 1.00 at N=64 is a concrete number a practitioner can type in.

**Red flags, ranked.** (1) Headline-vs-ablation gap: the title result is not the source of the empirical gain, and only the fine print says so. (2) The unexplained orthogonal-weight discrepancy. (3) γ = 1 fitted on the same two datasets used for evaluation. (4) Heavy LLM involvement in derivations and drafting, disclosed but not independently checked. (5) Narrow empirical base (two 28×28 datasets, one width, two depths, plain SGD, no normalisation/skip/conv/residual). None of these are fatal; (1) is the one a reader should carry into the abstract.

## Strengths

- A crisp, correct mechanistic insight — the typical-vs-ensemble split from random-matrix theory — applied to exactly the right dispute, with both sides of the disagreement computed and shown to sit on opposite sides of the mean-field line.
- Closed-form results that are cheap to evaluate and that reduce correctly to four independent known limits (linear, ReLU, leaky ReLU, typical-preserving σ²c(N)).
- The gradient result comes free from a transpose argument rather than from a second, separate calculation — a genuinely nice piece of physics.
- Simulation methodology is the exact finite-width recursion, not a mean-field surrogate, so the "theory vs simulation" comparisons are honest tests of the expansion rather than circular.
- Statistical discipline in the training experiments: 100 networks per cell, standard errors throughout, a pilot for learning rates, a 1000-pair same-seed paired test for the comparison that most needs it, and explicit reporting of diverged-network counts.
- Unusual candour about where the gain comes from. The sentence "Most of Crinit's gain over the standard rules therefore comes from the operating point and the first layer, not from the 1/N correction" is the kind of admission that most papers in this genre omit.
- The compression-budget framing (γ in nats, collapse onto κ(q*)L across depths and widths, plateau ∝ ½log₂(1 + 1.2 N/L)) turns a vague "don't be too nonlinear" heuristic into a measurable, depth-dependent rule with a Gaussian-channel interpretation.
- Reproducibility infrastructure: exact-chain simulator, `crinit` package, public code, all-orders numerical cross-check for the shift.

## Limitations

- Fully connected networks only, at initialisation only. The paper is explicit that convolutional and residual layers change the recursions and that training moves the weights; nothing here addresses whether the finite-width shift survives in a ResNet or a transformer, where the relevant "width" is the model dimension and normalisation layers already do part of this job.
- Empirical base is two 28×28 grayscale datasets, width 64 (plus 32), depths 32/96, plain SGD with momentum, no normalisation and no skip connections. The "large margin in deep networks" is a margin over baselines that are *failing*, in a regime (L/N = 1.5) that modern architectures avoid by construction.
- The 1/N correction itself is practically negligible at tested sizes: 0.03–0.07 in σ²w at N = 64, worth 0.009(12) accuracy at L=96 and 0.0004 at L=32. It matters for the *theory* of where the critical point is, not for the accuracy of a width-64 MLP.
- First order in 1/N, with two known breakdowns: near the zero-bias end of the line, where the displacement is O(N^{−1/2}) and the linearised formula is invalid, and at small q*, where N(1 − F′) < 3 makes the expansion fail (N = 16). The orthogonal-weight case is 13–18% off with no explanation.
- The operating-point constant γ = 1 is empirical, sits on a flat plateau (0.5–1), and was selected using the same MNIST/Fashion-MNIST runs used for evaluation; there is no independent dataset for the hyperparameter.
- In deep narrow networks (L > N) the zero-bias point compresses more than the budget allows (γ = 1.2 at L=96/N=64, 1.9 at N=32), and Crinit cannot fix this with a non-negative bias variance — the paper says so, but it means the rule has a hole exactly in the regime where deep-and-narrow networks live.
- The information measure is a proxy with a cubic-fit signal model and Gaussian-noise assumption, not a bound; the whole "where to sit" argument inherits that looseness.
- No compute accounting, no wall-clock, no comparison against adaptive optimisers, normalisation layers, or learned initialisations; "trains in fewer steps" is SGD steps to a training-accuracy threshold.

## Relevance

这篇直接落在「LLM Training Stability & Observability」这条线上：它把"初始化不当 → 深度方向信号/梯度按 e^{−ℓ/N} 系统性衰减 → 深层网络训练不动"这件事做成了可计算的定量判据（宽度 64 时 σ²w 应从 1.00 抬到 1.31），并给出免搜索的 Crinit 规则与开源包。虽然实验只在小 MLP 上做，但"典型网络与集合平均的临界点相差 1/N、单次训练看到的是典型值"这一区分，对理解大规模训练中"平均 loss 正常但个别 run 崩掉/学不动"的差异有直接借鉴价值。

## Questions for Further Exploration

1. Does the O(1/N) shift survive in architectures with normalisation? LayerNorm rescales activations per example, which removes exactly the signal-size fluctuation term (the x_ℓ contribution to a₁) — is the predicted shift then just the neuron-averaging term, and does Crinit collapse to something like a scaled LayerNorm gain?
2. What is the effective N for a transformer? If width is d_model but the relevant averaging is over d_ff or over attention heads, the predicted 8/N becomes a different number, and the paper's own N/L control parameter suggests the right variable might be d/L rather than d alone.
3. Why is the orthogonal-weight shift 13–18% below the first-order theory while the Gaussian case is 13% above at N=64? The paper leaves this open; a second-order calculation (the b₁-type term for the drift) would test whether it is a truncation artefact or a missing mechanism.
4. The 1000-pair paired test at L=32 gives a difference of 0.00006 ± 0.00016 — is there *any* width at which the 1/N correction alone produces a resolved accuracy gain, or does the correction only ever matter through the operating point it enables?
5. Crinit's advantage at L=96 comes largely from the first layer being scaled to q* rather than σ²w/d_in, and Table S10 suggests what matters is the *relative SGD step size* of W₁, not the signal it starts at. Is "first layer at steady signal" a signal-propagation result or an implicit learning-rate result? The paper half-answers this (weight scaling beats input scaling because of relative step size) but does not separate the two cleanly.
6. Can the compression budget γ be derived rather than measured? κ(q*) is known in closed form; if the budget of one nat follows from a rate-distortion or information-bottleneck argument, the last empirical constant in Crinit disappears.
7. Does the avalanche prediction (survival n^{−1/2}, exponents 3/2 and 4/3) hold in trained networks away from initialisation, where the weight distribution is no longer Gaussian and the walk's steps are correlated across layers?
