> **一句话总结：** Meta 生产训练集群里的 LEAP 用「13 桶分箱 + 每桶一个 GBDT 专家 + 分箱分类器」的级联模型，在作业提交时仅凭启动配置、硬件规格和历史吞吐预测该作业在每种候选加速器上的吞吐，再按「吞吐/成本」排序交给调度器；30 次夜间重训的中位 w20 为 77.0%，在旧查表法覆盖的 946 个作业上为 85.1% vs 53.2%，半年内可路由 GPU-hours 覆盖率从 58% 升到 95%，估计省下约 3% 训练算力成本。

> **TL;DR:** LEAP is a production throughput predictor at Meta that ranks candidate accelerators per training job at submission time, using only launch config, 12 hardware specs, and the job's own prior throughput — no profiling run and no computation graph. On the internal job-attempt table (≈25k attempts/day, 660 model types, 20+ hardware types; 30-day train window ≈750k attempts, next-day holdout), a 13-bin GBDT cascade reaches a median **w20 = 77.0%** over 30 nightly retrains (73.5–80.6), and **85.1% vs 53.2%** for the lookup table it replaced on the 946 jobs that table covered (+31.9 pts; the table covered only 13.3% of jobs at all). Tuned GBDTs beat every alternative on the same held-out day: TabPFN v2 79.1, Gemma 3 27B fine-tuned 69.0, Llama 4 LoRA 50.8, LightGBM 69.4, and a re-implemented DNNPerf GNN ties at 77.4 with the graph contributing +0.25 pts (95% CI −0.21 to 0.71). Worth reading if you build fleet-level performance models or schedulers; the negative results and the cold-start measurements are the most transferable part, the 3% cost saving is the least.

## 核心贡献

- **把「提交时吞吐预测」做成了一个生产服务**：LEAP 在作业启动路径上同步返回每个候选硬件类型的吞吐预测与「吞吐/单位成本」排序，替代了只覆盖 19 个模型类型、仅对 13.3% 作业给出估计的查表法。
- **分箱级联回归器**：把对数吞吐切成 13 个等频桶，用 EM 式迭代（≤3 轮）让每桶的 GBDT 专家专注于一个吞吐量级，再用一个分箱分类器在服务时选择专家；单次训练共拟合 52 个回归器。
- **量化了「什么真正提升精度」**：在同一个 held-out 日上做逐项消融，架构特征 +4.1、作业历史 +2.5、吞吐比值特征 +1.8、更深的直方图 GBDT +2.1、分箱级联 +0.5，合计 +11.0 个点（72.3 → 83.3）。
- **一组干净的负结果**：TabPFN v2、微调后的 Gemma 3 / Llama 4、Qwen3-Embedding 编码器（冻结或 LoRA）全部不如调好的 GBDT；重实现的 DNNPerf 图神经网络只能打平，却要 GPU 和约 400 GB 图缓存。
- **冷启动的定量刻画**：留出一个硬件类型会让 w20 掉 37 个点（随机删同样多的行只掉不到 2 个点），即使该硬件的规格落在训练分布内；留出一个模型家族时级联模型掉 49.7 点，图模型掉 37.0 点。
- **部署侧的经验教训**：在训练窗口内部打分曾把精度高估 20 个点以上；夜间波动约 7 个点，因此 1–2 个点的差异应视为不可判定。

## Key Contributions

- **A production service, not a benchmark result.** LEAP runs inside the job launcher as a linked library, called once per launch, returning a per-candidate preference list that the cluster scheduler treats as a soft preference. It handles ≈25,000 job attempts/day across ~660 model types and 20+ hardware types, and its nightly retrain publishes versioned artifacts behind a stable pointer.
- **Binned cascade regression for a 7-decade target.** Throughput spans seven orders of magnitude (91% of rows within two decades; per-hardware p95/p5 ratios of 42× to 701×), so LEAP regresses `ln q` under the pinball loss at τ=0.5 (conditional median), splits the target into 13 equal-frequency bins, alternates expert fitting and row reassignment EM-style, then trains a bin classifier so serving needs no label.
- **Ablation-first methodology.** Every model and feature variant is scored on the same held-out day with the same 30-day training window; the paper explicitly refuses to treat 1–2 point differences as signal because night-to-night variation is ~7 points.
- **Negative results on the fashionable alternatives.** TabPFN v2 (79.1), fine-tuned Gemma 3 27B (69.0), LoRA-tuned Llama 4 Scout (50.8), LightGBM (69.4), and five Qwen3-Embedding encoder configurations (best 73.6) all land below the deployed cascade on comparable held-out days.
- **A cold-start result with direct operational consequence.** Removing one hardware type from training costs 37 w20 points while removing the same number of rows at random costs under 2 — so a new accelerator cannot be ranked until it has run real jobs, and must be bootstrapped deliberately.
- **Deployment telemetry as evidence.** Coverage of eligible training GPU-hours rose 58% → 95% (77M GPU-hours) over H1 2026; jobs that actually landed on the top-ranked type were 19.9% of jobs but 30.0% of GPU-hours and 26.2% of cost-weighted usage; estimated fleet compute-cost reduction ≈3%.

## Methodology

**Problem framing.** In a mixed-vendor fleet, a "fungible" job can run on several accelerator types whose throughput and price differ by large factors. Placing it badly produces no error — the job succeeds, just slower and more expensively — so the loss is invisible in job status. The routing chain is: launcher determines eligible types → LEAP predicts throughput per type and ranks by predicted throughput / computational cost → scheduler resolves the ranking against live capacity. LEAP owns only the middle stage, which is also its main scope limitation.

**Features (73 columns: 56 numeric, 17 categorical).** Three joined sources: job-attempt records (launch config + measured outcome), cluster allocation logs, and a static hardware table. Hardware is encoded as 12 numeric specs (peak compute, memory capacity/bandwidth, interconnect bandwidth, …) plus a vendor id, so a new type enters as a new point in a familiar space. Job history per *job family* (normalized job name with run-specific identifiers stripped) supplies previous log throughput, its running mean and std, the count of prior observations, and days since last observation. The three feature groups that matter are job scale, job history, and model architecture; permutation importance gives job scale 33.2%, job history 29.8%, architecture 21.1%, config/package 10.2%, and **hardware specs only 5.8%** — with 18 of 73 columns having no measurable effect.

**Model.** A single regressor fits the extremes poorly, so LEAP uses a cascade: split `y = ln q` into K=13 equal-frequency bins; repeat {fit one pinball-loss GBDT expert per bin; reassign each row to the bin whose expert predicts it best} for ≤3 iterations or until <2% of rows move; refit experts on final bins; train a GBDT bin classifier `g` to predict the bin from features alone. Serving computes `q̂(x) = exp(f_k̂(x))` with `k̂ = argmax_k g_k(x)`. Rationale: the classifier only has to place a job in the right order-of-magnitude band, which is easier than exact regression, and each expert then specializes. A training run fits up to 52 regressors; the published artifact holds the 13 final experts plus the classifier.

**Serving-time engineering.** Because no label exists at submission, all inputs must come from static config, the hardware table, or data shipped in the artifact. History features would otherwise need a keyed lookup on the launch path, so each artifact embeds a snapshot of prior-throughput statistics for up to 250,000 job families; only recency is recomputed at serving. Unknown families get an explicit cold-start imputation, not zero. Any failure (empty candidate set, missing feature, unloadable artifact) degrades to an **empty preference list**, which the scheduler reads as "no opinion" and places as if LEAP did not exist. Runtime switches allow rolling routing out or back one model type at a time.

**Nightly retraining.** Each completed attempt writes its measured throughput into the daily-partitioned feature table and thus becomes tomorrow's label. A recurring job trains on the trailing 30 days (≈750,000 attempts) with a window computed relative to the run date (a fixed start date would eventually fall outside retention), holds out the most recent day, and publishes an immutable versioned artifact.

**Evaluation protocol.** Metric is `w20`: the share of job attempts whose prediction is within ±20% of measured throughput, pooled over all model types and weighted by attempt count. MAPE is rejected (a few tiny-throughput jobs dominate) and R² on the raw scale is rejected (near zero on many nights while w20 > 75%). Headline numbers come from 30 consecutive nightly retrains, not one run; cross-method comparisons always share a held-out day.

## Results & Highlights

- **Headline accuracy over 30 nightly retrains: median w20 = 77.0%**, with individual nights from 73.5% to 80.6% (a ~7-point spread). Each point is a model trained on the prior 30 days (≈750k attempts) and scored on the next, unseen day — so this is the deployed configuration, not a tuned best run.
- **Against the lookup table it replaced: 85.1% vs 53.2% w20** on the 946 jobs (of a 7,131-job, 276-model-type held-out subset) that the table covered — **+31.9 points**, and the gap widens at tighter tolerances: **w10 72.1 vs 33.4**, **w15 80.6 vs 45.0**, with median predicted/actual ratio **1.00 vs 1.11**. Crucially, the table only produced an estimate for **13.3%** of attempts (93% of that day's model types had no entry), while LEAP covers 100% of requests.
- **Tighter tolerances on a separate held-out day:** 68.9% within ±10%, 42.9% within ±5%, median absolute percentage error 6.9% — the tail of the error distribution is much heavier than the w20 headline suggests.
- **Ablation on one fixed August 2026 held-out day (w20):** 72.3 baseline → +job-history 74.8 → +throughput-ratio 76.6 → **+architecture features 80.7** → +deeper histogram GBDT 82.8 → +binned cascade 83.3. Total **+11.0 points**; the single largest jump is the 44 architecture summary values (+4.1), and the cascade that the paper's architecture section is built around adds only **+0.5**.
- **Model families on that same day:** TabPFN v2 **79.1**, Gemma 3 27B fine-tuned **69.0**, Llama 4 Scout LoRA **50.8**, LightGBM **69.4** — all below the 83.3 cascade. LightGBM scoring 2.9 points *below* the scikit-learn baseline on identical features is the odd one out.
- **Graph neural model (re-implemented DNNPerf), same jobs, three days, ten seeds:** **77.4** vs **77.4** for the cascade; without the graph it reaches 77.1, so the operator graph is worth **+0.25 points (95% CI −0.21 to 0.71)** — a clean null. It was not deployed because it needs a GPU at serving time and a **~400 GB** graph cache.
- **Language-model encoders (Qwen3-Embedding) are strictly worse:** best configuration 0.6B + LoRA at **73.6** vs cascade **77.3** (−3.7); a 7× larger 4B encoder is *worse* at **70.7**; adding the embedding to the GBDT's inputs reaches 72.4 (−4.9). At ±5% no encoder exceeds **30.4%** vs **42.9%** for the cascade, and best-encoder median error is **9.3%** vs **6.9%**.
- **Cold start is the dominant failure mode:** holding out 3 hardware types costs the graph model **−37.0** w20 while deleting the same number of rows at random costs **−1.7**; holding out 3 model families costs **−22.2**. On the one family where both were refit, the cascade loses **49.7** points and the graph model **37.0**.
- **Deployment impact:** ranked GPU-hour coverage 58% → 95% over H1 2026 (77M GPU-hours); in a six-day August 2026 window (~200k jobs), the top-ranked type was actually used for **19.9%** of jobs, **30.0%** of GPU-hours, and **26.2%** of cost-weighted usage; estimated total fleet training-compute saving **≈3%**, against a fleet where routable jobs are about half of training cost.

## Authors & Credibility

Six authors, all at Meta (Wei Sun and Sreen Tallam listed as "work done at Meta"), corresponding author Hossein Askari. This is an industry systems paper reporting on a deployed internal service, not an academic benchmark paper. That provenance cuts both ways: the scale and the deployment numbers are things almost no academic group can produce (25k attempts/day, 660 model types, 20+ hardware types, six months of production, 30 consecutive nightly retrains), and the operational details — artifact versioning, history snapshots, empty-list degradation, per-model-type rollout switches — read like they were written by people who had to keep the thing running. The cost is that nothing is externally checkable: no code, no data, no artifact, no public benchmark, and the ablation tables cannot be re-derived by a reader. Citations are current and appropriate (Habitat, DNNPerf, Gavel, Sia, Pollux, TabPFN, Gorishniy's numerical-embedding work, Bao as the "learned component under a conventional controller" precedent). The paper also volunteers unflattering facts that a weaker team would have buried: a >20-point accuracy overstatement from scoring inside the training window, a null result for the GNN they built, hardware features contributing only 5.8% to a decision that is entirely about hardware, and an unresolved serving/offline feature skew in future work. That candor is the strongest credibility signal here.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty is modest; the contribution is the evidence, not the method.** A 13-bin cascade of GBDTs with an EM-style reassignment loop is a sensible engineering trick, and the paper is honest that its own ablation values it at +0.5 w20 (82.8 → 83.3). Nothing here is a new learning algorithm; the value is a carefully controlled, same-day, 30-retrain evaluation at a scale nobody outside a hyperscaler can match, plus a set of negative results that are more useful than most positive ones.

**The headline metric and the money point in opposite directions.** w20 weights every job attempt equally, which the paper states plainly in Limitations — but the consequence deserves more weight than a sentence. The workload is extremely long-tailed (560 least-frequent model types = 22% of attempts), so 77.0% is dominated by many small jobs, while "a few large jobs account for most GPU-hours." A model that is 77% accurate on attempts and, say, 60% accurate on the GPU-hour-weighted distribution would still produce the reported 3% saving, and readers cannot tell which. The paper reports attempt-weighted numbers throughout and never gives a GPU-hour-weighted w20, even though the feature table plainly contains what is needed to compute it. That is the single most important missing number.

**The 3% saving is an estimate without a counterfactual.** No A/B, no held-out routing policy, no confidence interval, no description of how the counterfactual placement was constructed. It is confounded with everything else that moved over H1 2026 — coverage itself went 58% → 95%, fleet composition changed, and the scheduler only honored the top-ranked type for 19.9% of jobs (capacity-limited), so the realized benefit depends on an adherence rate the model does not control. The number is plausible and probably right in sign; it should be read as "our internal estimate," not as a measured effect.

**The baseline is a strawman, and no cheap statistical baseline is offered.** The rule engine is a four-key lookup table (`model_type, num_trainers, batch_size, hardware_type`) that covers 19 model types and 13.3% of attempts, and the paper itself shows the key does not determine throughput (for 56% of attempts the key's p10–p90 range exceeds 1.5×). Beating it by 31.9 points is real but nearly free. What is missing is the obvious cheap competitor: the per-`model_type` median log throughput, or a ridge regression on log features. If a 20-line baseline gets 70% w20, the case for a 52-regressor cascade trained nightly is much weaker. Gavel- and Sia-style measurement-based estimates are dismissed in Related Work as needing in-flight measurements, but they are never scored on the same held-out day, so the comparison stays rhetorical.

**The cross-family comparisons span different held-out days, and the abstract does not say so.** Table 1 and Table 3 put the cascade at 77.3; Table 2 puts it at 83.3 on a day the authors describe as "easier than the average night." Within-table comparisons are sound; the abstract's "at least as high as every alternative model we tested" quietly aggregates across tables scored on different days, and the night-to-night spread is ~7 points — larger than several of the reported gaps. TabPFN's 79.1 and the best encoder's 73.6 are not on a common footing with 83.3, and a reader skimming Table 2 will mis-rank them.

**The largest single gain is also the most suspicious one.** The 44 architecture summary values (layer counts, arithmetic intensity) buy +4.1 points — more than job history — and are plausibly a near-unique fingerprint of the model type, i.e. a memorization channel rather than a generalization signal. Two facts point that way: the cascade loses 49.7 points when a model family is held out (worse than the graph model's 37.0), and the paper's own explanation is that "the bin classifier has no basis for assigning an unfamiliar family to a bin." The test that would settle it — re-run the ablation with architecture features removed *under family holdout* — is never run. Without it, the headline feature gain and the headline cold-start failure may be the same phenomenon counted twice.

**Some baselines look undertuned, which weakens the "model family doesn't matter" conclusion.** LightGBM at 69.4 vs scikit-learn GBDT at 72.3 on identical features is not the expected ordering for two implementations of the same algorithm; no tuning budget, hyperparameter search, or parity check is reported for either. Llama 4 at 50.8 is the weakest kind of LLM strawman: serialize the config into up to 12k tokens and LoRA-tune the model to emit a number as text. The paper even diagnoses the cause (numeric tokens do not preserve magnitude) but never runs the obvious fix — classifying over the same 13 log bins the cascade already uses, which would remove the magnitude problem entirely. "Fine-tuned LLMs are much worse at this" is therefore a claim about one unengineered prompting format, not about the model class. TabPFN's 79.1 also depends on a retrieval scheme (most-similar training rows as context) that is never ablated for context size or retrieval metric.

**The cascade is a poor trade that the paper does not defend.** It buys +0.5 w20 over a single deeper histogram GBDT while requiring 52 regressor fits per night, a second model (the bin classifier), and a new failure mode on unseen model families (−49.7 vs −37.0 for the graph model). If the cascade were dropped, the system would be simpler, retrain faster, and degrade more gracefully, at the cost of half a point — well inside the paper's own 1–2 point "inconclusive" band. The architecture section is organized around the cascade, so this trade is never priced.

**A live serving/offline skew is disclosed and then left open.** Future work says the serving path "should supply the parallelism and scaling arguments that the model uses, or the model should stop using them." That is an admission that the deployed model consumes features the serving path may not faithfully provide — exactly the training-serving skew the paper cites Sculley et al. for. If those features are defaulted at serving, the reported w20 (computed offline, where all features are present) overstates production accuracy by an unstated amount. This belongs in Limitations, not in a forward-looking bullet.

**Smaller gaps.** No latency numbers for the routing call, despite "it runs on the CPU inside the launcher" being the central argument against the GNN — a p50/p99 would settle it. No label-noise ceiling, so the headroom behind the remaining 23% miss rate is unknown (they list measuring it as future work). No per-hardware-type error breakdown, even though ranking quality — not absolute accuracy — is what the scheduler consumes; a model with mediocre w20 but good pairwise ordering would serve the purpose equally well and might be much cheaper. And the paper never reports whether ranking by predicted throughput/cost beats ranking by raw predicted throughput, i.e. whether the cost normalization itself contributes anything.

## Strengths

- **Scale and duration that cannot be faked.** 25,000 attempts/day, ~660 model types, 20+ hardware types, 30 consecutive nightly retrains, six months of production telemetry. The ablation is run under the exact nightly protocol, not a curated offline split.
- **Uncommon reporting honesty.** The >20-point overstatement from in-window scoring, the ~7-point night-to-night variance, the null GNN result with a confidence interval that includes zero, the negative LLM results, 18 of 73 features with no measurable effect, and hardware specs at 5.8% importance in a hardware-selection paper — all of these are reported against the authors' own interest.
- **A genuinely useful cold-start result.** The contrast between −37.0 for a held-out hardware type and −1.7 for an equal number of random rows is a clean, quotable measurement, and it implies a concrete operational rule (bootstrap new accelerators with representative jobs before routing them) rather than a vague warning.
- **Systems details that transfer.** Embedding a 250k-family history snapshot in the model artifact to avoid a keyed lookup on the launch path; degrading to an empty preference list so the scheduler falls back to its default; per-model-type rollout switches; versioned artifacts behind a stable pointer with a date-relative training window. These are the parts a team building the same thing will actually copy.
- **The right metric argument.** The rejection of MAPE and raw-scale R² is well reasoned (ratios dominated by tiny-throughput jobs; R² near zero while w20 > 75%) and w20 is defensible as "close enough to rank correctly."
- **A counterintuitive finding worth propagating:** what predicts throughput is job scale and the job's own history (63% of importance combined), not the hardware specification (5.8%) — even though hardware identity is the decision variable. That reframes the problem from "model the hardware" to "model the job's scaling behavior and transfer it across hardware."

## Limitations

- **w20 is attempt-weighted, not GPU-hour- or cost-weighted.** The paper says so, but every headline number inherits the bias toward many small jobs, and no GPU-hour-weighted accuracy is reported. The metric that matches the 3% saving is absent.
- **The 3% cost reduction is an internal estimate with no counterfactual, no confidence interval, and no described methodology.** It cannot be verified or reproduced.
- **Cold start is unsolved and load-bearing.** New hardware types are unrankable before they run real jobs (−37.0 w20), new model families cost the cascade ~50 points, and the only fix offered (route unfamiliar graphs to a GNN) is a proposal, not a result.
- **Single-fleet, single-vendor-stack evidence.** No code, data, or artifacts; results depend on Meta's job taxonomy, hardware mix, and scheduler semantics. Whether 77% w20 transfers to a fleet with different workload composition is untested.
- **Serving/offline feature skew is disclosed but unresolved**, so production accuracy may be lower than reported.
- **No label-noise ceiling**, so the paper cannot say how much of the remaining error is irreducible measurement noise in "measured throughput" (which itself depends on co-tenancy, stragglers, and network state).
- **Baseline breadth is thin**: one lookup table, one GBDT family, one TabPFN configuration, one LLM prompting format, one GNN re-implementation. No measurement-based scheduler baseline, no simple statistical baseline.
- **Ranking quality is never evaluated directly**, even though the downstream consumer is a ranker, not a regressor.

## Relevance

归入 **Compute Infrastructure for Large-Scale ML**：异构集群的调度与吞吐预测，正是该方向的核心问题。更值得一提的是它给出了一种**静默失效**的范例——作业被放到次优硬件上照样成功、不报错、不留痕，效率损失只能靠预测模型去发现，这与你关注的「无声故障检测」在方法论上是同一类问题：先建立可观测的基线，再把偏离基线的行为当作信号。

## Questions for Further Exploration

1. What is the GPU-hour-weighted w20, and does the 3% saving survive if the model is optimized for cost-weighted rather than attempt-weighted accuracy?
2. How much of the +4.1 points from architecture features is memorization? Re-run the ablation with those features removed under the model-family holdout — does the cascade's −49.7 shrink toward the graph model's −37.0?
3. What does a trivial baseline achieve — per-`model_type` median log throughput, or ridge regression on log features? If it reaches ~70% w20, is the 52-regressor cascade worth its operational cost?
4. Why does LightGBM (69.4) underperform scikit-learn's GBDT (72.3) on identical features? A tuning-budget parity check would either vindicate or undercut the "model family doesn't matter" conclusion.
5. Would a fine-tuned LLM that classifies over the same 13 log bins (instead of emitting a number as text) close the gap to 83.3, or is the failure about tabular numerics rather than output format?
6. Does ranking by predicted throughput alone differ from ranking by throughput/cost? How often do the two orderings disagree, and which one does the scheduler's realized placement correlate with?
7. What are the p50/p99 latencies of a routing call, and how do they compare with the GNN's serving cost — enough to justify the CPU-only design on its own?
8. How should a new hardware type be bootstrapped? The paper suggests "a small set of representative jobs"; how many, chosen how, and what w20 does that buy on day one?
9. Can the bin classifier's cold-start failure be fixed by falling back to a single global regressor when the predicted bin distribution is flat or the model family is unseen?
10. Is "measured throughput" itself noisy enough to bound achievable w20? A repeated-measurement study on identical configurations would give the label-noise ceiling the authors list as future work.
