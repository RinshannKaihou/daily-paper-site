> **一句话总结：** 作者把量化、权重陈旧、权重噪声三类"训练-推理不匹配"统一到同一个尺度（rollout 引擎与训练器之间的每 token KL，nats/token），在 7B 学生上跑 17 种 rollout 配置发现 OPD 的失配预算约为 [0.184, 0.39] nats/token——是标准 BF16 引擎（0.0008）的 230 倍到 488 倍，而同样引擎下 REINFORCE 与 GRPO 分别在约 12 倍和 60 倍处崩溃；同时给出一个 24 分钟、单卡就能筛掉候选引擎的准入检查，以及一个反直觉的机理结论：FP8 KV cache 的失配 93%–99% 来自第一层，只把这一层留在 BF16 就能保住质量并拿到 1.93 倍的 KV cache 容量。

> **TL;DR:** A measurement study of training–inference mismatch in on-policy distillation (OPD), where the rollout engine runs FP8 or stale weights while the trainer stays in BF16. On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (teacher: Skywork-OR1-Math-7B), all 12 configurations with mismatch D ≤ 0.184 nats/token end within −5.0% to +6.4% of the standard run's held-out KL on 530 AIME24/MATH-500 problems (0.0481 nats/token, 60.93% accuracy), while two weight-noise runs at D ≈ 0.39 end +23.3% and +23.7% worse — an empirical "mismatch budget" of [0.184, 0.39], i.e. 230×–488× the standard engine's 0.0008. Under the same engines REINFORCE and GRPO break at roughly 12× and 60× that baseline. The actionable parts: keeping only the first layer's KV cache in BF16 removes 93%–99% of FP8-cache mismatch (1.93× more KV-cache tokens, same quality), an offline admission check called all 8 pre-registered verdicts correctly in 24 minutes on one GPU versus 5.5–6 hours on eight, and FP8 + 8-version-stale rollouts reach the synchronous run's final KL in 3.69 h instead of 5.98 h (1.62×) at 60.46% vs 61.54% accuracy. Read it if you run OPD or async RL and are deciding whether FP8 rollouts are safe; skip it if you need results beyond 200 steps, beyond math, or beyond two model families.

## 核心贡献

- **统一的失配度量**：把量化（bit 宽度）、异步（陈旧版本数）、权重噪声三种无法直接比较的扰动，统一折算成 rollout 引擎分布 q 与训练器分布 p_θ 在引擎实际访问前缀上的每 token KL（nats/token，式 2）。标准 BF16 vLLM 引擎本身就有 0.0008 nats/token 的底噪，于是"200 倍失配"这类说法第一次有了可比口径。
- **提出并实测"失配预算"（mismatch budget）**：定义质量判据 Δ(c)（held-out KL 相对标准 run 的变化率，τ = 0.20）与经验预算区间 B_emp(τ) = [通过组的最大值, 失败组的最小值]。7B 学生上 B_emp(0.20) = [0.184, 0.39] nats/token；12 个 D ≤ 0.184 的配置全部通过，D ≈ 0.39 的两条噪声 run 全部失败。
- **跨模型验证"预算不可迁移"**：1.5B 学生的每层 FP8 失配（0.36–0.39）反而比 7B 的同类配置（0.65–0.69）更低，但 held-out KL 恶化 +420% 到 +479%、准确率掉 10.7–10.9 个点，而 7B 在相近失配 0.39 处只恶化 +23%。Llama-3.2-3B 每层 Model Optimizer FP8 的失配仅 0.0098、恶化 +5.6%，直接通过。
- **定位到"第一层 KV cache"这一具体元凶**：在 7B 上，FP8 KV cache 在全部 7 种权重/激活精度组合里都主导失配，而第一层的 cache 单独贡献了其中 93%–99%；只把第一层 KV cache 留在 BF16，vLLM FP8 cache 的离线估计从 0.52 降到 0.0037 nats/token。作者用 DeepSeek 蒸馏模型缺少 QK normalization、存在 key-projection bias、首层 key 幅度远大于其他层来解释，并用 keys-only 仿真把该层效应的 82% 归给首层 key 的舍入。
- **提出分钟级准入检查（admission check）**：候选引擎只生成 2 条 response × 16 道题，由标准 BF16 引擎重打分得到离线估计 D̂(c)，再用安全系数 α = 1.5 与准入线 L = 0.41 判定。6 个配置单卡 24 分钟筛完（一次完整训练是 8 卡 5.5–6 小时），8 个预先固定的判定全部与真实训练结果一致（其中 5 个来自离线检查）。
- **量化 OPD 与 RL 的失配耐受差异，并给出机理解释**：OPD 的期望更新在 p_θ = p_T 处恒为零（附录 D.2 给出单前缀下的证明），所以失配改变的是"路径"而非"目标"；RL 的误差项是 R·δ，不随学生接近教师而消失。据此解释为何 REINFORCE 在 OPD 能通过的 3 个配置上全部崩溃、GRPO 崩溃 2 个。
- **指出 per-token 校正的结构性盲区**：把失配误差拆成"逐 token 误差 T"与"前缀漂移 S"，从通过噪声级（0.097）到失败噪声级（0.39），S 增长 5.3 倍而 T 只增长 2.1 倍；分数中心化（score centering）与截断重要性采样都只能作用于 T，实测对 OPD 无益甚至有害。

## Key Contributions

- **A common scale for mismatch.** Quantization is expressed in bits, asynchrony in stale versions, and numerics in nothing at all; the paper reduces all three to the expected per-token KL between the engine's distribution q and the trainer's p_θ over the prefixes the engine actually visits (Eq. 2, nats/token). The unmodified BF16 vLLM engine sits at 0.0008, which makes "230× the standard engine" a meaningful statement for the first time in this literature.
- **An empirical mismatch budget, measured rather than assumed.** With a pre-registered quality rule (Δ(c) ≥ 20% relative held-out-KL increase plus a problem-paired bootstrap interval excluding zero), the 7B student passes at every D ≤ 0.184 and fails at D ≈ 0.39, giving B_emp(0.20) = [0.184, 0.39] nats/token. The bracket is explicitly a transition range between tested configurations, not a threshold.
- **Evidence that the budget does not transfer across students.** The 1.5B student tolerates *less* absolute mismatch than the 7B despite introducing *less* of it, and Llama-3.2-3B tolerates every-layer FP8 at D ≈ 0.01 that would be catastrophic elsewhere. The paper's conclusion — calibrate per student, never infer safety from bit width or staleness — is the practically important one.
- **Layer-level diagnosis with a falsifiable mechanism.** 93%–99% of FP8-KV-cache mismatch is attributable to the first layer across seven recipes; a keys-only emulation attributes ~82% of that layer's effect to key rounding; the explanation (key-projection bias, absent QK normalization, anomalously large first-layer keys in DeepSeek-R1 distills) predicts that Llama (no bias) and Qwen3 (QK-normalized) should be insensitive, and both are.
- **A cheap admission check.** Two responses × 16 problems per candidate, re-scored by the standard engine, gives an offline estimate that separated all tested passing (D̂ ≤ 0.030) from failing (D̂ ≥ 0.408) engines, with a boundary at L/α ≈ 0.27. Eight of eight pre-registered decisions matched the training outcomes.
- **A mechanism for why OPD differs from RL, plus a negative result on inherited fixes.** The teacher-anchored advantage makes the expected update vanish at p_θ = p_T regardless of which engine samples, so mismatch perturbs the path but not the fixed point. Consequently score centering and truncated importance sampling — both standard RL remedies — do not help OPD, and score centering actually leaves held-out KL 2.3%–3.7% *above* uncentered OPD under weight noise.
- **A negative result about where the damage lives.** Under weight noise the engine visits different prefixes, and the resulting prefix-shift error S grows 5.3× from a passing to a failing noise level while per-token error T grows only 2.1×. Correcting S would require reweighting whole responses, which the authors estimate at ~10²⁷ responses per effective sample even at the smallest noise level — so staying inside the budget remains the only practical remedy.

## Methodology

**Setup.** Three student–teacher pairs from two families: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B ← Skywork-OR1-Math-7B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B ← JustRL-DeepSeek-1.5B, and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct ← GT-Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct-MATH. Each student is distilled from an RL-fine-tuned version of itself for 200 steps. Rollouts come from vLLM 0.19.1, training from verl; the trainer, teacher and all checkpoints stay in BF16, and only the rollout engine is perturbed. Hardware is two nodes of four NVIDIA GH200 GPUs; one node hosts the teacher, the other hosts the trainer plus four rollout replicas.

**Training recipe.** 8 prompts × 8 samples per step from DAPO-Math-17k at temperature 1, up to 16,384 response tokens, one AdamW step per batch at lr 1e-6, weight decay 0.01, gradient clipping 1.0. Loss is verl's k1 estimator in policy-gradient form with the teacher-log-ratio advantage A_t = log p_T(y_t|y_<t) − log p_θ(y_t|y_<t); no task reward, KL penalty, entropy bonus, or rollout importance correction. A 200-step run takes 5.5–6.0 hours on eight GPUs; in BF16, generation plus teacher scoring is 76% of the step time on the 7B student, which is the economic motivation for the whole paper.

**Mismatch measurement.** At each step, over the N_n tokens the engine sampled, d_n = (1/N_n) Σ [log q(y_t|y_<t) − log p_θ(y_t|y_<t)], an estimate of E[KL(q‖p_θ)] on the engine's visited prefixes. D(c) averages d_n over steps 1–200. Because the trainer recomputes the student's log-probabilities and the advantage from the trainer's own weights, the engine changes *which tokens and prefixes* enter training, not the advantage assigned at a given prefix — a distinction that becomes central in §6.

**Quality rule.** Held-out KL K_x(c) is the student's per-token KL to the teacher on 530 held-out math problems (AIME24 + MATH-500), always evaluated with the standard BF16 engine regardless of which engine trained. Δ(c) = K̄(c)/K̄(0) − 1. A run fails if Δ(c) ≥ τ = 0.20 *and* the 95% problem-paired bootstrap interval for its gap excludes zero; τ = 0.20 is well above the ≤1.5% run-to-run spread, and for scale the standard run reduces held-out KL from 0.0965 to 0.0481, so a 20% increase undoes about a fifth of the gain. Accuracy (AIME24, AIME25, non-overlapping AMC23, 16 samples, ±2-point intervals) is a secondary rule requiring a ≥5-point drop with an interval excluding zero; only the primary rule defines the budget. The rule and all readings were entered in a dated, append-only pre-registration plan before the corresponding jobs ran.

**Rollout configurations (7B, 17 configs).** (i) Quantization: NVIDIA Model Optimizer FP8 (weights + activations + calibrated FP8 attention cache), NVFP4 (4-bit weights/activations, FP8 KV cache, *emulated* in BF16 so quality-only), and vLLM's own FP8 KV cache with BF16 weights/activations — each every-layer and with the first (or first and last) layer's KV cache kept in BF16. (ii) Staleness: k ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8} versions old via verl's fully asynchronous mode, plus a colocated run capped at 2 versions. (iii) Weight noise: a fixed Gaussian offset σ·s_w·ε_w added to each of the 196 projection matrices at every weight sync, with σ ∈ {0.0665, 0.15, 0.234, 0.325} landing at D = 0.055, 0.097, 0.184, 0.39. (iv) Two combinations of FP8 with k = 4 and k = 8.

**Admission check.** A candidate engine generates 2 responses on each of 16 math problems; the standard BF16 engine re-scores the same tokens with the same student weights, giving D̂(c) (Eq. 5). The rule is admit iff α·D̂(c) < L with α = 1.5 (from a 100-step pilot showing in-training mismatch up to 1.47× the offline estimate) and L = 0.41 nats/token (from two pilot runs passing at step 100 at ~0.41), i.e. an effective boundary of 0.27. No teacher scoring and no training run is needed for the candidate, though the budget and line themselves require per-student calibration.

**RL comparison.** GRPO and REINFORCE train the same 7B student on a 0/1 boxed-answer reward with no teacher, 16 problems × 8 responses per step, lr 1e-6, no KL penalty and no importance correction, 200 steps, each under four rollout configurations plus its own standard-engine baseline. A run "breaks" if held-out DAPO accuracy on 1,000 problems drops ≥5 points with an interval excluding zero, or entropy/length collapses with held-out confirmation. Notably, neither standard RL run improved held-out accuracy measurably by step 100, so the comparison is about failure, not learning gains.

**Mechanism analysis.** A gradient probe computes the exact expected OPD loss gradient at fixed prefixes in closed form (verified against autograd to 5.2e-5 relative error) and decomposes it into G_KL, G_bias and G_drift; a prefix-shift experiment splits the update error into per-token error T and prefix shift S using 64 held-out problems × 4 responses with a redraw placebo.

## Results & Highlights

- **The budget bracket is [0.184, 0.39] nats/token on held-out KL.** On the 7B student, all 12 configurations with D ≤ 0.184 nats/token finish within −5.0% to +6.4% of the standard BF16 run's step-200 held-out KL of 0.0481 nats/token (accuracy 60.93%), while both weight-noise runs at D ≈ 0.39 finish +23.28% and +23.69% worse (intervals [+19.92, +26.60] and [+20.50, +26.89]). That is 230×–488× the standard engine's 0.0008 nats/token, and the bracket is a transition range, not a threshold — no run was placed inside it.
- **Every-layer quantization fails, but only one configuration trips the accuracy rule.** Every-layer FP8 and FP8-cache engines reach D = 0.637 and 0.680 and end +57.96% and +53.92% worse in held-out KL, with accuracy −3.41 and −3.63 points; every-layer NVFP4 reaches D = 0.563 and ends +59.66% worse at −5.21 points, the single accuracy failure by the pre-registered ≥5-point rule. Under every-layer FP8 the rollout answers are "almost never correct from the start", so the student is distilling from responses it could not have produced.
- **The same bit width costs the 1.5B student far more than the 7B.** Two every-layer FP8 recipes on the 1.5B student produce D = 0.36–0.39 — *lower* than the 7B's 0.65–0.69 — yet end at Δ(c) = +419.7% and +478.5% with accuracy −10.71 and −10.87 points, whereas 7B weight-noise runs at the same D ≈ 0.39 end at only +23%. Keeping the 1.5B student's first-layer KV cache in BF16 removes the damage entirely (Δ(c) = −4.3%, accuracy +0.53). The paper is careful to note this comparison confounds model and mismatch source, so it argues against transferring the budget rather than proving a model-tolerance difference.
- **FP8-cache mismatch is a one-layer problem.** Across the seven FP8-cache recipes in Figure 5, the first layer's KV cache accounts for 93%–99% of the total mismatch, and it dominates at every weight/activation precision (W8A16 through W4A4). On the 7B student, retaining that single layer in BF16 drops vLLM's offline estimate from 0.52 to 0.0037 nats/token; on Llama-3.2-3B, every-layer Model Optimizer FP8 only reaches D = 0.0098 and passes at +5.6% held-out KL.
- **The admission check called 8/8 pre-registered decisions and costs ~1/700 of a training run.** Offline estimates fell at D̂ ≤ 0.030 for every passing engine and D̂ ≥ 0.408 for every failing one, so any line in between — including the budget's own lower end 0.184 — reproduces the record. Six configurations took 24 minutes on one GPU, against 5.5–6 hours on eight GPUs for a single training run: roughly 0.4 GPU-hours versus 276 GPU-hours for the same six verdicts.
- **Asynchrony gives 1.62× throughput and then saturates.** Updates per hour rise from 34.8 (synchronous) to 43.3 (k = 1), 53.4 (k = 2), 56.5 (k = 4, 1.624×) and 55.2 (k = 8, 1.586×), with every run passing and finishing within 3% of the standard held-out KL; trainer wait time falls from 70.3 s per step to 0.1 s at k = 4. FP8 on top adds nothing (1.629× at k = 4, 1.547× at k = 8) because generation is off the critical path — the trainer, not the engine, becomes the bottleneck at ~55 updates/hour.
- **Capacity, not speed, is where FP8 pays.** At 64 sequences per step, Model Optimizer FP8 raises throughput only 1.082× with the EAGLE-3 draft head (0.990× for vLLM's FP8 cache); at 1,536 sequences per step, where the BF16 cache fills and triggers 7,083 preemptions against zero for FP8, the gains are 1.111× and 1.161×. FP8 holds 1,668 KV-cache tokens versus BF16's 864 (1.93×), or 1,808 (2.09×) for Model Optimizer's recipe.
- **Time-to-quality improves 1.62×, at a small accuracy cost.** FP8 with 8-version-stale rollouts reaches the synchronous run's final held-out KL (0.047643) in 3.694 hours versus 5.980 hours, with final accuracy 60.46% versus 61.54%; the second pre-registered target (90% of the standard run's KL improvement) is reached 1.69× sooner with FP8 and k = 4, final KL within 0.8%.
- **RL breaks where OPD holds, by a wide margin.** REINFORCE fails under all three OPD-passing configurations: at noise σ = 0.15 (D = 0.097) its reward reaches zero and held-out accuracy falls 21 points; under NVFP4 with a BF16 first-layer cache, responses hit the length limit and accuracy falls 59 points; under FP8 with first/last layers in BF16, accuracy falls 63 points. GRPO fails under two of three (NVFP4: entropy collapse, −15 points; noise: more length-limit hits) but passes FP8-with-BF16-ends. In units of the standard engine's mismatch, OPD tolerates ~230×, GRPO ~60×, REINFORCE ~12×.
- **Per-token corrections do not help OPD, and the prefix shift explains why.** Under weight noise at D = 0.097, score centering leaves held-out KL 2.3%–3.7% *above* uncentered OPD, and truncated importance sampling (cap 2) raises it 2.9% at step 100 and 7.0% at step 200 while every-layer FP8 still fails. The decomposition shows prefix shift S growing from 0.098 to 0.517 (5.3×) between the passing and failing noise levels, against per-token error T growing from 0.198 to 0.417 (2.1×).
- **Mismatch matters more late in training, not less.** At three noise levels on fixed prefixes, the update error at step 200 is 0.11–0.13 of its size at the untrained student, but the update itself shrinks faster: at the middle level the error-to-update ratio is 0.93 at the untrained student and 1.88 at step 200. Consistently, the 0.39 noise level passes at step 100 (+19.5%) and fails only at step 200 (+23.3%).
- **Offline OPD sits comfortably inside the budget.** Semi-OPD and Lightning OPD reuse rollouts sampled once from the untrained student, which is equivalent to an engine with fixed weights; the authors measure that mismatch at 0.082 nats/token at steps 100 and 200 on 7B standard-run checkpoints — well below the bracket's lower end of 0.184. This retroactively explains why those methods work despite never refreshing rollouts.

## Authors & Credibility

Eight authors across six institutions: Cong Yu and Songlin Jiang (Aalto University), Ziting Wang (Nanyang Technological University), Sitong Zhang (independent), Chengliang Chai (Beijing Institute of Technology), Youhe Jiang and Eiko Yoneki (University of Cambridge Computer Laboratory), and Tuo Shi (Shenzhen University of Advanced Technology). Eiko Yoneki is a Cambridge systems researcher with a long record in runtime systems and data-centric machine learning, which fits the paper's systems-measurement framing; the rest of the group appears to be earlier-career. The work is not from a frontier lab, and there is no evidence of a large-scale production OPD run behind it — the compute footprint is two 4×GH200 nodes.

Credibility signals are unusually strong for an empirical systems paper. The study is pre-registered with a dated, append-only plan per experiment (Appendix B), including the verdict thresholds, the admission line, and the breakage criteria; the paper reports where the pre-registration was *loosened after seeing data* (one correction check relaxed from 1e-4 to 1e-3, the admission window fixed after the first Model Optimizer run, a level at 0.184 added after the earlier runs were read, and a post-hoc 30-step speed check with a changed reporting rule). It also reports a pre-registered speed rule that **failed** (Appendix E: no engine met the 1.15/1.30/1.30 batch-time thresholds; best was 1.25/1.10/1.19 against raised thresholds of 1.43/1.37/1.38). Code is released at github.com/benyucong/train-inference-mismatch-opd. References are current through late 2026 and engage directly with the async-OPD literature (AsyncOPD, Semi-OPD, Lightning OPD, f-OPD) rather than strawmanning it.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty.** The core measurement — per-token KL between rollout engine and trainer — is not new (Zheng et al. 2025 factor an importance weight into mismatch and staleness; the RL mismatch literature uses similar quantities), but applying it as a *common currency* across quantization, staleness and injected noise, and then turning it into an operational budget with an admission test, is a genuine contribution. The first-layer-KV-cache finding is the most novel and most useful result in the paper, and it is the one thing here I have not seen stated elsewhere.

**Rigor — where it is strong.** The pre-registration discipline is real and the paper is candid about its violations. The quality rule was fixed before the runs, τ = 0.20 is justified against a measured ≤1.5% run-to-run spread, the evaluation engine is held fixed at BF16 across all configurations so that a run cannot be scored by the engine that trained it, and the mechanism claims come with closed-form derivations verified against autograd plus a placebo (independent redraw) control in the prefix-shift experiment.

**Rigor — where it is weak, and this is the main problem.** The RL comparison in §5.5, which carries the paper's most quotable claim ("OPD remains stable under rollout configurations that destabilize RL"), is confounded. The authors state plainly that "neither standard RL run improves held-out accuracy measurably by step 100, so we assess failure rather than learning gains" — so the RL baselines never demonstrated successful training even on the standard engine, and "breaking" is being measured on runs that were not learning in the first place. The RL runs also use different prompts from the OPD runs, and the paper itself warns that "each method uses different prompts, so compare it only with its own dashed standard-engine baseline." A reader should treat "OPD tolerates 230× where REINFORCE breaks at 12×" as a statement about *these* RL instantiations, not about RL as a class. The claim that "initial mismatch and early weight changes do not explain the contrast" (RL starts at 0.88–1.02× OPD's mismatch and moves weights only 0.43–0.90× as far) is a partial control, but it does not address the underlying issue that the RL runs were unhealthy.

**Missing ablation.** Table 11 reports time-to-quality for exactly three configurations: synchronous BF16, async k = 8 + FP8, and async k = 4 + FP8. There is **no async-BF16 column**, so the headline "1.62× sooner" cannot be decomposed between asynchrony and FP8. Table 8 makes this consequential: async BF16 at k = 8 already delivers 1.586× the updates per hour and k = 4 delivers 1.624×, i.e. essentially all of the 1.62×. Combined with Appendix E's failed pre-registered rule — FP8 quantization alone did not speed up batch time at 16, 64 or 128 sequences per replica (best 1.25/1.10/1.19 vs thresholds 1.43/1.37/1.38) — the honest reading is that the speedup is asynchrony's, and FP8's contribution is KV-cache capacity (1.93×) plus a modest 1.08–1.16× throughput at memory-bound batch sizes. The abstract's phrasing ("Combining FP8 with asynchronous rollouts reaches the same training quality 1.62× sooner") is technically accurate but invites the wrong attribution.

**The budget bracket is thinner than it looks.** No run was placed inside [0.184, 0.39], so the bracket is bounded by one failing level (two runs at 0.39) and one passing level (two runs at 0.184). The authors admit this. Worse, the budget is not monotone in mismatch at stricter thresholds: Appendix Table 1 shows that at a 6.35% KL line the σ = 0.0665 noise run at D = 0.055 *fails* while D = 0.097 and D = 0.184 *pass* — the paper labels this "not ordered" rather than confronting it. The σ = 0.0665 run is also the worst-behaved passing configuration (+6.35% at step 200), which suggests weight noise has structure (a fixed direction, held constant across syncs and replicas) that a scalar KL does not capture. A single scalar budget is a useful engineering fiction, not a validated safety certificate.

**Statistical power.** One run per configuration; four were trained twice, and repeats differ by 1.0% and 1.5% in held-out KL. Intervals resample problems, not seeds, so the reported 95% intervals are narrower than the true run-to-run uncertainty. The accuracy rule is barely powered: only the NVFP4 every-layer run clears the 5-point bar (−5.21), while the FP8 every-layer runs' −3.41 and −3.63 points have intervals including zero — so the text's "lose 3–5 accuracy points" is descriptive, not significant. The two second runs at 0.097 and 0.39, and most likely the FP8 k = 4 run, used a newer GPU driver without a control, which the authors flag as contaminating that run's speed ratio.

**Data quirk.** The 530-problem held-out set includes 38 problems flagged as overlapping the DAPO-Math-17k training pool. Since held-out KL is the primary metric and the standard run's improvement is only 2× (0.0965 → 0.0481), a 7% contamination could plausibly matter for the marginal configurations near the boundary — though not for the 20%-level effects the budget rests on.

**Overclaiming.** "OPD tolerates substantial mismatch without meaningful loss of training quality" is defensible at τ = 0.20 but the phrase "without meaningful loss" is doing work: the passing band spans −5.0% to +6.4%, and "training quality" here means agreement with the teacher, not task accuracy. Accuracy across all passing configurations moves by less than 1.5 points, and the standard run only reaches 60.93% — so the paper is measuring the fidelity of a distillation process whose downstream benefit is itself small at 200 steps. The 1.5B result is the strongest counterexample to the paper's own framing and is reported honestly, but the abstract's "OPD tolerates substantial mismatch" would read differently if the 1.5B number were in it.

**One unexplained finding.** Adding a positive constant b to every advantage (b = +0.15, +0.5) flips the drift coefficient's sign, moves the student *toward* the noisy engine, and yet ends held-out KL 6.5% and 6.9% *below* the standard run's — better than no noise at all. The authors list this as open. It is either an important regularization effect or an artifact of the fixed noise direction, and the paper does not distinguish.

## Strengths

- **A measurement contribution that changes practice.** The common nats/token scale, the admission check, and the "keep the first layer's KV cache in BF16" recipe are directly usable by anyone running OPD with FP8 rollouts, and the last one is nearly free (one layer out of 28 in BF16 retains 1.93× capacity).
- **Mechanism, not just correlation.** The paper does not stop at "FP8 KV cache is bad"; it localizes the damage to one layer, quantifies the share (93%–99%), attributes 82% of that layer's effect to key rounding via a keys-only emulation, and offers a structural explanation (key-projection bias, no QK normalization, large first-layer keys) that correctly predicts Llama's and Qwen3's insensitivity. That is a falsifiable story that survives out-of-sample checks.
- **Genuine pre-registration, including a reported failure.** Appendix E documents a speed rule that was fixed in advance and did not hold. Most papers bury this; here it usefully bounds the quantization-speed claim.
- **The teacher-anchored stationarity argument.** The closed-form result that OPD's expected update vanishes at p_θ = p_T for *any* engine, contrasted with RL's R·δ term that need not shrink, is a clean theoretical reason for the empirical asymmetry — and it correctly predicts that RL-style per-token corrections will not help.
- **Honest limitations.** The appendix lists the dropped Qwen3-4B pair (it "failed our check that plain OPD trains on it"), the unlocated staleness limit (all stale runs stay below 0.005 nats/token), the absent in-bracket run, the untested admission edge, the emulated NVFP4, and the fact that GRPO/REINFORCE never passed the learning test.
- **Useful negative results.** Score centering and truncated importance sampling — both widely deployed in production OPD pipelines — measurably *hurt* OPD here. That is an uncomfortable, valuable finding.

## Limitations

- **One main pair, one domain, 200 steps.** The budget [0.184, 0.39] is the 7B DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen student's on math (DAPO-Math-17k → AIME24/MATH-500). The teacher is an RL fine-tune of the student, i.e. a close pair; a farther-apart pair may have a much smaller budget. The 1.5B budget is never located, and the Llama-3.2-3B run can only detect held-out-KL rises of 14%–20% or more. Qwen3-4B was dropped because plain OPD did not train on it, so the "two model families" claim rests on DeepSeek and Llama only.
- **No run inside the budget bracket.** The transition is bracketed by tested configurations at 0.184 and 0.39, and at stricter quality lines the ordering breaks entirely (0.055 fails where 0.097 passes). The budget is a screening heuristic, not a validated threshold.
- **Verdicts are step-dependent.** The 0.39 noise level passes at step 100 and fails at step 200; the paper's own reading is that mismatch impact grows late in training. A 200-step run cannot say what happens at 2,000 steps, which is the regime where production OPD actually operates.
- **One run per configuration, problem-resampled intervals.** Repeats differ by 1.0%–1.5%, and the reported intervals understate seed variance. Several secondary claims (the −3.4/−3.6-point FP8 accuracy losses) are not significant.
- **The RL comparison is not a fair test of RL.** The standard-engine GRPO and REINFORCE runs never improved held-out accuracy by step 100, use different prompts from OPD, and are assessed only for failure. The "OPD holds where RL breaks" headline should be read as a comparison against these specific, non-learning RL instantiations.
- **No async-BF16 arm in the time-to-quality table**, so the 1.62× headline cannot be attributed between asynchrony and quantization — and Table 8 plus the failed Appendix E rule suggest asynchrony accounts for nearly all of it.
- **NVFP4 is emulated**, giving quality-only results with no memory or speed number; the practical case for 4-bit rollouts is therefore untested.
- **Hardware and speed scope.** GH200 only, 200 steps, and each FP8 speed gain comes from one short run; the 1,536-sequence memory benchmark credits a 5.7% gain against a 5% bar, and one run likely used a different driver.
- **The mechanism section's fixed-prefix analysis may overstate shrinkage.** The authors note that on fixed prefixes the KL to the teacher falls 28-fold while held-out KL falls only 2-fold, so the gradient-probe measurements are not a faithful model of what happens during training.

## Relevance

- **RL Post-Training Dynamics & Failures**：这篇直接研究 on-policy distillation 在量化 rollout、权重陈旧（staleness）与权重噪声下的稳定性边界，给出了可复现的失配度量、经验预算区间和崩溃判据，并把 OPD 与 GRPO/REINFORCE 的耐受差异追溯到"教师锚定的更新在 p_θ = p_T 处为零"这一结构性原因——正是"on-policy distillation / 异步 RL 的 staleness 病理与漂移"这一兴趣点的核心。
- **Reliability under Extreme Performance Optimization**：把 FP8/NVFP4 量化、KV cache 低精度与异步带来的数值偏差当作可靠性问题来量化，定位到"第一层 KV cache 贡献 93%–99% 失配"这一具体精度敏感点，并给出"只保留首层 BF16"的最小代价修复方案，属于"量化误差 + 低精度数值稳定性"的典型样本，而不是纯 speed/memory 收益报告。

## Questions for Further Exploration

1. **Does the budget shrink as the teacher–student gap widens?** The 7B teacher is an RL fine-tune of the student, so the pair is close by construction. Would a genuinely stronger teacher (e.g. a 32B teacher into a 7B student) reduce the tolerance by an order of magnitude, and does the first-layer-KV-cache rule still hold?
2. **What happens past 200 steps?** The paper's own data shows the 0.39 level passing at step 100 and failing at 200, and the error-to-update ratio rising from 0.93 to 1.88. Does a configuration admitted at step 200 drift out of budget at step 1,000, and does the admission check need a step-dependent line rather than a constant L?
3. **Why does a positive advantage offset improve held-out KL below the standard run?** b = +0.15 and +0.5 move the student toward the noisy engine yet end 6.5% and 6.9% better than the noise-free standard run. Is this a regularization effect of the fixed noise direction, or does the offset act as an implicit entropy/KL term? A control with random noise directions would separate the two.
4. **Is "first layer" the right abstraction, or is it "the layer with the largest key norm"?** The explanation invokes key-projection bias and missing QK normalization. A model with QK normalization but an artificially amplified first-layer key scale would discriminate between "position in the network" and "key magnitude" as the causal factor.
5. **Can the prefix shift be corrected cheaply?** The paper estimates whole-response reweighting at ~10²⁷ responses per effective sample at the smallest noise level, which rules out naive importance sampling — but a partial correction (e.g. resampling only the first divergence point, or rejecting responses whose prefix KL exceeds a threshold) might capture most of S at a workable acceptance rate.
6. **Do the RL conclusions survive a healthy RL baseline?** The GRPO and REINFORCE runs here never learned. Running the same mismatch sweep with a recipe that demonstrably improves held-out accuracy — and with matched prompts — would test whether "OPD tolerates 230× where RL breaks at 12×" is a property of the objectives or of these particular runs.
7. **Does the 1.5B anomaly generalize?** The 1.5B student suffers far more than the 7B at *lower* measured mismatch. Is that a capacity effect (smaller models have flatter teacher distributions and thus more brittle KL), a tokenizer/architecture effect, or an artifact of the Model Optimizer recipe's calibration on that model?
