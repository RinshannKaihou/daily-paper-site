Why this matters Relevance

Large RL jobs burn NPU time on padding and queue jitter; a pre-run scheduler can recover some of it without touching the training loop.

Compute infrastructure for large-scale ML. The scheduling layer, not the model, is the lever.

!Critical edge Caveat

The 10.9% that belongs to the scheduler is a single unreplicated pair of 200-iteration runs — about 1.6× the authors' own 6.8% run-to-run CV.

01

The mechanism: solve it offline, then only queue and reload

ASP treats rollout scheduling as two problems — what goes into a minibatch, and when that minibatch is dispatched — instead of one bin-packing problem.

01

Predict frozen

A fine-tuned Qwen2.5-1.5B, trained on 4,000 prompt–length pairs, ranks prompts by expected output length. Kendall's τ = 0.64 on Qwen3-30B-A3B.

02

Partition O(κn) DP

Sort by predicted length, then cut the list into minibatches of 32–34 that maximize predicted token utilization (b=32, κ=2).

03

Reorder simulated annealing

Permute minibatches into groups of 6 with near-equal predicted load. 0.31 s for 10⁷ minibatches on one CPU core.

04

Integrate online

Trainer (TU), generation actors (GUs), and a CPU-side dispatcher (CU) share one bounded Training Queue; actors finish in-flight work before reloading weights. Measured d_off ≈ 3.3 trainer steps, η ≈ 0.912.

Three bubbles

Why a minibatch can be fast while the pipeline stays slow.

Padding

Short sequences idle while the longest one in the batch decodes.

Skewness

Heavy-tailed lengths shrink the effective active batch near the end of decoding.

Sorting new here

Sorted dispatch makes arrivals decay over time: the queue first fills, then drains.

OFFLINE — predict · partition · reorder
ONLINE — bounded queue + deferred reload only

03

Trade-offs: a good idea, an honest but thin empirical chain

The reframing is stronger than the machinery; the engineering is stronger than the wall-clock evidence.

Strengths

What holds up

  • Sorting bubbles is a useful reframing: minibatch composition and dispatch order are separate problems.
  • Zero online overhead — 0.31 s of annealing for 10⁷ minibatches; nothing is solved during rollout.
  • The C0–C4 ladder holds the architecture fixed, separating scheduler gain from disaggregation gain.
  • Negative results are reported: the τ=0.25 crossover, the DP's ~0.002 gain, C3 beating C4 at τ=0.
  • Lag is measured, not asserted: d_off ≈ 3.3 steps, η ≈ 0.912, with a full timing breakdown.

Read this for the mechanism and the ablation design.

Limitations

What doesn't (yet)

  • Single-seed wall-clock: 10.9% and 23.4% come from one pair of runs; the authors' own CV is 6.8%.
  • Ascend-only: 73.9 s reload and 84.6 s conversion are NPU-stack artifacts; GPU gains are unknown.
  • No StreamRL / AsyncFlow / AReaL / Laminar run; the only disaggregated baseline is verl, which has no generation-side composition.
  • Ablation uses simulated training times, and the real queue stays ≤1.25 batches — the bubble may not bite at this operating point.
  • Convergence is 30 GRPO iterations, one seed, one maze task, no matched-compute on-policy control.
  • Offline prompt set, fixed predictor, and scaling stops at 128 NPUs where training becomes the bottleneck.

Read the 59.9% as disaggregation + scheduling vs. disaggregation alone — not as a SOTA win.

04 · Open questions

What would change the verdict

Four experiments the paper leaves open — each one is cheaper than the headline run it would clarify.

Q1

How much of the 10.9% survives three seeds?

Q2

Does balanced ordering still pay when the Training Queue is not near-empty?

Q3

What does the DP buy in the p95/p99 iteration tail?

Q4

What happens on GPUs where weight reload is cheap?
