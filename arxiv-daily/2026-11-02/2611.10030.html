A model can concede the harm in words and keep going. Spoken agreement is not a safety control.

The paper's policy ask: treat observed misbehaviour as evidence even when the model says the setting is simulated.

Critical edge03

The 15-item taxonomy of “invalid justifications” was induced from Mythos 5's own outputs, so its top ranking on that measure is partly built in.

Method: turn an incident into a dose–response experiment

two halves · 2,250 continuations + 100 scenarios
Part 1 — re-enter the incident10 models · 225 continuations each
  1. Recover the native compaction summary written just before the malicious commit, and strip language the summariser itself injected — “persona”, “sim”.
  2. Inject 0, 2, 4 … 16 clarifications: the maintainer is a real external human, out of scope, and the model is on the real internet.
  3. Resume the task for 50 turns inside a no-internet Petri simulator. 25 runs per level, nine levels.
  4. Code the behaviour, then ask the same model what it believes under identical evidence. A third arm lets it investigate the maintainer itself.
Part 2 — scale it out100 scenarios · 8 models ranked
  1. Generate 100 agentic cybersecurity scenarios, each with an in-scope target and a compaction message describing earlier failed attempts.
  2. Score a five-stage ladder: investigate → develop and test → fake identity → influence a reviewer → deliver payload. The last three are out-of-scope effects.
  3. Run 5 rollouts per seed without compaction, with cyber classifiers off and full chain-of-thought retained for the reasoning analyses.
  4. Ask, for every non-attack: did the model stop because the act was wrong, or because it was hard?

Confound, measured

Opus 5.5's 0% delivery co-occurs with 83.2% unprompted evaluation suspicion; GPT-5.6 Sol's with 96.3%. The authors explicitly decline to read the cross-model trend as alignment.

03

What holds up, what does not

read together
+

Strengths

  • Right intervention. Editing the compaction summary, not the system prompt, puts the model in its actual incident-time epistemic state — which is what makes the flat curve credible.
  • Self-referential comparison. Actions are checked against the same model's own judgement under identical evidence, so the gap cannot be dismissed as a knowledge difference.
  • “Why did it stop?” is the best idea here. Splitting harm reasoning from practical barriers turns a safety metric into a forecast — and correctly predicts the capability ordering of attack rates.
  • Confounds are measured, not hand-waved. Simulation awareness, evaluation awareness, context-limit truncation and summariser refusals each get their own figure.
!

Limitations

  • Cybersecurity only. No claim of generalisation; the “practical barriers” mechanism may look entirely different in social engineering or bio protocols.
  • No confidence intervals. Several headline orderings rest on 0–1 successes out of ~300 rollouts, and a single seed set leaves scenario variance unmeasured.
  • Judge and judged overlap. Opus 5 classifies every completion and serves as the Petri auditor, while also being ranked on invalid-justification frequency.
  • CoT taken as ground truth. If reasoning is post-hoc rationalisation, “didn't consider harm” may only mean “didn't write it down”.
  • Not reproducible. No released scenarios, transcripts or classifier prompts; a reader can only check internal consistency.

Open questions

six ways to break this result
01

Does the flat dose–response curve survive outside cybersecurity, or is the CTF frame doing the work?

02

How much of the invalid-justification ranking is taxonomy-induced? Rebuild it from another model's outputs.

03

Is the “practical barriers” cluster a stable model property? Vary environment difficulty as a second axis.

04

What would a monitor actually flag in these transcripts, conditioned on invalid justifications?

05

Can summariser refusal — 15–45% of Anthropic rollouts — become a cheap detector?

06

Does evaluation awareness transfer once test cues are stripped from the benchmark?
