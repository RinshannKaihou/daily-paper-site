Key metric

97.61%→0

Identical-input replays returning different FP32 outputs: atomic Split-K=8 versus the fixed-order kernel, across 109,760 comparisons.

Why this matters

Last-bit kernel noise silently flips greedy-decoding answers. A cheap fixed-order reduction buys reproducible LoRA serving without surrendering Split-K's 19% speedup.

Critical edge

No code or kernel is released, and the deterministic baseline the paper claims to beat never appears in any table.

Critical assessment · Limitations

Causal trace

One coordinate, one wrong answer

  3. 03 32 elements

    BF16 entries of the value projection change in the KV cache.

  4. 04 "There" → "Each"

    Token 36 diverges after 35 identical tokens.

  5. 05 7 → 8

    Correct answer becomes incorrect; Split-K=1 repeats stay identical.

Methodology

Four moves

  1. 01
    Isolate.

    Capture 27,440 shrink ops (X·Aᵀ, rank 16 or 32 against H=3,072) and replay each five times under three kernels.

  2. 02
    Diagnose.

    Each tile's reduction is split eight ways, and the partials merge through FP32 atomicAdd; order follows GPU scheduling, and float addition is not associative.

  3. 03
    Fix.

    Partials land in a scratch buffer. A per-tile integer counter elects the last-finishing CTA, which sums partitions 0..7 in fixed order, scales, and writes. No second kernel launch.

  4. 04
    Measure.

    Three architectures × two ranks on GSM8K and MBPP, plus Civil Comments and 12 timing configs on one RTX PRO 6000.

Integer atomics coordinate the order. They never touch the sum.

Strengths

  • Clean isolation. Identical captured operands replayed across three kernels, with the 1.08e-8 mean element difference quantified.
  • Complete causal chain, backed by a cache-substitution control and an honest 2/10 injection success rate.
  • Transferable kernel trick. Eight-way parallelism, no extra launch, 1.69% mean overhead.
  • Reasonable breadth. Dense, local/global attention and Mamba-2 hybrid models; H100 and B200 spot checks.
  • Negative results reported. No accuracy gain, and 39.06% cross-batch drift survives.

Limitations

  • No batch invariance. 39.06% of cross-batch comparisons still differ; bucketed batching is suggested, not tested.
  • No artifact, no baseline. No code link, and the claimed deterministic competitor is absent from every table.
  • Thin statistics. Five timing trials, no intervals, so the 1.69% overhead may sit inside noise.
  • Narrow envelope. Models ≤4B, ranks 16/32, TP=1, one request at a time, no continuous batching or multi-LoRA.
  • Small slices. 50 prompts per dataset; 18–21 flips per config cannot support cross-model claims.

Questions for further exploration

  1. Does the fixed-order sum agree bit-exactly with Split-K=1, and which sits closer to an FP64 reference?
  2. Would Kahan/Neumaier summation or FP64 accumulation beat fixed ordering on both cost and accuracy?
  3. How does the kernel behave under continuous batching with ragged M and per-request adapter switches?
  4. At rank 64/128 or prefill-dominated M, does Split-K's advantage vanish or invert?
