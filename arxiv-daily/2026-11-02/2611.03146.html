pass@k is just @1 with a prompt-adaptive KL coefficient.

One calibration bank of 30 reference completions turns DPO, A⋆PO or GRPO into a coverage-aware @k optimizer — no new estimator, no k-completion groups.

−75% at k=4 · 128k vs 512k

Why this matters

RLVR collapses into entropy collapse and loses coverage on hard problems. This makes the coverage/pass@1 trade-off a calibrated knob rather than a new estimator.

Critical edge

The headline sharpening claims are figure-only, on a 30-problem AIME set with no error bars — while the tabulated wins come from the in-distribution MATH split the method is bound to flatter.

Critical assessment · rigor

01

Methodology

theory → wrapper → three instantiations
  1. Start from the @k objective. Jk(π) = Ex[Vk(π;x) − β·KL(π‖πref)], where Vk is pass@k for binary rewards and max@k for continuous ones. Both collapse to @1 at k=1.
  2. Apply a variational Gibbs lemma. Any optimum has the form π* ∝ πref·exp(Aπ*/β); for pass@k the score is A = k(1−p)k−1·R, so the global β is replaced by a prompt-dependent βeff(x).
  3. Read off the equivalence. The pass@k optimum is exactly the @1 optimum under βeff(x) — or, identically, under a reward scaled by k(1−p*)k−1. That is the optimizer-independent explanation of why @k resists sharpening.
  4. Wrap, don't retrain. KAPPO samples M=30 reference completions per prompt once, estimates p̂ref, solves the scalar fixed point per (k, β), and reuses that bank across seeds, learning rates and step counts. Training code is untouched.
  5. Instantiate three ways. @k-DPO swaps β inside the sigmoid (regularization side); @k-A⋆PO substitutes the adaptive reward into a squared-regression loss; @k-GRPO transforms rewards before group normalization, with no KL term at all.
βeff(x) = β k(1 − p*(x))k−1

p* is pinned by a scalar fixed point: k(1−p*)k−1 = β·log[p*(1−p_ref) / p_ref(1−p*)]. Easy prompts get large βeff (strong regularization); hard prompts get weak regularization and large reward multipliers.

03

Strengths & limitations

read side by side

Strengths · what holds up

  • The equivalence is actionable. No new policy-gradient estimator, no k-completion groups — a lookup table computed once, then demonstrated on DPO, A⋆PO and GRPO rather than merely asserted.
  • The theory is tight. Unique root for the fixed point via strict monotonicity of Φq, boundary cases handled, and the continuous-reward section explicitly refuses the regularization-side shortcut instead of papering over it.
  • Appendix K grades its own homework. DKW uniform consistency, O(M−1/2) noise, and a candid +18.27% upper-tail bias — enough for a practitioner to size the bank.
  • Contamination-aware evaluation. AIME 2026 postdates both checkpoints, LiveCodeBench v6 problems are dated against the model release, and OLMo is picked for documented decontamination.

Limitations · what to discount

  • No statistics behind the main claim. AIME 2026 has 30 problems — 3.33 pts each — and the sharpening curves are figure-only, single-seed, without confidence intervals.
  • k saturates at 2 with no analysis. k=2 and k=4 are within 0.9 pts on both models, which undercuts "k directly controls sharpening."
  • The DPO instantiation deviates from its own theory. βeff is capped at 2, and the cap binds on the easy prompts where the mechanism is supposed to act. No ablation.
  • No cheap control baseline. No entropy bonus, temperature sweep or KL sweep — so the value of the machinery over a one-line regularizer is unmeasured. @k-GRPO also runs without KL, outside the theory.

Open questions three that would settle the paper

  1. Why does k saturate between 2 and 4 — compressed βeff dynamic range, a flattening fixed point, or upper-tail win-rate noise swamping the extra pressure?
  2. What happens to @k-DPO with the βeff cap removed or raised (2 / 5 / 20 / ∞)? The cap binds exactly where the theory claims the effect lives.
  3. Does a compute-matched PKPO — same total rollouts, more prompt batches at n=k — close the −2.61 pt pass@1 gap on Qwen3-4B?
