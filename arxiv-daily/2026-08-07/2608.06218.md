> **一句话总结：** 本文证明 Muon 优化器在 Stiefel 流形（正交约束矩阵）上的线性最小化子问题存在精确闭式解（即 skew(MX^T) 的矩阵符号函数），据此提出 Skewon 算法，在保持与 Muon 相同 O(np²) 渐近复杂度的同时避免了迭代逼近，并给出非凸光滑目标下 O(1/√T) 的一阶收敛速率。

> **TL;DR:** The authors resolve the open problem of adapting the Muon optimizer's spectral-norm linear minimization oracle (LMO) to the Stiefel manifold St(n,p) by showing it admits an exact closed-form solution `Y_opt = -msign(skew(MX^T))`, equivalent to (not a relaxation of) the original tangent-space problem. They turn this into the **Skewon** algorithm, which matches Muon's O(np²) per-step cost when p ≪ n (vs. iterative methods like Bernstein 2025, Cesista 2025, Su 2025 that need 5+ inner steps), and on the synthetic SMP benchmark with n=64 it drives the Frobenius-norm relative error to ≈10⁻¹³ (at the 10⁻¹¹ CVXPY/SCS reference floor), orders of magnitude below the ≈10⁻⁴–10⁻⁷ errors of the iterative baselines, while establishing a first-order convergence guarantee `min‖gradf‖_F ≤ √(8pΔL/T)`.

## 核心贡献

- **闭式解的推导**：将 Stiefel Muon 问题（SMP）`min⟨M,B⟩ s.t. ‖B‖₂≤1, B∈T_X St(n,p)` 等价地转化为反对称矩阵上的 **Skewon 问题** `min⟨skew(MX^T), Y⟩ s.t. ‖Y‖₂≤1`，并给出其解析解 `Y_opt = -msign(N)`（N=skew(MX^T) 非奇异时），其中 msign 为矩阵符号函数。
- **等价性定理（Theorem 2）**：证明 Skewon 问题不是对 SMP 的松弛，而是 *等价* 改写——两者的最优值完全一致，且任一 SMP 最优解 B* 都可由某个反对称最优解 Y* 通过 Y*X = B* 还原。关键工具是对 A_n（so(n) 李代数）的直和分解 A_n = L_X ⊕ K_X（Theorem 1），其中 K_X 是 Stiefel 流形的稳定子李代数。
- **Skewon 算法**：给出两种实现——p~n 时（Algorithm 1，O(n³)）与 p≪n 时（Algorithm 2，利用 `[-X M]` 的 BCGS2/QR 分解把内层降到 2p×2p，仅 O(np²)），与 Muon 同阶渐近复杂度。
- **收敛性保证（Theorem 3）**：在 Riemannian 优化标准的光滑性假设下，对非凸光滑目标给出一阶收敛速率 `min_{t<T} ‖gradf(X_t)‖_F ≤ √(8pΔL/T)`，步长 η = √(2Δ/(pLT))。

## Key Contributions

- **Exact closed form for the Stiefel Muon LMO**: Reframes the tangent-space spectral LMO (2) as the *Skewon problem* over skew-symmetric matrices and shows its optimum is `Y_opt = -msign(skew(MX^T))` (Proposition 1), obtained from a single SVD/matrix-sign evaluation rather than an inner loop.
- **Equivalence theorem (Theorem 2)**: Establishes via the direct-sum splitting of so(n) (Theorem 1, A_n = L_X ⊕ K_X) that Skewon is *not a relaxation* of the SMP — the two share identical optima and any SMP optimum B* lifts to an antisymmetric optimum Y* with Y*X = B*. This closes the open problem posed by Bernstein (2025) on solving the Stiefel spectral LMO.
- **Skewon algorithm with Muon-matching cost**: Algorithm 1 handles p~n in O(n³); Algorithm 2 handles the deep-learning-relevant regime p≪n by QR-decomposing `[-X M]` and reducing the inner solve to a 2p×2p matrix `½RJR^T`, achieving O(np²) per iteration — the same asymptotic cost as vanilla Muon, with no inner iterations.
- **First-order convergence guarantee**: Under the standard Riemannian smoothness assumption (Assumption 1), proves `min_{t<T} ‖gradf(X_t)‖_F ≤ √(8pΔL/T)` with η = √(2Δ/(pLT)) for both algorithms when the polar factor is computed via thin SVD.

## Methodology

The paper proceeds in three logical stages.

**(1) Reformulation as a skew-symmetric problem.** The key observation is that for any Y ∈ A_n, the product YX lies in the tangent space T_X St(n,p) (since X^T(YX) + (YX)^T X = Y + (-Y) = 0). Imposing ‖Y‖₂ ≤ 1 then automatically enforces ‖YX‖₂ ≤ ‖X‖₂‖Y‖₂ ≤ 1. This motivates the Skewon problem (6): minimize ⟨skew(MX^T), Y⟩ subject to ‖Y‖₂ ≤ 1. Proposition 1 shows its solution is the negative matrix-sign of N = skew(MX^T), proven via the substitution Ŷ = -U^T Y V and the fact that a contraction with a unit diagonal entry must have all other entries in that row/column equal to zero.

**(2) Equivalence to the SMP (Section 3).** The non-trivial step is showing Skewon is equivalent to — not looser than — the original tangent-space LMO. Theorem 1 decomposes so(n) = L_X ⊕ K_X, where L_X ≅ T_X St(n,p) corresponds to "active rotations tilting the frame X" and K_X ≅ so(n-p) is the stabilizer algebra that leaves X invariant (so K_X elements satisfy Y_ker X = 0). Theorem 2 then constructs, for any SMP optimum B*, a skew-symmetric lift Y* = B*X^T - XB*^T - X(X^T B*)X^T + (I-XX^T)C(I-XX^T) and uses the Davis–Kahan–Weinberger dilation (Proposition 3) to choose C such that ‖Y*‖₂ = ‖B*‖₂ = 1, proving both feasibility and value-matching.

**(3) Algorithm and theory.** Algorithm 1 evaluates the closed form directly; Algorithm 2 exploits the structure of `[-X M]` (whose first block -X already has orthonormal columns) via block classical Gram–Schmidt with reorthogonalization (BCGS2), keeping the inner solve on a 2p×2p matrix. The convergence proof (Theorem 3) telescopes the descent inequality from Assumption 1, using that `⟨∇f, B_t⟩ = -‖skew(∇f X^T)‖_*` and bounding the Riemannian gradient norm by twice the nuclear norm via the identity `2(I - XX^T) skew(∇f X^T) X = gradf(X)`.

## Results & Highlights

- **Convergence rate (Theorem 3, analytic)**: With step size η = √(2Δ/(pLT)) and Δ ≥ f(X₀) - inf f, both Algorithm 1 and Algorithm 2 satisfy `min_{t∈{0,…,T-1}} ‖gradf(X_t)‖_F ≤ √(8pΔL/T)` — an O(1/√T) rate matching standard non-convex Riemannian steepest-descent bounds, with an explicit √p dependence on the column dimension.
- **Per-step complexity**: Skewon attains O(np²) flops per iteration when p ≪ n (Algorithm 2 with BCGS2), and O(n³) when p ~ n (Algorithm 1) — the *same asymptotic cost as Muon itself*, unlike prior Stiefel-Muon methods (Bernstein 2025, Cesista 2025, Su 2025) which add an inner iterative loop (5 steps used in the experiments).
- **Accuracy on the synthetic SMP benchmark** (n = 64, p varied 1–30, 50 runs, reference via CVXPY/SCS at 10⁻¹¹ tolerance): Skewon's Frobenius-norm relative error `‖B_CVXPY - B_alg‖_F / ‖B_CVXPY‖_F` sits at ≈10⁻¹³, essentially at the reference-solver floor, whereas the iterative baselines (Bernstein, both Cesista variants, Su SVD) plateau at ≈10⁻⁴ to 10⁻⁷ — a gap of 6–9 orders of magnitude.
- **Accuracy–latency frontier (Figure 3, n=64, p=32)**: Skewon reaches ~10⁻¹³ relative error at sub-millisecond per-step cost; the same frontier plot shows the iterative baselines cannot reach below ≈10⁻⁷ regardless of how many inner steps are allotted within the tested time budget.
- **Rank-deficient robustness (Proposition 4, Appendix A.3)**: When N = skew(MX^T) is rank-deficient of rank 2k, the skew-symmetrized polar factor S = (UV^T - VU^T)/2 has exactly 2k singular values equal to 1 and the remaining n-2k values in [0,1], so the SVD-based variant produces a valid contraction without spurious directions.

## Critical Assessment

- **Novelty**: Genuine. The closed-form `Y_opt = -msign(skew(MX^T))` and the equivalence theorem are the central insight and, as far as the related-work survey shows, were genuinely unknown — Bernstein (2025) explicitly framed solving the Stiefel spectral LMO as an open problem and resorted to Lagrangian-duality iterations. The so(n) = L_X ⊕ K_X splitting that makes the equivalence proof work is a clean geometric observation (Remark 2 frames K_X as the stabilizer Lie algebra).
- **Rigor**: High for the theory. One caveat: the convergence guarantee (Theorem 3) requires the polar factor to be computed *exactly* via thin SVD, whereas the practical Algorithm 1 uses a Newton–Schulz iteration (Line 4). The paper acknowledges this gap but does *not* prove convergence for the Newton–Schulz variant actually recommended for GPU friendliness — a missing analysis that matters for practitioners.
- **Reproducibility**: Mixed. The algorithm pseudocode (Algorithms 1–2) is precise and implementable from the paper. However, the experimental section reports no code release, no random-seed protocol beyond "50 runs, median reported", and — more importantly — *no end-to-end deep-learning training results*. All experiments are synthetic SMP solves (random Gaussian X and M), which validates the closed form but says nothing about whether Skewon actually improves downstream training of orthogonality-constrained networks.
- **Significance for ML**: The motivation is training orthogonality-constrained / low-rank-adapter networks, yet the paper stops short of any neural-network benchmark. A reader cannot tell from this paper alone whether the 10⁻¹³ LMO accuracy translates into measurable training-loss or generalization gains over the cheaper iterative baselines (whose 10⁻⁴–10⁻⁷ error may be entirely sufficient in practice).
- **Red flags / overclaiming**: None egregious — claims are scoped to "we give the exact closed form and an efficient implementation" and the convergence rate is standard. The weakest link is the implicit suggestion that exactness is worth its cost; the experiments only show that Skewon is *more accurate*, not that the extra accuracy matters for any real objective.
- **Missing ablations**: No comparison of Newton–Schulz vs. Polar Express vs. CANS as the inner sign solver inside Skewon; no sweep over how Skewon's accuracy degrades when the Newton–Schulz iteration count is reduced (the paper studies this only for the *competing* methods, Figure 3); no test on the p ~ n regime where Algorithm 1's O(n³) cost becomes the bottleneck.

## Strengths

- Closes a cleanly stated open problem with a short, self-contained proof; the equivalence theorem is the kind of result that, once seen, is obviously the "right" formulation.
- Algorithm 2's reduction to a 2p×2p inner problem via BCGS2 is a genuinely useful implementation trick that keeps per-step cost at Muon's O(np²).
- Honest about scope: the convergence theorem explicitly requires exact SVD-based polar factors and flags that Newton–Schulz is the practical but unproven alternative (and cites Gonon et al. 2026 on why exactness can even *hurt* in some Muon settings).
- Theorem 1's direct-sum decomposition (so(n) = L_X ⊕ K_X) is independently useful geometric machinery for anyone working on Stiefel-valued dynamics.

## Limitations

- **No downstream ML evaluation.** All experiments are isolated SMP solves on random data; there is no evidence that Skewon improves training of real orthogonality-constrained networks, LoRA adapters, or attention heads.
- **Theory–practice gap.** The convergence guarantee covers the SVD-based variant; the Newton–Schulz variant recommended for GPUs is analyzed only informally, and prior work (Gonon et al. 2026, cited as [19]) suggests exactness can degrade Muon-style performance.
- **Single CPU, small scale.** Experiments run on an Intel i5-10400F CPU with n up to 125 and p up to 30 — far below the dimensions of modern hidden-layer weight matrices (n in the thousands). GPU timing and memory are not reported.
- **Limited baselines on the Pareto frontier.** Figure 3 varies inner iterations for competitors but not for Skewon itself, so the exactness-vs-cost tradeoff is characterized asymmetrically.
- **No stochastic/mini-batch analysis.** The convergence result uses the full Euclidean gradient ∇f(X_t); the mini-batch (stochastic) regime relevant to SGD-style training is not covered.

## Questions for Further Exploration

- Does Skewon's 10⁻¹³ LMO accuracy yield measurable training-loss / generalization gains over the iterative baselines (whose 10⁻⁴–10⁻⁷ error may be "good enough") on real orthogonality-constrained networks or LoRA fine-tuning?
- Can the convergence guarantee be extended to the Newton–Schulz (or Polar Express / CANS) inner solver, with explicit dependence on the inner-iteration count and the singular-value gap of N?
- What is the GPU throughput of Algorithm 2 at production scale (n = 1024–8192, p = 64–512), and does the BCGS2 reduction actually beat a fused Newton–Schulz on tensor cores?
- Can the so(n) = L_X ⊕ K_X splitting be exploited to derive closed forms for related spectral LMOs — e.g., on the Grassmann manifold, or for the tensor-spectral Muon generalization (Tensorion, 2606.25975)?
- How does Skewon interact with stochastic mini-batch gradients and with adaptive step-size schedules (the current theory assumes constant η)?
- The proof relies on N being nonsingular for the clean `-msign(N)` form; in practice, how often is N rank-deficient during training, and does Proposition 4's SVD-thresholding variant add measurable cost?
