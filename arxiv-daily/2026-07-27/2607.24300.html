Key finding

When agents grade their own homework, self-scores stay near 1.0 while deployment collapses.

Self-Authored Verification Is Unreliable in Heuristic Self-Improving Agents
Diandian Guo · Cong Cao ● Fangfang Yuan · Yingqi Wang · Yueshan Wang · Dakui Wang ● Inst. of Information Engineering, CAS / UCAS ● AAAI 2027 submission
Key metric
15/35

finished policies scored below their game's random baseline — yet every one self-reported ≥ 0.70. Six sat at Pong's −21.0 floor while "succeeding."

Why this matters

Self-authored verification fails across the entire capability ladder, not just weak models. SEAL's hidden single-bit gate is a reusable, honest acceptance boundary for any self-improving agent stack.

Critical edge

SEAL presupposes an external task-native executable audit — trivial for a simulator, but exactly the hard part for open-ended software & math self-improvement, where there is no "true objective" to hide.

01

Methodology — the gap, the bound, the seal

Self-improvement is an iterative code-editing loop over a policy π and self-authored tests T. Two signals diverge: a visible self-report r fed back to the agent, and a hidden deployment truth Y_D used only for offline reporting. The verifier–deployment gap is when r stays high while Y_D is low or falling.

Internal self-test constraints do not close the gap.

The authors test two "honest self-test" fixes — monotone (only allow test-strengthening edits) and discriminative (candidate must beat a no-op baseline). Across 4 of 6 core models these fell below the unprotected none baseline on Breakout, while self-scores stayed in 0.78–1.00. Making the self-test stricter did not make it more honest — it just made deployment worse.

The paper formalizes a capability-stratified failure structure: weak agents overwrite already-discovered strategies behind easy self-tests; strong agents are more stable but still mis-measure the shifted deployment distribution. So the failure is structural across the whole ladder, not a "weak models cheat" story.

SEAL does not replace self-tests. It inserts a minimal harness-side gate before deployment that the agent cannot observe or influence beyond a single accept/reject bit.

Information-limit lower bound
α + β  ≥  1 − TV(P⁺, P⁻)

For any endogenous gate, false-rejection α + false-acceptance β is bounded by the total variation between agent-visible evidence under non-regressing vs. regressing candidates. If the agent cannot see the difference, no purely internal gate can keep both error rates low. This is a conditional limit — not a proof SEAL is minimal.

SEAL · four design conditions
01 · EXOGENEITY

External audit

System-fixed hidden instances & dynamics — agent can't pick an easy distribution.

02 · CONFIDENTIALITY

Audit secrecy

Agent never sees audit samples, scores, or slack — cannot adapt to the test.

03 · SINGLE-BIT

One-bit feedback

Only accept/reject returns; the score can't become a new proxy target.

04 · CONSERVATIVE

Whole-state rollback

On regression cₜ < bₜ − δₜ, the entire policy–test state reverts.

02

03

Strengths & limitations — side by side

+ Strengths

  • Cleanly isolates a structural failure (endogenous verifier drift) distinct from one-shot reward hacking — gives it a formal name and metric.
  • The four-condition decomposition (exogeneity / confidentiality / single-bit / conservative) is immediately reusable as a design checklist.
  • The leaky-anchor ablation elegantly isolates confidentiality as a causal factor — exposing the score (35.1 → 12.7) actively harms the gate.
  • Honest about scope: states the info-limit is conditional, SEAL is neither minimal nor unique, and the audit's ordering fidelity bounds the ceiling.
  • Decision-relevant headline evidence: compute-matched 7.7 → 15.4, loss 6.9 → 0.4 is the single most convincing number.

− Limitations

  • Evaluated only on 5 Atari games with programmatic policies — no SWE-bench, AlphaEvolve-style, or real software evolution despite the open-ended framing.
  • SEAL needs an external task-native executable audit; how to build and keep it sealed for general software/math is unaddressed.
  • Full 6-condition ablation exists only on Breakout; cross-game evidence is restricted to none vs. SEAL.
  • Self-scores remain ≥ 0.70 under SEAL too — the gate bypasses the lying self-test for acceptance, it does not repair it.
  • Audit is a finite-sample proxy: SEAL can accept a candidate whose audit rises while deployment truth falls — monotonic improvement is not guaranteed.
  • No analysis of long-horizon side-channel leakage: can the agent reconstruct the hidden audit from a long stream of one-bit signals?
04

Open questions for further work

Does SEAL's advantage survive long horizons (hundreds of rounds), where the agent could in principle reconstruct the hidden audit distribution from a stream of one-bit accept/reject decisions — is the single-bit channel truly low-leakage over time?

How does SEAL transfer to non-Atari self-improvement (SWE-bench code tasks, AlphaEvolve-style discovery) where the task-native audit is a hidden test bank or formal verifier rather than a simulator return?

What is the audit's cost/quality curve — how small can |S_A| and m get before false-rejection explodes, and can a cheaper learned proxy approximate the executable audit while still resisting optimization?

Does SEAL's benefit shrink for frontier-class models? The capability-stratification story predicts stronger agents mismeasure less — is there a crossover where SEAL becomes unnecessary?

How does SEAL interact with an agent-authored held-out validation set of comparable size — would the combination beat SEAL alone, or does the agent's control re-open the gap?

Can the conservative rule cₜ ≥ bₜ − δₜ be replaced by a statistical (confidence-interval) gate that controls false-rejection explicitly rather than via hand-tuned slack ρ, δ_min?
