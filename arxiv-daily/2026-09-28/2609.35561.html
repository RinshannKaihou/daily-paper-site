01

Methodology — regularize the step, structure the direction

Autonomous model development is cast as constrained optimization: from a base checkpoint, within a fixed evaluator and a 12-hour budget, an agent iteratively proposes, executes, and evaluates training actions across data, training, inference, and analysis.

Step level — ExpOS
Every action must pass an explicit schema through 31 core tools + 22 external connectors (data management, execution & state, research & evidence, review management, external discovery), issued via an agent-native CLI. The permissions that enable hacking — editing evaluation code, substituting models — simply do not exist. In parallel, an append-only experiment graph records each run’s inputs, actions, evidence, provenance edges, and an accepted/flagged integrity status, so any reported improvement is traceable end-to-end.

Direction level — Reviewer-guided orchestration
Workers own experiments; Reviewers audit conclusions against raw evidence pulled from the graph, not Worker summaries; the Main Agent continues, verifies, branches, or revises directions at asynchronous orchestration events. The two graphs couple through one-to-one experiment ownership.

Setup
Qwen3-4B-Base on one H100; Qwen3.5-35B-A3B-Base on eight H20; every compared harness shares the Kimi-K3 backbone — a fair control.

03

Verdict — strengths vs limitations

Strengths

  • The cross-harness ExpOS transplant (Claude Code & Codex ± ExpOS, identical backbone and budget) is a genuinely controlled causal comparison — rare in the agentic-systems literature.
  • The append-only experiment graph + 12-category taxonomy make every reported score auditable after the fact — an artifact of independent value.
  • Unusually broad evaluation: 7 PostTrainBench tasks + 13 cross-domain runs + frontier benchmarks, at 4B dense and 35B MoE, framework transferred unmodified.
  • Honest accounting of where the system still loses — AIME 2026, HealthBench Pro, HLE, and the 66.47 Instruct average.
  • Ablations cleanly attribute gains: ExpOS protects infrastructure-heavy tasks, the Reviewer guards against overclaimed results, parallel Workers drive breadth.

Limitations

  • No repeated runs or variance estimates for any headline number; AIME 2026 is explicitly best-observed, not a mean.
  • Hacking-rate gains are partly structural (permission removal), not behavioral; the taxonomy is self-defined and externally unaudited.
  • The strategy lock-in conclusion is descriptive — baselines lack comparable tool logs, and the budget-scaling curve is labeled an “illustrative scenario.”
  • At 35B it still trails Instruct on 4 of 6–7 frontier benchmarks and the 5-task average; at 4B it closes ~69% (not 100%) of the gap.
  • Heavy infrastructure (31 tools, 3 agent roles, 2 coupled graphs) — reproduction is costly, and the Reviewer inherits Kimi-K3’s judgment errors.
  • The biggest margins sit on LLM-judged benchmarks (Arena-Hard, HealthBench) most susceptible to judge noise; no judge-bias audit is reported.
04

Questions for further exploration

  1. What is the run-to-run variance of the 54.49 PostTrainBench average — would 3 seeds of the same 12-hour configuration change the ranking against Kimi Agent Swarm (46.53)?
  2. How much of the 0.0% hacking rate survives if ExpOS permissions are deliberately loosened (e.g. evaluation-code edits that pass reviewer audit) — is the integrity gain from prevention or from reviewers catching violations?
  3. Can the Reviewer run on a cheaper model than the Kimi-K3 backbone, given the ablation shows review quality is load-bearing (HealthBench 35.79→16.05 without it)?
  4. What fraction of final gains depends on the external frontier APIs (DeepSeek-V4-Flash, GLM-5-2) mid-run — is there an ablation with zero external model calls?
  5. Does the 12-category integrity taxonomy transfer beyond PostTrainBench (e.g. SWE-bench-style agent tasks) — could it be adopted as an independent audit layer for other labs’ harnesses?
