Anyone deploying quantized Mamba or hybrid inference inherits rounding errors that compound through thousands of decode steps — this is a drop-in, RNG-free fix.

01

Methodology

Three rounding rules, one formula. To store x with quantization step s, write x/s = k + f and round up iff a threshold u ≥ 1−f. The rules differ only in how u is chosen:

RTNu = 1/2 always — silently drops any update |δ| < s/2, exactly the typical write of a long-memory entry.
SRu ~ U(0,1) per write — unbiased, needs an RNG; the production default in NVIDIA Nemotron 3.
WEYLu<sub>i,t</sub> = {αi + tα}, golden increment α = φ−1 ≈ 0.618, 2-D per-(block, position) offsets — deterministic, no RNG, low discrepancy.

Only the recurrent state is quantized — INT8 / FP8 E4M3 / BF16 — while weights and activations stay FP32. In the decode regime the prompt runs in full precision; error is per-token KL divergence to the FP32 model, teacher-forced over 32 WikiText-103 chunks (pure Mamba) or PG-19 books (hybrids), with 95% bootstrap intervals over documents.

Discipline: hypotheses pre-registered per notebook; a logit-verification gate excluded Falcon-H1 1.5B and Zamba2 1.2B; CPU dry-runs before GPU time; every number traceable to a notebook output. Hardware: one Tesla T4 (16 GB).

02

Mechanism & theory

One threshold formula unifies all rules: accumulated error equals s × the star discrepancy of the threshold sequence (Prop. 1), giving distinct growth laws:

RTNsT·min(f, 1−f) — grows ~linearly with decode steps
SRs√(Tf(1−f)) — random walk, ~√T
WEYL≤ Cs(1 + log T) — logarithmic, essentially flat

The theory predicts the ordering, not the gain size (the real Weyl/SR ratio is a roughly constant ~0.7–0.75).

Localized and predictable: dithering only the 25% longest-memory entries keeps 75–87% of the full gain; recurrence-replay ratios (0.71–0.74) match measured KL ratios (0.70–0.74). Recipe: dither long-memory entries, hold the 10% longest in FP16 → still beats SR by 23–24%.

04

Three silent pitfalls

1 · Near-rational increments

α′ = 1/2 + 2⁻¹²

≈ 40× worse than SR — as bad as RTN for up to 1/(8ε) steps.

2 · Space-time aliasing

Knuth 2654435761golden 2654435769

The constants differ by only 8·2⁻³²; the wrong offset costs +34–40% KL on Mamba-2 INT4.

3 · float32 phase computation

t = 32767 → 512 distinct sequences

Harmless within ~2k steps (median KL shift 0.1%) but reverses the Weyl/SR ordering at 32k context.

All three run green: the code passes every test while the benefit silently vanishes.

05

Strengths

  • 15/15 consistent answer with pre-registered hypotheses and 95% bootstrap discipline — rare rigor at any scale, exceptional for a solo independent researcher.
  • A mechanistic account, not a leaderboard: discrepancy theory predicts the ordering; recurrence replay isolates the cause.
  • Immediately actionable: the 25%-of-entries / 75–87%-of-gain result yields a mixed-precision recipe you can ship.
  • The pitfalls catalog is worth the paper alone for anyone implementing dithered rounding.
  • Operational bonus: a deterministic rule removes RNG generator state from serving systems.
06

Limitations

  • Scale gap: 130M–1.4B on one T4, FP32 host compute, custom runners — no throughput/latency numbers, nothing at 7B+.
  • KL is not task quality: only +1.0 LAMBADA mean in a stress regime; sampled generation showed no detectable difference.
  • Regime-dependent: the edge mostly vanishes under periodic checkpointing on hybrids; SR wins under Hadamard rotation at INT4.
  • Open anomaly: RTN is best for the first ~512 steps on Granite 4.0-H; four pre-planned explanations all failed.
  • Narrow hybrid coverage: 3 models from 2 families — two larger hybrids were excluded after failing verification.
  • Solo, AI-assisted codebase with three found-and-fixed consequential bugs; no independent reimplementation yet.
07

Questions for further exploration

  1. Does the Weyl advantage survive production fused kernels, 7B+ hybrids, and end-to-end task accuracy rather than teacher-forced KL?
  2. Can Granite 4.0-H’s early-decoding RTN advantage be explained — and does it predict which hybrid families favor RTN in short benchmarks?
  3. How does the dither interact with orthogonal transforms (Hadamard rotation) and learned mixed-precision assignments beyond the single INT4 probe?
  4. Can a cheap online estimator identify the 25% longest-memory entries at runtime, enabling a mixed FP16 / INT4+dither cache?
  5. Do the three silent pitfalls exist in deployed SR implementations — including NVIDIA’s hardware stochastic-rounding generator?
