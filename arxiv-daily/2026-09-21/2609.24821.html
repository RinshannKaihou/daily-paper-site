Why this matters

Gives a counterintuitive, testable answer to what probes and steering vectors actually read out — reshaping how interpretability experiments are designed and evaluated.

Unifies self-consistency, semantic entropy, probing and steering under one object: the model’s answer measure, where all continuations yielding the same answer form an “answer basin.”

Critical edge

The headline overclaims: the paper shows label–basin alignment flips readouts — not that concept directions don’t exist — and its only baseline is the authors’ own construction.

Finite-choice masses come from the model’s own averaged letter logits, so “basin ordering” is partly the model agreeing with itself.

Methodology — how the claim was tested

  1. Build the answer measure. Estimate each answer’s probability mass (“basin”): K-sample generation counts (K=64–128) or permuted finite-choice letter logits averaged back onto answers.
  2. Read two boundaries. Hidden states at the final prompt token (direction wQ, question boundary) and final answer token (wA, answer boundary).
  3. Test linear predictions. Signed changes of collision concentration C₂ and pairwise log-mass contrast Δlog m should be linearly readable (Eqs. 5/6/8); linear probes predict mass ordering, Spearman ρ, Top-1.
  4. Two decisive constructions. Wrong-only: supervise solely on mass orderings between two incorrect answers — zero labels. Align/Conflict: keep correct/wrong labels fixed, flip which answer carries larger mass (e.g. paired basin counts 108.22 vs 1.00 → 26.22 vs 101.78).
  5. Steer causally. Add label-defined correct−wrong contrast vectors (v·α ≤ 0.10) into Gemma-3-4B-PT at blocks 19/16; measure length-normalized answer log-prob shifts with bootstrap intervals.

Strengths

  • Crisp, falsifiable formalization: three numbered predictions, each mapped to a dedicated experiment.
  • Wrong-only probe is an elegant supervision-free control — its near-tie with concept supervision is striking.
  • Align/Conflict isolates the alignment variable with labels held fixed; reversal in all 12 combinations, candidate and free-form alike.
  • Honest scope statement: open-ended answer-grouping limits are stated plainly, not buried.
  • Code release plus unusually thorough appendices make the work easy to audit.

Spans two model families, two scales, three attribute domains (correctness, social bias, safety).

Limitations

  • Most experiments need fixed candidate-answer spaces; open-ended generation needs reliable grouping that doesn’t exist yet.
  • Steering evidence: one model, two datasets, one layer each, small strengths (α ≤ 0.10); no layer sweep.
  • No external baselines (CCS, Azaria–Mitchell) — comparisons are internal to the authors’ own constructions.
  • Descriptive-correlational at the concept level; “the answer measure organizes representations” is a strong reading of weaker evidence.
  • Weak cells (SafetyBench ρ=0.387; Low-gap below baseline on ARC-C) suggest the theory fits the mid/large-gap regime best.

The central object is currently best measured exactly where it is least interesting — experimenter-defined candidate sets.

Questions for further exploration

  • Does ABRH survive truly open-ended generation with entailment-based semantic answer grouping?
  • How do wQ and wA relate across layers — coincide, split, rotate? Is there a layer of maximal linear exposure?
  • Can the transport hypothesis (Eq. 8) make per-question steering predictions, not just aggregate-curve ones — and hold at larger α?
  • Do instruction tuning and RLHF systematically shift label–basin alignment, explaining base-vs-aligned steering gaps?
  • Can basin-oriented directions coexist with polysemantic concept directions under superposition — and be disentangled?
