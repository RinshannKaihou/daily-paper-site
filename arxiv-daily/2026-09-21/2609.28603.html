Turning “interestingness” — verified proof length per statement length — into an RL reward makes AI-conjectured theorems 4.3× more interesting.

Learning to Discover Interesting Mathematics

Niket Patel, Ahmad Rammal, Amaury Hayat, Remi Munos, et al. — Meta FAIR · NYU · CERMICS

Key metric

4.3×

Mean Lean-verified interestingness of generated theorems rises 1.76 → 7.58 after RL on the interestingness reward — evaluated over 160 machine-checked statements across 8 areas. ∎

I(T|P) = 100 · V(T|P) / L(T|P)

Why this matters

A working template for open-ended discovery: turn a computable formal metric into an optimizable RL reward, with labels synthesized from one library — no humans.

「可验证环境中的代理指标 → RL → 抑制分布内复现」范式，对其他自举式发现系统（自博弈、自动实验设计）同样有借鉴价值。

⚠ Critical edge

Circular evaluation: ground-truth “interestingness” is measured by Claude Code’s own proof lengths — a theorem trivial for a stronger prover would lose interestingness without changing its mathematics.

judge = prover = baseline → Claude Opus 4.6 · no human-mathematician validation

§01 Methodology

  1. Axiomatize proof difficulty Conditional cost V(T|P) = Lean proof lines to derive T from premises P, under three axioms: grounding, premise monotonicity, Bellman-style composition.
  2. Synthesize labels — premise expansion Walk the Mathlib dependency DAG, “pull out” a cited lemma from the premises; the label is the extra cost c+ℓ of re-deriving it. 113,547 declarations → 18,318 train groups (109,908 prompts) + 923 val groups. Median label 49 lines, max 500 — zero human annotation.
  3. Train the estimator GRPO on Qwen3.6-27B, 350 steps. Reward = 0.5·truth (log scale) + 0.35·Bellman residual + 0.15·premise-drop penalty.
  4. Define interestingness I = 100·V/L — proof lines per statement description length. On Mathlib: 1ⁿ=1 ranks bottom, Fermat (n=3) top; tracks utility U₀ (lines saved downstream) at ρ = 0.756.
  5. Optimize it twice (a) as GRPO reward for a conjecturer (75 steps, R = 0.25 + log(1+İ/100)); (b) as the promotion rule of a 6-round discovery loop. Every headline score uses ground-truth, Lean-verified proof lengths — never the learned estimator.

§03 Strengths

  • Lean-verified grounding — every headline number uses machine-checked proofs; no hallucinated theorems enter any result.
  • Premise expansion is deterministic and seed-controlled: one library → ~110k supervision signals, zero human labels.
  • Only evaluated model that stays calibrated at 300–400 proof lines — exactly where frontier models fail.
  • Pre-selection analysis (Table 2) rules out the cheapest explanation — pure selection of long proofs.
  • Exceptional transparency: full prompts, judge rubrics, hyperparameters, seeds, and negative results all published.

§04 Limitations

  • Lean line counts are a stylistic artifact — automation tactics (aesop, grind) change them at runtime; the authors suggest Levin complexity as a more robust proxy.
  • Small scale: 160 verified statements, one premise pool, 6–10 discovery rounds.
  • One commercial model everywhere — Claude Opus 4.6 is conjecturer, prover, containment judge, and quality judge; self-preference risk.
  • Anchored to one library (Mathlib); cross-library and cross-prover transfer untested.
  • Missing ablations: the three-term reward is never ablated component-wise; no baseline vs TxGraffiti-lineage conjecturing systems.

§05 Questions for further exploration

  1. Do the 1.76 → 7.58 gains survive a weaker or stronger proving agent, given scores come from Claude Code’s proof lengths?
  2. How much does the Bellman residual alone contribute to ρ = 0.912 calibration?
  3. Over tens of rounds, does promoted interestingness keep rising — or collapse into near-duplicate families?
  4. Would human mathematicians endorse the Claude-judge 60% ranked-first verdicts?
  5. Do promoted lemmas actually accelerate later proofs — closing the loop the ρ = 0.756 correlation only suggests?
