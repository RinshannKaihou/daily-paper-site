01

Methodology, distilled

Two evidence streams, both deliberately copied from the paper being critiqued (Guggenmos et al., 2018) to remove the “you changed the setup to win” objection.

Empirical reliability. Reanalysis of the public Cichy, Pantazis & Oliva (2014) MEG dataset — 92 object images, 306 sensors, two sessions per subject. Reliability = Lin's concordance correlation coefficient (ρc) between the two sessions' pairwise distance matrices, per subject and per time point. Between-estimator comparisons use a sign-permutation test across subjects, Bonferroni-corrected for FWER over time from 50 ms onward.

Simulated accuracy. Correlated-normal ground-truth patterns (306 × 92) plus a condition-nonspecific component at 5× the pattern variance, replicated over 2 sessions × 20 trials, with Gaussian noise at SNR {0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2}; 500 iterations per SNR. Accuracy is Lin's CCC between estimated and true distance matrix — sensitive to both geometry and scale.

Appendix A — the reframing d²CV(x,y) = ½[d²(xA,yB) + d²(xB,yA)] − ½[d²(xA,xB) + d²(yA,yB)]

Appendix B — Pearson ↔ Euclidean d²(Z(x), Z(y)) = 2n (1 − r(x,y))  ⟹  correlation distance cross-validates with no regularization.

▸ The decisive result

+0.32 / +0.30 CCC

G.C.V. correlation distance beats within-class correction by +0.32 CCC at SNR 1 (0.43 vs 0.10) and +0.30 at SNR 2 (0.83 vs 0.50) — the part that could actually change practice, and a direct contradiction of Guggenmos et al.'s recommendation to use W.C.C. for correlation distance.

03

Strengths & limitations

▲ Strengths

  • Appendix A turns cross-validation from a trick into an interpretable bias subtraction; both the G.C.V. generalization and the G.C.V.–W.C.C. unification fall out of it.
  • Diagnoses the clipping artifact — the kind of scrutiny methods papers usually skip.
  • Reports results that cut against its own recommendation (W.C.C. wins Euclidean accuracy) rather than burying them.
  • Simulation protocol deliberately copied from the critiqued paper.
  • Concrete, one-line-of-code recommendations; Mahalanobis & Spearman extensions are free.

▼ Limitations

  • No quantitative tables; every effect size must be read off figure axes.
  • No code or data release, no reference implementation, no toolbox integration.
  • The random-partition assumption is never stress-tested — G.C.V. is invalid for blocked designs, and no simulation quantifies the damage.
  • Single dataset, single modality (one MEG dataset; fMRI/EEG/ECoG transfer untested).
  • Correlation-distance bias is reduced, not eliminated; full debiasing still needs generative modelling (PCM).
  • Real-data effect sizes are tiny (~0.003–0.004 ρc Euclidean); large gains appear only in simulation.
04

Open questions for follow-up

  1. How much bias does G.C.V. incur under blocked / non-exchangeable designs, and is there a cheap diagnostic (within- vs between-partition distance distributions) to flag when it is unsafe?
  2. Does the G.C.V. correlation advantage over W.C.C. survive when W.C.C. uses optimally-sized pseudo-trials — and is G.C.V. just the limit of maximal pseudo-trial averaging?
  3. Does the variance reduction scale further with K > 2 partitions (all K(K−1) ordered pairs), and where does it saturate against per-partition SNR loss?
  4. How do these estimators compare against a properly specified generative alternative (PCM / crossnobis with modelled noise covariance)?
  5. Is Lin's CCC the right reliability metric when estimators occupy different natural scales — would rank-based or scale-invariant measures change the ordering?
  6. Do the conclusions hold for Mahalanobis in practice, where the finite-data noise-covariance estimate is shared across partitions?
