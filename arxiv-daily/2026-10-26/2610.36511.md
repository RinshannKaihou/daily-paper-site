> **一句话总结：** 本文审计了 7 种已部署的 AI-科学家流水线新颖性检查，发现"拒得狠的也误杀得多"（如 Si et al. 的检索过滤捕获全部 380 篇伪装论文却误拒 96% 后来被人类实现的点子），并提出声明级（claim-level）可解释模块 CLAIMNOV：以 97% 捕获率、1.5% 误报率兼顾两端，在 100 篇"新颖点子+拼接先验贡献"的混合提案中保住 91 篇（最强基线 CoI-Agent 仅 76 篇），且每次判定成本仅为后者的 1/4（$0.010 vs $0.039）。

> **TL;DR:** The authors audit seven deployed novelty checks in AI-scientist pipelines using a temporal-ground-truth benchmark of 1,060 proposals (disguised ICML 2026 spotlights, later-realized ideas from NeurIPS 2025 anchors, mined ICLR 2022-23 reviewer disputes, and spliced hybrid proposals) and show an awkward trade-off: Si et al.'s retrieval filter catches all 380 disguises but false-rejects 96% of ideas humans later published, while ScholarEval catches nothing at all. Their CLAIMNOV module — claim extraction, same-dimension retrieval, per-claim 5-level entailment, rank-discounted aggregation, and a Sonnet-4.6 verdict over the claim profile — catches 97% of disguises at 1.5% false alarms (informedness J=+0.95 vs +0.93 for CoI-Agent, at a quarter of the per-verdict cost, $0.010 vs $0.039), and on 100 hybrid proposals keeps 91 vs CoI-Agent's 76 while still naming the spliced prior-art paper.

## 核心贡献

- **首个针对已部署新颖性检查的系统审计**：构建了以"时间 ground truth"（知识截止日 T=2026 年 3 月）为核心的评估框架，在提案级与声明级两个粒度上审计了 7 种现有检查（AI Scientist v1、CoI-Agent、Si et al.、ARC、ResearchAgent、ScholarEval、AI-Scientist-v2），揭示出核心矛盾——**捕获率高的检查对"后来被人类实现"的点子误报也高**（Si et al. 误报 96%、ResearchAgent 98.5%），而几乎不拒绝的检查（ScholarEval 捕获 0/380）形同虚设。
- **声明（claim）而非提案（proposal）是新颖性的正确单元**：提出可解释、可即插即用的 CLAIMNOV 模块——抽取带类型维度的贡献声明（Problem/Methodology/Resource/Result）、同维度检索池内声明、逐对 1–5 级蕴含评分、折扣加权聚合成新颖性分数，最后由更强判定模型基于完整声明画像给出 pass/fail 并指名先验论文。
- **四个共 1,060 个提案的评测数据集**：400 篇 ICML 2026 spotlight 的四级伪装（换语态→改写→重构动机→对抗性重组，≤2% 六元组重合）、400 个"后来被实现"的点子（30 篇 NeurIPS 2025 锚文+截止后引用池验证）、160 个从 8.3k 条 ICLR 2022-23 评审中挖掘的"审稿人指认先验"争议对、100 个"新颖点子+拼接先验贡献"的混合提案。
- **细粒度且便宜的判定**：CLAIMNOV 对含单一已建立贡献的提案只拒绝 3%（ICLR 已录取争议论文），远低于会拒绝整篇提案的整体式检查；每次判定调用成本 $0.010，为统计上打平的 CoI-Agent（$0.039）的 1/4。

## Key Contributions

- **A temporal-ground-truth audit of deployed novelty checks.** With a fixed knowledge cutoff T = March 2026 (all judge models have earlier cutoffs), the authors evaluate seven checks from real AI-scientist pipelines plus an OpenNovelty-style claim baseline on four datasets totaling 1,060 proposals, and expose a robustness/false-alarm trade-off: checks that catch non-novel work also reject work the field later publishes (Si et al.: 96.0% false alarms; ResearchAgent: 98.5%; AI Scientist v1: 51.8%), while permissive checks catch almost nothing (ScholarEval: 0/380; ARC: 85/380).
- **CLAIMNOV, an interpretable claim-level novelty module.** It (1) extracts typed contribution claims (dimension ∈ {Problem, Methodology, Resource, Result} plus attribute–value pairs, 2.4–2.7 claims per proposal on average), (2) retrieves k nearest same-dimension pool claims via text-embedding-3-large (k = 0.2·nd, capped at 40), (3) rates entailment on an ordinal 1–5 scale with gpt-4o-mini at temperature 0, (4) aggregates into a novelty score with a rank-discounted mean (α = 0.5) and dimension weights (Problem 0.4, Result 0.3, Methodology 0.2, Resource 0.1), and (5) passes the full claim profile to a Sonnet 4.6 verdict judge that outputs pass/fail, the offending prior paper, the entailed claim subset, and a reason.
- **Claim-level evaluation methodology with reviewer-dispute ground truth.** They mine 8.3k ICLR 2022-23 reviews for cases where a reviewer attributes paper A's contribution to prior work B (160 usable pairs; human annotation: 81/100 raw agreement, Cohen's κ = 0.62 on dispute genuineness), and construct 100 hybrid proposals where a verified prior contribution is spliced into a later-realized idea — fixing correct behavior by construction (pass + name B).
- **Empirical demonstration that whole-proposal checks cannot do claim-level attribution.** Existing checks name prior art only alongside rejection (Si et al. names B for 47% by rejecting ~80% of proposals); given the same dense-retrieval attribution ranking, CLAIMNOV passes and names B for 36% of disputed proposals vs 31% for CoI-Agent (p = .016), and keeps 67/100 hybrids with B named vs 53 for CoI-Agent (p = .004).

## Methodology

CLAIMNOV is a five-stage drop-in module. **Claim extraction** (gpt-4o-mini, temperature 0) turns a proposal's title + 150–400 word description into 1–4 contribution claims, each typed with a dimension from {Problem, Methodology, Resource, Result} and an (attribute, value) pair (e.g., attribute = CIFAR-10 accuracy, value = improvement from 30.1% to 76.9%); the same extraction runs once over every pool paper's abstract. **Claim retrieval** embeds each claim (with its paper title) using text-embedding-3-large and pulls the k-nearest same-dimension pool claims, at most one per pool paper, with k scaled by pool size (k = 10 minimum, k ≈ 35 for the 400-paper anchor pools). **Claim entailment** has gpt-4o-mini rate every (proposal claim, pool claim) pair on a 5-level ordinal scale (1 = unrelated … 5 = full coverage), mapped to e ∈ [0,1] by (ℓ−1)/4. **Novelty aggregation** combines entailments per claim with a rank-discounted mean (α = 0.5, emphasizing the strongest match) and combines claims into N(P) = 1 − A(P)/Amax(P) using hand-set dimension weights. **Novelty verdict** hands the full profile — claims, matched prior claims, sources, entailment levels — to a stronger judge (claude-sonnet-4-6), which returns pass/fail plus, on fail, the prior-art paper, the entailed claim subset, and a 1–2 sentence reason.

The evaluation framework is built around a temporal cutoff T = March 2026: disguised sources are ICML 2026 spotlights, "later-realized" ideas are validated by post-T citations to NeurIPS 2025 anchors, and all judge models (Sonnet 4.6, Jan 2026 cutoff; Opus 4.6 labeler, Aug 2025 cutoff) predate T, mitigating training-data contamination. Disguises are generated at four escalating rungs with a ≤2% six-gram-overlap constraint and a Sonnet 4.6 verifier confirming the core contributions survive. Searches inside existing checks are sandboxed and replaced by dense retrieval over the same pools, so all systems see identical inputs (title + description + pool of {id, title, abstract}).

## Results & Highlights

- **Whole-proposal detection (Table 1, n = 380 disguises + 400 later-realized ideas):** CLAIMNOV catches 367/380 (97%) disguised papers and names the source in 366, with a 1.5% false-alarm rate on later-realized ideas (J = catch − FA = +0.95, pool-flip Δ = +0.92). The strongest deployed check, CoI-Agent, is statistically level (368/380, FA 3.8%, J = +0.93) but its check call costs $0.039 vs CLAIMNOV's $0.010 per verdict. Aggressive checks collapse: Si et al. catches all 380 but rejects 359/380 even with the source removed and false-alarms on 96.0% of later-realized ideas (J = +0.04); ResearchAgent's J is −0.10 (98.5% FA); permissive checks catch nothing (ScholarEval 0/380).
- **The claim profile is what keeps false alarms low:** removing it (verdict judge reads only top-8 nearest abstracts) raises the false-alarm rate from 1.5% to 11.8%; replacing the verdict judge with a per-claim rule ("fail if any central claim is entailed") catches all 380 but explodes false alarms to 56.8% and wrongly rejects 197/380 source-removed proposals — evidence that a proposal must not be treated as one big claim. On noisier generated ideas matched to a pool paper, CLAIMNOV rejects 18% vs 55% for the profile-free judge.
- **Claim-level prior art (Table 2, n = 160 ICLR disputes):** CLAIMNOV rejects only 3% of the 62 ICLR-accepted disputed proposals (CoI-Agent 10%; AI Scientist v1 53%; Si et al. 81%; ResearchAgent 47%) — one disputed claim should not kill a proposal. Given the same dense-embedding attribution ranking, CLAIMNOV passes and names the reviewer's prior work B for 36% of proposals vs 31% for CoI-Agent (p = .016); notably, plain dense embedding alone finds B top-1 for 37% while CLAIMNOV's own claim-centric ranking finds it for only 21%.
- **Hybrid proposals (Table 3, n = 100):** CLAIMNOV keeps 91/100 spliced proposals vs 76 for CoI-Agent (p = .004); with a shared BM25 ranking it keeps 67 hybrids with B correctly named vs 53 for CoI-Agent (p = .004), while the OpenNovelty-style baseline keeps 64 only at the cost of its 11.5% false-alarm rate. Reliability check: in hybrids where the spliced claim is extracted and rated entailment ≥ 4, 13/15 named papers are B, and only 9/400 later-realized ideas ever reach that entailment level.
- **Ground-truth quality:** two annotators plus a blinded third reach 2-of-3 majority of 59/100 genuine disputes and 67/100 correctly identified B (pairwise raw agreements 81/100, κ = 0.62 and 73/100, κ = 0.47), and the 36–40-pair label-clean subset is reported separately.

## Authors & Credibility

The four authors (Miri Liu, Xiayu Zhao, Encheng Xie, ChengXiang Zhai) are from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign; Zhai is a long-established IR/text-mining researcher, and the paper's retrieval-grounded framing of novelty is consistent with that lineage. The work is unusually careful for an LLM-evaluation paper: a fixed temporal cutoff with judge models chosen to predate it, sandboxed search replaced by identical dense retrieval for all systems, paired exact McNemar tests and cluster-bootstrap intervals (grouping by source paper and anchor) in the appendix, human annotation with reported Cohen's κ (including dropping a label set whose κ was too low), ablations of every stage, and honest reporting that plain dense embedding beats their own claim-centric ranking at attribution. Code and data are released at github.com/emmatliu/claimnov. The first author acknowledges Amazon AI PhD Fellowship and DOE CSGF support; the work is funded in part by NSF grants 2229612 and 2433308.

## Critical Assessment

- **Novelty:** Genuine and well-motivated. Prior work evaluated novelty judges against static human labels; the counterfactual disguise/later-realized design (akin to paraphrase attacks on detectors) plus reviewer-dispute mining at claim level is a real methodological step forward, and the audit of seven deployed checks is the first of its kind.
- **Rigor:** Strong on statistics (McNemar, cluster bootstrap, κ reported, ablations, per-dataset appendix results), but the headline "97% catch at 1.5% FA" sits on a favorable regime: the source paper is guaranteed present in a 20-paper pool, versus open-ended search in deployment. The authors admit pools "limit our ability to evaluate the retrieval of novelty checks." The later-realized ground truth is LLM-labeled (Opus 4.6) with only a small human audit, and the claim-dispute clean subset shrinks to 36 pairs.
- **Self-judging bias:** Sonnet 4.6 generates the disguises and ideas and also serves as the verdict judge for every system — a known self-preference confound, acknowledged but not eliminated; Opus 4.6 as labeler partially mitigates it.
- **A quiet negative result is buried in §5.2:** CLAIMNOV's claim-based ranking identifies the reviewer's prior work B top-1 for only 21% of disputed proposals, *worse* than the plain dense-embedding baseline (37%) — the paper's fix is to feed the dense ranking to the verdict, meaning the claim machinery drives the verdict but not the attribution it advertises. Extraction failures are cited as the cause.
- **Hand-set aggregation weights:** the dimension prior (Problem 0.4, Result 0.3, Methodology 0.2, Resource 0.1) is asserted, not learned or ablated against data, and the authors concede it may not generalize beyond AI research or English.
- **Overclaiming risk:** the abstract's "catches 97% of disguised papers" is accurate but the disguises are synthetic rewrites of one source each; the audit's most damning numbers (96–98.5% FA) come from checks run with the authors' chosen decision rules for three systems (marked † in Table 1), though best-in-sample thresholds are provided in Appendix K.

## Strengths

- Identifies and quantifies a real, previously undocumented failure mode of AI-scientist pipelines: novelty checks that burn the pipeline's budget of novel ideas by rejecting what the field later publishes.
- The claim-level unit is the right abstraction — matching how incremental science actually works — and the module outputs an interpretable, auditable profile (which claim, which prior paper, what entailment level) rather than an opaque score.
- Excellent evaluation hygiene: temporal cutoffs, sandboxed/standardized inputs across heterogeneous systems, paired significance tests, human annotation with reported (and acted-upon) inter-annotator agreement.
- Practically cheap: $0.010 per verdict makes it deployable as a filter (or even a training reward, as the authors propose) rather than a one-off benchmark.

## Limitations

- Fixed reference pools (20–400 papers) rather than open search, so retrieval quality — arguably the hardest part of real novelty checking — is largely untested.
- The headline dataset is adversarial rewrites of a single source with only 19 distractors; real plagiarism/reinvention is messier and often spans papers outside any top-19 embedding neighborhood.
- Claim-disputed papers predate T and may be in judges' training data (accepted by the authors because ground truth is reviewer attribution, not a post-T event, but it weakens the temporal argument for that dataset).
- Dimension weights and the α = 0.5 discount are hand-set heuristics validated only implicitly; the benchmark itself decays as newer judges (post-T cutoffs) are released and everything must be regenerated.
- No evaluation of the check inside a live generate-reject-regenerate loop (only a small cost pilot in Appendix M), which is where such modules will actually be deployed.

## Questions for Further Exploration

- How does CLAIMNOV behave with open-ended Semantic Scholar search instead of curated 20–400 paper pools — does the 1.5% false-alarm rate survive when hundreds of near-neighbor papers compete with the true source?
- Can the claim-ranking attribution gap (21% vs 37% for dense embedding) be closed by better claim extraction, or is whole-proposal embedding similarity simply the right signal for attribution while claims are only right for verdicts?
- Since CoI-Agent is statistically level on whole-proposal metrics at 4x the cost, what is the Pareto frontier when CLAIMNOV is used as a training reward for idea-generation agents rather than as a filter?
- Do the hand-set dimension weights (Problem 0.4 > Result 0.3 > Methodology 0.2 > Resource 0.1) match expert intuition, and can they be learned from reviewer-dispute data?
- How do the results transfer beyond AI/ML research and English-language proposals, where claim extraction conventions and contribution typing may differ?
