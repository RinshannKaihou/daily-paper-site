01

How it works

Methodology

PrimeScientist wraps the autonomous-research loop in an explicit allocation policy. A reflector (GPT-5) drafts a structured Markdown research plan; after each execution it reads the recorded reward, repository state, and tree context, then proposes up to three substantively different children — every edge stored as an executable diff.py, so any shelved direction survives to be revisited.

A budget-adaptive MCTS rule samples branches with probability ∝ Qαt, where αt = min(1/rt, 10) and rt is the remaining-budget ratio: dispersed exploration while tokens are plentiful, convergence onto high-value branches as the budget drains. Picking an untested node launches a full research attempt via a Codex CLI coding agent; picking an evaluated one grows alternatives. Values backpropagate as descendant means; children ≥0.05 below their parent's branch value are pruned. Planning and execution are charged to the same 1.5M-token budget — choosing directions costs the same currency as running them.

  • Gains are concentrated. Drop MCQ Selection Bias (0.429→1.000) and the FIRE-Bench edge falls to +3.7% (0.7533 vs 0.7266); non-gated AutoLab tasks are a wash (0.5052 vs 0.5061).

  • Planning is not free. The reflector consumed 38% of tokens (coding agent 62%) — "fewer attempts" partly restates "tokens spent planning instead."
03

Strengths

what holds
  • A real, previously implicit problem — agents propose more directions than they can afford to test — made formal, with resources inside the state.
  • The executable plan tree does real scientific work: diff-based edges make strategies reconstructable and auditable (case study: image-structure tests lift F1 0.689 → 0.857).
  • Above-average evaluation hygiene: planning tokens charged, matched-token and matched-attempt comparisons, budget sweep, and an ablation varying only the selection rule.
  • Honest sub-analyses — excludes the best task, reports accuracy behind a gated zero reward, and names the n=1 weakness in its own limitations.
04

Limitations

what doesn't
  • Single GPT-5 search per configuration; no confidence intervals anywhere — and repeated runs show the reward advantage collapsing (0.4363 vs 0.4359).
  • Beats naive allocation, not the state of the art: AIDE, AI Scientist-v2, R&D-Agent, MARS, AlphaLab appear only in a comparison table, never in the experiments.
  • Plan-level granularity: effort can't be reallocated mid-attempt — even a small plan tweak costs a full executor invocation.
  • Token accounting omits training compute, GPU-hours, and monetary cost.
  • Hyperparameters (α_max=10, δ=0.05, m=3) fixed globally, no per-task sensitivity; FIRE-Bench is an LLM-judge evaluator co-authored by the team — reward noise compounds the n=1 issue.

Open questions

worth chasing
Q1

Does the 0.7738 vs 0.7018 gap survive 3–5 GPT-5 searches — or AlphaLab's Strategist at the same budget?

Q2

Could the allocation policy be learned rather than hand-designed via the αt schedule?

Q3

Can allocation go intra-attempt — suspend, resume, or reallocate mid-execution?

Q4

Does FIRE-Bench's double-execution counting inflate the attempt-reduction headline?

Q5

Is strategic allocation worth more or less once cheap executors make brute force affordable?

Q6

How susceptible is the tree to exploiting its single LLM-judge reward signal?
