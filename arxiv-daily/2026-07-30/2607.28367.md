> **一句话总结：** 对 150 条来自五个主流计算机使用代理（CUA）基准（OSWorld、WebArena、VisualWebArena、WorkArena、AssistantBench）的公开失败轨迹进行人工+双 LLM 裁判复核，发现 15.3% 的 FAIL 判定是错的（10.7% 是评估器假阴性，4.7% 是任务本身损坏），并将真实失败细分为三层诊断体系，指出"验证/反馈盲目"（39.3%，其中无效重复动作占 29.5%）而非执行/落地错误（仅 13.9%）才是主要失败来源。

> **TL;DR:** This audit paper re-examines 150 publicly released FAIL-scored trajectories across five CUA benchmarks (OSWorld, WebArena, VisualWebArena, WorkArena, AssistantBench) using two independent vision-enabled LLM judges plus human blind review, finding that 15.3% of FAIL verdicts are wrong — ranging from 0% (WorkArena) to 21.7% (WebArena string-matching false negatives; AssistantBench broken tasks from CAPTCHA/dead search engines) — and that among genuine failures, verification/feedback breakdowns (39.3%, dominated by feedback-blind action repetition at 29.5%) outweigh execution/grounding errors (13.9%), overturning the common assumption that CUA failures are mostly click/localization mistakes.

## 核心贡献

- 提出一个四阶段"评估可靠性框架"（任务构建 → 执行环境观测 → 打分 → 报告），把 CUA 基准得分重新定性为"测量管道的输出"而非对代理能力的直接观测，并在框架中定位三类系统性风险：任务有效性/污染、评估器脆弱性、诊断不透明性。
- 对 5 个基准共 150 条公开的、已被判定为失败（FAIL/zero-reward）的轨迹做了确定性分层抽样复核（随机种子 20260717），用两名独立的视觉增强 LLM 裁判（GPT-5.5 via OpenAI Codex CLI、Anthropic Claude Sonnet）加两组独立人工复核者进行盲评，量化"判决可信度"。
- 将多智能体失败分类法 MAST（Cemri et al., 2025）改造为单智能体计算机使用场景的三层诊断体系（Tier 1 规划/意图设定、Tier 2 执行/落地、Tier 3 验证/反馈），并给出从 MAST 到该分类法的完整映射表（附录 B）。
- 通过消融实验证明"证据完整性"（尤其是截图）对判决和诊断结果有实质性影响：去掉截图会翻转 12-13 条 LLM 裁判判决，并使检测到的评估器假阴性数量近乎减半。
- 基于审计结果，为基准设计者提出分阶段的具体准则（任务构建应做预发布重放校验、环境应固定并公开执行栈、打分应用校准过的评审/评分标准而非纯脚本、报告应披露轨迹证据与诊断而不仅是标量成功率）。

## Key Contributions

- Frames a CUA benchmark score as the output of a four-stage measurement pipeline (task construction → execution environment → scoring → reporting) rather than a direct observation of agent capability, and locates three recurring threats within it: task validity/contamination, evaluator fragility, and diagnostic opacity.
- Audits 150 publicly released FAIL-scored trajectories drawn from five benchmarks (OSWorld-Verified, and AgentRewardBench's WebArena/VisualWebArena/WorkArena/AssistantBench subsets) via a deterministic, stratified sampling procedure (seed 20260717), reviewed independently by two vision-enabled LLM judges and two blind human annotator groups.
- Adapts MAST (Cemri et al., 2025), originally a multi-agent coordination failure taxonomy, into a single-agent, three-tier CUA diagnostic codebook: Tier 1 (planning & specification: spec violation, plan loop, hallucinated feature), Tier 2 (execution & grounding: grounding, tool arguments, state loss), Tier 3 (verification & feedback: premature termination, missed verification, feedback-blind no-op repetition), with a documented provenance mapping (Appendix B, Table 4).
- Shows via an evidence-ablation experiment that removing screenshots from the trajectory flips 12 of the Codex judge's and 13 of the Claude judge's verdicts, and roughly halves the number of evaluator false negatives each judge detects (14→8 and 10→6 respectively), establishing that screenshot-inclusive, replayable trajectories are a precondition for both auditing checker validity and diagnosing genuine failures.
- Derives stage-specific design guidelines (Table 3) for task construction, environment disclosure, calibrated scoring, and evidence-complete reporting, and previews next-generation mechanisms (living task pools, seeded-randomization environments, Agent-as-a-Judge scoring, automatic no-op/critical-window diagnostic probes, dollar-cost-per-verified-success reporting).

## Methodology

The paper treats CUA evaluation as a four-stage pipeline (Figure 1): (1) **task construction** — is the task domain-valid and specified without leakage/staleness; (2) **execution environment** — is the environment reproducible and observable; (3) **scoring** — does the checker recognize valid outcomes; (4) **reporting** — does the output support diagnosis. Reliability failures are mapped onto specific stages (task/environment validity and contamination at stages 1-2; evaluator fragility at stage 3; observability/diagnostic opacity spanning stages 2 and 4).

For the empirical audit, the authors formed unique (benchmark, task, agent, experiment) tuples from AgentRewardBench (Lù et al., 2025), shuffled within benchmark-agent strata, round-robin interleaved agents, and kept only trajectories with released cumulative reward zero until reaching quotas of 25 WebArena, 25 WorkArena, 24 VisualWebArena, and 24 AssistantBench trajectories (158 total, 8 later excluded for codebook calibration). From OSWorld-Verified they took 20 zero-reward trajectories from each of three released agent runs (60 total), round-robin interleaved across application domains. The final audited set is 150 trajectories: 57 OSWorld, 23 each of WebArena/VisualWebArena/AssistantBench, and 24 WorkArena. No new agent rollouts were performed — every sampled unit was a released FAIL/zero-reward trajectory, so the audit measures false negatives among recorded failures, not false positives among successes.

Labeling is a two-stage decision. **Stage 1** classifies each recorded FAIL as: genuine agent failure (a human grader would also reject it), evaluator false negative (the outcome actually satisfies the task but the checker rejects it), broken task (infeasible spec, stale reference state, or environment/harness blocks completion), or unclear (evidence insufficient). **Stage 2** applies the three-tier taxonomy only to genuine failures, assigning the *earliest* decisive failure in the trajectory (a "boundary rule": e.g., repeated actions without state change is Tier 1 if no alternative strategy exists, but Tier 3 if a viable plan existed and the agent simply failed to notice an ineffective action). Two vision-enabled LLM judges (GPT-5.5 via OpenAI Codex CLI v0.144.5, and an Anthropic Claude Sonnet model) independently label all 150 trajectories, blind to each other's outputs, using the same written codebook and step-level reasoning/actions/screenshots. Human review covers all 74 rows where the LLMs disagree plus a stratified 30-row sample of LLM-agreement rows (104 rows total), each independently labeled by two human groups via a screenshot-replay interface, blind to each other and to the LLM outputs; the remaining 46 rows use two-LLM consensus (since every LLM disagreement was human-reviewed).

## Results & Highlights

- **Overall verdict reliability**: Across 150 audited FAIL verdicts, **15.3%** are wrong (95% Wilson CI [10.4, 22.0]) — **10.7%** [6.7, 16.6] are evaluator false negatives and **4.7%** [2.3, 9.3] are broken tasks; a further 3.3% remain unclear. Of the 122 genuine failures identified after removing wrong/unclear verdicts, only these are passed to the diagnostic stage.
- **Per-benchmark wrong-verdict rates diverge sharply by mechanism**: WebArena is wrong on **21.7%** of its 23 audited FAILs, driven by string/URL/DOM-based answer matching that rejects acceptable alternative answers (5 of the 5 WebArena EFNs cited stemmed from strict string matching, e.g. an agent that correctly listed five single-book recommendations among top-ten-books posts was still scored FAIL). AssistantBench is also wrong on **21.7%** of its 23 FAILs, but almost entirely from broken tasks (dead search engines, Google CAPTCHA walls hit during open-web navigation) rather than evaluator false negatives. OSWorld is wrong on **17.5%** of 57 FAILs (8 evaluator false negatives from state checkers missing alternative valid solutions, e.g. a `/sys/class/power_supply`-based battery task that was unsatisfiable on the released VM image, plus 2 broken tasks). VisualWebArena is wrong on **13.0%** of 23 FAILs. WorkArena's programmatic ServiceNow-record/field checks produced **0 wrong verdicts in its 24-trajectory sample (0.0%)** — the most reliable checker in the audit.
- **Failure-diagnosis distribution among the 122 genuine failures** overturns the "execution error" intuition: Tier 3 (verification & feedback) failures dominate at **39.3%** — with feedback-blind no-op repetition (repeating an action while the screen observably does not change) alone accounting for **29.5%**, the single largest category (illustrated by a genuine WorkArena failure where the agent clicked "ALL RESULTS" 27 times while the screenshot remained unchanged). Tier 1 (planning & specification) failures account for **35.2%** (specification violations 14.8%, plan loops 16.4%, hallucinated features 4.1%). Tier 2 (execution & grounding — the category closest to "the model clicked the wrong thing") accounts for only **13.9%**. A residual 6.6% are ambiguous across tiers and 4.9% fall outside the three tiers entirely.
- **Evidence sensitivity ablation**: removing screenshots from the reviewed trajectories flips 12 of the Codex judge's verdicts and 13 of the Claude judge's verdicts, reduces the evaluator false negatives the two judges detect from 14→8 and 10→6 respectively (roughly halved), and lowers their Stage-1 verdict agreement from κ=0.71 (raw 92.7%) to κ=0.60 — demonstrating that screenshot-inclusive trajectories are necessary, not merely nice-to-have, for both checker auditing and failure diagnosis.
- **Annotator/label reliability itself is only moderate**: Stage 1 (genuine-failure vs. false-negative vs. broken) LLM-LLM agreement is κ=0.71 (substantial), but Stage 2 (three-tier diagnosis) LLM-LLM agreement drops to κ=0.41 (moderate). On the 104-row blind human-review stress set, human-human κ=0.59 (85.6% raw), pairwise human-LLM κ ranges 0.19-0.32 (76.0-81.7% raw), and four-rater Fleiss' κ=0.36. Across the four annotator groups (GPT-5.5, Claude Sonnet-5, human group 1, human group 2), the binary wrong-verdict rate on the same 104 rows varies from 7.7% to 20.2% (mean 13.7%, std 5.1 percentage points), underscoring that even the paper's own headline 15.3% figure is annotator-sensitive.

No baseline agent or scoring model is proposed or beaten in this paper — it is a measurement/audit study of existing benchmarks' released verdicts, not a new agent or leaderboard entry, so there is no agent-performance delta to report beyond the wrong-verdict rates above.

The team does not include authors of the original benchmarks being audited (WebArena, OSWorld, WorkArena, AssistantBench, AgentRewardBench), which supports the audit's independence but also means the paper is not calibrated by, or accountable to, the benchmark maintainers' own internal QA processes.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty**: The core idea — that a binary FAIL/PASS score is a pipeline artifact rather than a direct capability measurement — is not entirely new (it echoes concerns already raised in the cited AgentRewardBench, WebArena-Verified, and MAST papers), but this paper's contribution is a systematic, cross-benchmark empirical quantification of the problem (15.3% wrong-verdict rate with confidence intervals) plus a clean adaptation of MAST to single-agent CUA. This is a meta-evaluation/audit contribution, not a new benchmark or new agent.

**Rigor**: The double-blind, two-LLM-judge-plus-two-human-group design with a documented codebook, deterministic seed, Wilson confidence intervals, and an explicit "boundary rule" for earliest-failure attribution is a genuinely careful audit protocol, and the authors are commendably transparent about its weak points. Two paper-specific limitations stand out: (1) the Stage 2 diagnostic taxonomy — the very three-tier breakdown that produces the paper's most quotable finding (39.3% Tier 3 vs. 13.9% Tier 2) — only reaches κ=0.41 LLM-LLM agreement and κ=0.36 four-rater Fleiss' κ, meaning the tier attribution itself is only moderately reliable, a tension the authors surface in their own "Annotation reliability" subsection rather than hiding; (2) the authors explicitly flag in their Limitations section that per-benchmark samples are small (23-57 trajectories), so per-benchmark rates (e.g., WorkArena's 0/24) "carry wide intervals and should be read as evidence that evaluator error occurs at a material rate, not as calibrated per-benchmark estimates."

**Reproducibility**: Sampling procedure (seed 20260717), quotas, and the MAST-to-CUA provenance mapping (Appendix B, Table 4) are documented in enough detail to reconstruct the sampling frame, and the scoping-review protocol (Appendix D) is unusually candid about its own limitations (no PRISMA-style tallies, iterative rather than single-pass screening). However, the paper does not state whether the 150 labeled trajectories, the human-annotation interface, or the full codebook text are being released as a dataset/artifact — without that release, third parties cannot independently re-verify the 15.3% headline figure or the tier-diagnosis labels.

**Significance**: If the audit's findings generalize, they have real implications for how the field reads CUA leaderboards: a 15.3% wrong-FAIL rate (and up to 21.7% for some benchmarks) is large enough to reorder rankings between similarly-performing agents, and the finding that verification/feedback failures (39.3%) dominate over grounding failures (13.9%) redirects where engineering effort should go — toward better self-verification and loop detection rather than purely better click/localization models.

**Red flags / honest caveats already disclosed by the authors**: (1) the audit samples only FAIL-scored trajectories, so it says nothing about false positives (reward hacking / superficially-correct-but-invalid successes) — the authors explicitly note "the complementary audit of PASS verdicts remains open"; (2) the §6 "automatic diagnostic probe" proposal (flagging feedback-blind no-op repetition at zero model cost) is explicitly labeled "conceptual" and is never validated against the human labels the paper itself collected — an admitted gap between proposal and validation; (3) WorkArena's suspiciously clean 0/24 wrong-verdict rate is presented at face value without much scrutiny of whether this reflects genuinely superior checker design or simply a small, favorable sample (n=24 is the smallest per-benchmark cell besides VisualWebArena/AssistantBench/WebArena at n=23).

## Strengths

- Careful, statistically transparent audit design: deterministic stratified sampling, two independent LLM judges plus two independent human groups, Wilson confidence intervals, and an explicit boundary rule for attributing the earliest causal failure rather than the most visually salient symptom.
- A concrete, actionable evidence-ablation finding (screenshot removal flips 12-13 verdicts and nearly halves detected false negatives) that gives benchmark designers a falsifiable reason to release screenshot-inclusive trajectories rather than just final scores.
- Honest, detailed self-reported limitations (small per-benchmark n, no PASS-side audit, unvalidated diagnostic-probe proposal, non-PRISMA screening) that model good scientific practice rather than overclaiming.
- Practical, stage-mapped design guidelines (Table 3) that translate the audit findings into checklist-style recommendations benchmark authors can act on directly.

## Limitations

- Small per-benchmark sample sizes (23-57 trajectories per benchmark) mean the per-benchmark wrong-verdict rates carry wide confidence intervals and should not be treated as precise, comparable benchmark rankings.
- The audit only reviews trajectories that were already scored FAIL; it cannot detect or quantify false positives (agents rewarded for invalid shortcuts), leaving reward-hacking-style evaluator failures entirely unmeasured.
- The Stage 2 three-tier diagnostic taxonomy has only moderate inter-rater reliability (LLM-LLM κ=0.41, four-rater Fleiss' κ=0.36), which weakens confidence in the specific tier-share numbers (e.g., the 39.3% Tier 3 vs. 13.9% Tier 2 split) even though the broader Stage 1 genuine-failure-vs-wrong-verdict distinction is more reliable (κ=0.71).
- The literature survey underlying the benchmark landscape (Table 1, §3) is explicitly a non-systematic "scoping review" without PRISMA-style inclusion/exclusion counts, so the claimed representativeness of the benchmark taxonomy is not independently auditable.
- The proposed lightweight diagnostic probe (automatic no-op detection) is conceptual only and untested against the paper's own human-labeled data, so its practical accuracy and false-positive rate (e.g., on legitimate no-change intervals from page loads/async rendering, which the authors themselves warn could be misflagged) remain unknown.

## Relevance

这篇论文对任何依赖公开 CUA 基准（如 OSWorld、WebArena、AssistantBench）做模型选型或论文对比的研究者都直接相关：它提示读者审视 FAIL/PASS 标量分数背后的评估管道，尤其在做 agent 能力横向比较或复现基准结果时，应关注截图等原始证据是否可得、评估器是否可能存在假阴性偏差。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- What would the complementary audit of PASS-scored trajectories reveal about evaluator false positives (reward hacking), and how would that shift the net wrong-verdict rate reported here?
- Would validating the proposed automatic no-op/feedback-blind detection probe against the paper's own 150 human-labeled trajectories confirm its claimed zero-cost diagnostic value, or would it misfire on legitimate no-change intervals (e.g., async page loads) as the authors themselves worry?
- How does the 15.3% wrong-verdict rate, and the Tier 3 (verification/feedback) dominance finding, change if the audit is repeated on the newer long-horizon benchmarks the paper discusses but does not empirically audit (OSWorld 2.0, Agents' Last Exam, WeaveBench)?
- Would using a stronger or larger panel of LLM judges (beyond the two used here) raise the only-moderate κ=0.41 Stage 2 diagnostic agreement to a level that would make the tier-share breakdown more trustworthy as a benchmark-design signal?
- Can the stage-specific design guidelines in Table 3 be operationalized into an automated "benchmark reliability score" that practitioners could compute before trusting a new CUA leaderboard?
