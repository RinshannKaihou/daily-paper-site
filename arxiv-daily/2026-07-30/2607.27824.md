> **一句话总结：** STEREODISCO 把心理学中的"语义差异法"搬进 LLM 内部激活空间，用 WordNet 反义词对构造约 2000 条候选语义轴，通过 mass-mean probing 在注意力头输出中恢复几何轴，再用 KS 检验判断社会群体投影是否偏离随机对照，发现 Llama-3-8B 与 Mistral-7B 在社会群体刻板印象上彼此的一致度（Warmth 0.73、Competence 0.75）远高于二者与人类标注的一致度（Warmth 0.55-0.57、Competence 0.62-0.63），并独立验证发现了三条社会心理学词典之外的新刻板印象轴（humble vs. proud、narrow-minded vs. broad-minded、cowardly vs. brave）。

> **TL;DR:** STEREODISCO adapts the semantic differential method to LLM internal representations: it builds ~2,000 candidate semantic axes from WordNet antonym synsets, recovers each as a geometric direction via mass-mean probing over attention-head outputs, and uses a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to flag axes along which social-group projections diverge from a frequency-matched random-phrase baseline. Applied to LLAMA-3-8B-INSTRUCT and MISTRAL-7B-INSTRUCT, position-prediction accuracy against Fraser et al. (2021)'s human Warmth/Competence labels is only 0.55-0.63, while the two models agree with each other at 0.73-0.75 — evidence that LLM-internal stereotype content diverges more from documented human stereotypes than it converges with them. The framework also surfaces three new stereotypical axes (cowardly/brave, narrow-minded/broad-minded, humble/proud) outside the Nicolas et al. (2021) stereotype dictionary, independently confirmed by human annotators.

## 核心贡献

- 提出 STEREODISCO 框架，包含四个可独立替换的组件：语义轴来源（WordNet 反义词 synset）、语义轴表征（probing 得到几何轴）、概念投影（把目标概念在几何轴上打分）、以及刻板印象性检验（KS 检验对比目标概念与随机参照集的投影分布）。
- 首次将"语义差异法"系统性地应用到 LLM 的内部激活（而非词嵌入或层平均表示），并证明语义轴在 Llama-3-8B-Instruct 与 Mistral-7B-Instruct 的一小部分中后期注意力头中呈线性可分的几何方向。
- 案例研究覆盖 1,999 条候选反义轴中的社会群体刻板印象子集，用 Fraser et al. (2021) 汇编的 Warmth/Competence 人类标签和自建的 100 条分层抽样语义轴人工标注作为金标准。
- 发现两个不同厂商、不同架构的 LLM 在社会群体评分上彼此的一致性显著高于它们与人类标注的一致性，说明 LLM 编码的刻板印象内容与社会心理学文献记载的人类刻板印象存在系统性偏离。
- 在 Nicolas et al. (2021) 的刻板印象词典之外，独立发现并经人工标注确认的新刻板印象语义轴，包括 humble vs. proud、narrow-minded vs. broad-minded、cowardly vs. brave。

## Key Contributions

- A four-component, swappable framework (semantic axis source, representation, concept projection, stereotypicality test) that operationalizes the semantic differential method inside LLM activation space rather than on word embeddings.
- Empirical localization: linear probing across all layer-head pairs of LLAMA-3-8B-INSTRUCT and MISTRAL-7B-INSTRUCT shows semantic axes are linearly encoded as geometric directions in a small subset of middle-to-late attention heads.
- A large-scale candidate pool (1,999 WordNet antonym-synset pairs, 5,182 unique adjectives) that goes well beyond the small, theory-driven axis sets (e.g., Warmth/Competence) used in prior stereotype research.
- A case study comparing STEREODISCO's social-group projections and stereotypicality classifications against both established social-psychology survey labels (Fraser et al., 2021) and a fresh 100-axis human annotation effort (5 annotators after quality filtering, Fleiss' κ = 0.375).
- Discovery of previously uninvestigated stereotypical axes (e.g., cowardly/brave, narrow-minded/broad-minded, humble/proud) that fall outside the standard stereotype dictionary but are independently confirmed by human raters — with the practical implication that steering/mitigation methods targeting only predefined axes will miss such content.

## Methodology

STEREODISCO has four components, each instantiated with a specific design choice in this paper:

1. **Semantic axis source**: candidate axes are pairs of antonymous WordNet adjective synsets (M = 1,999 pairs, 5,182 unique adjectives; WordNet defines antonymy at the sense level, e.g., `large.a.01` vs `small.a.01`). Each pole is turned into N = 30 labeled sentences by inserting the adjective and its WordNet definition into 30 "Listing" templates (e.g., "List adjectives similar to {ADJ}, in the sense of {DEF}."), chosen over "Simple" templates (e.g., "{ADJ} means: {DEF}.") based on an ablation showing Listing templates outperform Simple templates on both models, with the gap widening as ensemble size k grows.

2. **Semantic axis representation**: for each axis, sentences are passed through the LLM and attention-head outputs x_{ℓ,h} are read at every layer ℓ ∈ {1..32} and head h ∈ {1..32} (d_head = 128 for both models), using the **mean activation over all tokens** (chosen over last-token pooling because an ablation and layer-wise variance-ratio heatmaps show last-token signal concentrates in early layers, inconsistent with the known middle-to-late emergence of semantic representations in decoder-only LLMs). **Mass-mean probing** (difference of positive-pole and negative-pole class means) is used instead of logistic regression because it deterministically preserves the axis direction under label swap. Candidate layer-head pairs are scored by a between-class/within-class variance ratio (Eq. 4), and the top-k = 128 pairs (chosen via an ablation sweeping k up to 1024) are aggregated by averaging z-scored projections.

3. **Concept projection**: for a concept c (e.g., a social group name), the LLM is prompted with "Provide the best description of {c} using adjectives," the resulting head activation is projected onto each geometric axis via cosine-style normalized dot product (Eq. 6), and per-head projections are z-scored jointly over the concept set C and a reference set C′ before being averaged across the selected top-k heads.

4. **Stereotypicality test**: a two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test compares the projection distribution of a relevant concept set C (e.g., 50 social group mentions) against a frequency-matched random reference set C′ (50 random phrases); an axis is classified stereotypical when p < 0.05. A Monte Carlo bootstrap power analysis (1,000 resamples) is used to assess whether n=50 per set gives adequate statistical power.

The case study on social-group stereotypes uses two ground-truth sources: (a) Fraser et al. (2021)'s compiled Warmth/Competence labels for 25 social groups, mapped onto 50 Warmth and 42 Competence semantic axes drawn from the Nicolas et al. (2021) stereotype dictionary, for **position prediction** (does the sign of the projection match the ground-truth pole); and (b) a fresh human annotation effort over a stratified sample of 100 WordNet antonym axes (filtered to 76 after removing "not applicable" axes), rated by volunteer annotators on a 1-5 scale per social group, for **stereotypicality identification** (does STEREODISCO's KS-test verdict match majority human judgment).

## Results & Highlights

- **Position prediction accuracy** (STEREODISCO's projection sign vs. human Warmth/Competence ground truth, Fraser et al. 2021 labels, Fig. 1): Human-Llama agreement is 0.63 on Competence and 0.57 on Warmth; Human-Mistral agreement is 0.62 on Competence and 0.55 on Warmth. Crucially, **Llama-Mistral agreement with each other (0.75 Competence, 0.73 Warmth) exceeds either model's agreement with humans** on both dimensions — the paper's central empirical finding.
- **Stereotypicality classification agreement** (Fig. 2, 76 stratified WordNet axes, KS test at α=0.05 vs. majority human vote): STEREODISCO instantiated with LLAMA-3-8B-INSTRUCT agrees with human stereotypical/non-stereotypical judgments on 43/76 axes (57%); with MISTRAL-7B-INSTRUCT, agreement is 45/76 axes (59%). Of the 76 axes, both humans and STEREODISCO(Llama) jointly flag 31 as stereotypical and jointly flag 12 as non-stereotypical, while 19 are human-only and 14 are model-only.
- **Newly discovered, human-confirmed stereotypical axes** (Table 1, KS statistic per model, all significant at p<0.05 except one flagged entry): unreasonable/reasonable (Llama 0.62, Mistral 0.44), cowardly/brave (Llama 0.56, Mistral 0.32), narrow-minded/broad-minded (Llama 0.34, Mistral 0.60), retarded/precocious (Llama 0.32, Mistral 0.40), and humble/proud (Llama 0.28, Mistral 0.16 — the Mistral value is explicitly marked as **not** significant, i.e., this axis is only confirmed as stereotypical for one of the two models).
- **Ablation on ensemble size k** (Appendix A, position prediction accuracy on the Warmth/Competence benchmark): accuracy rises from ~0.50 at k=1 to ~0.68-0.69 at k=512-1024 on Competence, but only modestly on Warmth, peaking around k=128 before plateauing — motivating the paper's fixed operating point of k=128.
- **Qualitative projection distributions** (Fig. 3): on the stereotypical axis inferior.a.01↔superior.a.02, social-group mentions ("uneducated people" vs. "CEOs") spread across the full range while random phrases cluster near the center; on the non-stereotypical axis slow.a.01↔fast.a.01, social groups and random phrases have near-identical medians, visually confirming the KS test's distinction.

No quantitative comparison against a computational baseline method (e.g., WEAT/CEAT-style word-embedding stereotype tests) is reported in the main results — the paper's own text (Appendix A) notes there is no ground truth for what an LLM "should" encode, so cross-method comparison is treated as an open problem rather than a benchmarked result.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty**: Genuinely novel angle — prior computational work (WEAT/CEAT, SEAT, POLAR/SensePOLAR) operates on static or layer-averaged word embeddings, and even the closest prior work using attention heads (Wu et al., 2026, "Scout") targets a small predefined set of stereotypical axes for output control rather than systematically discovering axes from a large candidate pool. Building the axis pool directly from WordNet's 1,999 antonym-synset pairs, rather than a curated social-psychology dictionary, is a meaningful scope expansion.

**Rigor**: The paper is commendably careful about statistical validity — it runs an explicit bootstrap power analysis (Appendix C) to check whether n=50 concept sets give adequate power for the KS test, and reports a 4-way ablation (template type, template count, token position, ensemble size k) rather than presenting the final pipeline as self-evidently correct. However, the headline position-prediction accuracies (0.55-0.63) are only modestly above chance for what is effectively a binary classification task, and this is not really foregrounded as a limitation — a reader could come away over-crediting "alignment with humans" when the absolute numbers are fairly weak.

**Paper-specific weak points**: (1) One of the paper's own showcase "novel discoveries," humble vs. proud, is reported in Table 1 with a Mistral KS statistic of 0.16 that the authors themselves mark as not significant — so the claim that this axis is "independently confirmed" really rests on one model (Llama) plus human annotators, not on convergent LLM evidence, which slightly overstates the abstract's framing that both LLMs' new axes are confirmed. (2) Human ground truth is thin: after quality-control filtering, only 5 of the original 11 volunteer annotators remained, and inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss' κ = 0.375) is only in the "fair" range — meaning part of the 41-45% disagreement between STEREODISCO and "human" judgment could reflect noisy human labels rather than model error, a caveat the paper acknowledges but doesn't fully quantify. (3) Only two models are tested, both instruction-tuned 7-8B decoder-only LLMs (Llama-3-8B and Mistral-7B) — whether the "LLMs agree with each other more than with humans" finding holds at larger scale or across model families with different training recipes (e.g., RLHF strength, safety tuning) is explicitly left open. (4) No comparison against a word-embedding-based baseline (WEAT/POLAR) on the same axes is shown, so it's not established that internal-activation probing surfaces stereotypes that word-embedding methods would miss — the improvement over prior paradigms is argued conceptually, not benchmarked empirically.

**Reproducibility**: Code and data are stated to be supplementary material "and will be made publicly available with the camera-ready version" — i.e., not yet available at the time of this preprint, so independent verification of the pipeline is currently not possible.

**Significance**: The practical takeaway — that stereotype-mitigation techniques targeting only a fixed, theory-driven axis list will miss content like retarded/precocious or narrow-minded/broad-minded — is a useful and concrete contribution to LLM auditing, even though the underlying accuracy numbers suggest the method's signal, while statistically real, is not overwhelmingly strong.

## Strengths

- Clear four-component decomposition makes the framework's design choices (and their ablations) transparent and independently swappable.
- Extends stereotype discovery far beyond the small, theory-driven axis sets used in nearly all prior LLM stereotype work, using WordNet's full antonym-synset structure (1,999 pairs).
- Careful statistical apparatus: KS test with explicit significance threshold plus a dedicated bootstrap power analysis, rather than an ad hoc distance metric.
- Localizes stereotype encoding to specific layer-head pairs, which the authors note makes the recovered axes directly usable for activation steering without additional prompting or fine-tuning.
- Human validation effort is methodologically thoughtful: comparative (group vs. axis) rating design rather than abstract stereotypicality judgments, stratified sampling across three domain strata, and quality-control filtering via unambiguous control items.

## Limitations

- Case study restricted to social-group stereotypes because that is the only domain with widely accepted ground truth (from decades of psychological surveys); the authors explicitly flag that applying STEREODISCO to brands, food items, or individuals would require comparable ground truth that does not yet exist.
- Semantic axes must be "meaningful" for the concept under study (e.g., geometry or chemistry-derived antonym pairs like symmetric/asymmetric are not applicable to humans); this restriction is only partially controlled by stratified sampling and otherwise relies on analyst judgment.
- English-only, WordNet-dependent; extending to other languages needs both translated templates and a multilingual lexical resource (ConceptNet or BabelNet) to replace WordNet.
- No ground truth exists for what stereotypes an LLM "should" encode, so the human-comparison results are a proxy, not a direct validity check of STEREODISCO itself — divergences between model and human labels could reflect genuine model-human differences rather than framework failure.
- Restricted to two ~7-8B instruction-tuned models; generalization to larger models, base (non-instruct) models, or substantially different training objectives is untested and flagged as future work.
- Human annotation pool is small (5 annotators post-filtering) with only "fair" agreement (Fleiss' κ = 0.375), limiting the precision of the human baseline itself.

## Relevance

该框架把可解释性探针技术（layer-head probing、mass-mean probing）和刻板印象研究结合，为审计 LLM 内部表征中的社会偏见提供了一套可复用、可扩展到任意概念族（不限于社会群体）的统计检验流程，对关注 LLM 安全对齐、偏见检测与激活层可解释性的研究方向具有参考价值。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- Would the "LLMs agree with each other more than with humans" finding hold for much larger models (e.g., 70B+) or for base (non-instruction-tuned) models, where RLHF-driven alignment pressure on stereotype content is weaker or absent?
- How would STEREODISCO's projections compare quantitatively, axis-by-axis, against a word-embedding baseline (WEAT/POLAR) on the same 76-axis human-annotated set — does probing internal activations actually surface stereotypes invisible to embedding-based methods, or largely recover the same signal?
- Since the recovered geometric axes are tied to specific layer-head pairs, does steering along a "novel" axis like narrow-minded/broad-minded produce coherent, controllable output shifts, or do the axes found via mass-mean probing degrade under intervention (unlike axes validated with logistic-regression probes)?
- Given the only "fair" human inter-annotator agreement (κ=0.375), would a larger or more expert annotator pool change which axes are classified as stereotypical, and how sensitive is the 57-59% STEREODISCO-human agreement rate to that noise floor?
- Can the framework be extended past adjective antonym pairs (e.g., to verb-based or noun-based semantic axes) to capture stereotype dimensions that adjectives alone cannot express?
