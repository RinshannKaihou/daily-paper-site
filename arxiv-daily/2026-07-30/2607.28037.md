> **一句话总结：** ClawTrack 是一个覆盖 320 个真实任务（8 大领域）的双评估基准，用 12,541 条任务专属细粒度规则同时打出"结果分"和逐轮"过程分"（目标一致性/效率/信息利用/结果验证四维度），发现 Claude-Opus-4.7 单次通过率最高（Pass@3=76.4%）但 Claude-Opus-4.8 三次全过率更高（Pass³=51.1% vs 46.7%）、过程分与结果显著相关（Pearson r=0.466），并用过程分做数据筛选，在 Qwen3 三种规模上把 SFT 后的 Pass³ 相比随机采样基线提升 +10 到 +19 个百分点。

> **TL;DR:** ClawTrack introduces a dual-assessment benchmark (320 real-world agent tasks across 8 domains, graded by 12,541 task-specific rubric items along 4 process dimensions) that decouples *what* an agent achieves (Task Score) from *how* it achieves it (Process Score); evaluating 21 models over 16,000+ trials shows Claude-Opus-4.7 leads Pass@3 (76.4%) while Claude-Opus-4.8 has higher deployment consistency (Pass³=51.1% vs. 46.7%), and applying the Process Grader as a trajectory filter for SFT data curation yields +10 to +19 Pass³ points over random sampling on Qwen3-14B/30B-A3B/80B-A3B relative to a no-filtering/random-5k baseline — demonstrating that process-aware evaluation is both a diagnostic tool and a practical data-curation lever.

## 核心贡献

- **双评估框架**：把智能体评测拆成"结果分"（Task Score：完成度、安全性、工具容错的加权组合）和逐轮"过程分"（Process Score：目标一致性、效率、信息利用、结果验证四维加权），二者互为补充而非彼此推导，解决了"运气好蒙对答案"和"能力不足却无法定位具体缺陷"两个纯结果评测的盲区。
- **320 任务 × 12,541 条规则**：8 个领域（教育、金融、法律、办公、DevOps、零售、旅行、多模态媒体）各 40 个任务，一半改编自 Claw-Eval、WildClawBench、PinchBench、GAIA 等已有基准，一半为新采集的真实工作流场景；每个任务约 39.2 条细粒度规则锚点，由半自动化流水线（40 个专家种子任务人工撰写规则 → 强LLM蒸馏成可复用的规则生成"技能" → 自动一致性检查+人工复核）生成，人机评分一致性达 Pearson r=0.912。
- **实证发现**：(1) 过程分与任务结果显著相关（r=0.466, Cohen's d=0.945）但保持独立诊断价值；(2) 四个过程维度中度正交（r=0.49–0.67），结果验证是普遍的系统性瓶颈；(3) 用 4 个不同裁判 LLM 重新打分，任务级相关性均值达 0.81，证明评分由规则锚点驱动而非裁判偏好；(4) 峰值能力与部署一致性会背离——Claude-Opus-4.7 单次通过率最高但三次全过率反而低于 Claude-Opus-4.8。
- **过程感知的数据筛选**：将 Process Grader 用作后训练数据筛选器，从约 2 万条 ToolBench/τ-bench/WildClawBench 轨迹中，先保留结果正确的约 7,200 条，再按过程分（≥0.65）取 Top 5,000 条做 SFT；在 Qwen3-14B/30B-A3B-Thinking/Next-80B-A3B-Thinking 三种规模上，相比随机采样同等数量的"结果正确"轨迹，Pass³ 分别提升 +10、+16、+19 个百分点，且提升幅度随模型规模增大而增大。

## Key Contributions

- **Dual-assessment design**: ClawTrack simultaneously scores *what* an agent achieves (Task Score, combining a deterministic/LLM-judged completion score, a binary safety flag, and a robustness-to-tool-error score) and *how* it achieves it (Process Score, a per-turn rubric-anchored score along four dimensions), explicitly designed so process quality is an independent construct rather than derived from outcome — addressing the "lucky pass" and "unattributable failure" blind spots of outcome-only agent benchmarks (Table 1 compares 15+ prior benchmarks, none of which combine full outcome, process, safety, multi-trial, and reproducible-environment support).
- **320-task, 12,541-rubric-item benchmark suite**: Tasks span 8 domains (Education, Finance, Legal, Office, DevOps, Retail, Travel, Media), instantiated in Docker-based workspaces with 25+ deterministic mock services; half of the 320 tasks are adapted from existing benchmarks (Claw-Eval, WildClawBench, PinchBench, GAIA) and half are newly authored. Rubrics are produced via a semi-automated pipeline: 5 human experts author seed rubrics for 40 tasks (Cohen's κ=0.874 inter-annotator agreement), which are distilled by Claude-Opus-4.8 into a reusable rubric-generation skill applied to the remaining 280 tasks, validated against human judgments (Pearson r=0.912, Cohen's κ=0.851 on 200 sampled score pairs).
- **Empirical findings across 21 models, 16,000+ trials**: (1) process scores correlate with but remain independent of task success (r=0.466, d=0.945); (2) the four process dimensions are moderately orthogonal (r=0.49–0.67), with result verification as the most independent axis and systematic bottleneck (Figure 6, Figure 10); (3) the framework is robust to evaluator choice — four different judge LLMs (Claude-Opus-4.8, Gemini-3.1-Pro, Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-5.5) produce mean pairwise task-level r=0.81; (4) peak single-trial capability and multi-trial reliability diverge across models.
- **Process-based trajectory filtering for post-training**: The rubric-anchored Process Grader is repurposed as a data-curation filter — retaining trajectories that are both outcome-correct *and* well-reasoned (process score ≥ 0.65) — and shown to yield consistent SFT gains over random sampling of outcome-correct trajectories across three Qwen3 model scales.

## Methodology

ClawTrack is organized into four layers (Figure 3):

1. **Task Layer.** Each of the 320 tasks bundles a natural-language instruction, optional input artifacts (documents/images/videos/spreadsheets), a reference solution, and task-specific rubrics. Rubrics are structured along four process dimensions (goal alignment, efficiency, information utilization, result verification), each with five scoring tiers and behavioral anchors grounded in observable agent actions (~39.2 rubric items per task, 12,541 total).

2. **Harness Layer.** A Docker-based workspace hosts 25+ deterministic mock services (calendar, email, e-commerce, medical records, code execution, etc.) exposing real API surfaces over frozen synthetic data, ensuring identical initial state across trials. Agents interact via an iterative ReAct-style loop (thought → action → observation) until task completion or a step budget is reached.

3. **Evaluation Layer.** Two independent graders operate on complementary evidence: the **Outcome Grader** compares generated artifacts against the reference solution and inspects audit logs/environment snapshots for task completion, safety compliance, and tool-error robustness, producing the Task Score $s_{task} = s_{safe}\cdot(\alpha\cdot s_{comp} + \beta\cdot s_{rob})$ with safety as a hard multiplicative gate (α=0.80, β=0.20 weighting completion vs. robustness). The **Process Grader** scores each turn along the four rubric dimensions (weighted: efficiency and information utilization at 0.40 each, result verification at 0.20, with goal alignment acting as a multiplicative gate — an off-goal turn scores zero regardless of other dimensions), averaged uniformly (not position-weighted) across all turns into a trajectory-level Process Score.

4. **Reporting Layer.** Each task is run for k=3 independent trials under a dual-threshold pass criterion ($s_{task}\geq 0.75$ AND $s_{proc}\geq 0.60$), yielding Pass@k (peak capability, any trial passes) and Pass^k (deployment consistency, all trials pass).

**Rubric generation** bootstraps from 40 human-expert-authored seed-task rubrics (5 per domain), distilled by a strong LLM (Claude-Opus-4.8) into a reusable rubric-generation "skill" that, given a new task specification, produces a full four-dimension rubric for the remaining 280 tasks, with automatic consistency checks and human review.

**Trajectory filtering for post-training** collects ~20,415 trajectories from ToolBench (8,247), τ-bench (3,156), and WildClawBench (9,012) using Claude-Opus-4.7, GPT-5.5, and GLM-5.2. Trajectories are retained if outcome-correct per the source-benchmark oracle AND average process score ≥ 0.65 (~7,200 pass this filter), from which the top 5,000 by process score are selected for full-parameter SFT (LLaMA-Factory, lr=1e-5, 3 epochs, effective batch size 32) on Qwen3-14B, Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking, and Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Thinking, compared against a "Random-5k" baseline (uniformly sampled from the same outcome-correct pool) and a "Base" (no SFT) control.

## Results & Highlights

- **Non-multimodal main results (Table 3, 229 tasks, 20 models):** Claude-Opus-4.7 leads Pass@3 at **76.4%** (Task Score 0.847, Avg Proc 0.660), but Claude-Opus-4.8 achieves higher Pass³ at **51.1% vs. 46.7%** for Claude-Opus-4.7, and higher Avg Process Score (0.670 vs. 0.660) — a direct empirical demonstration that peak single-trial capability and multi-trial deployment consistency diverge. GPT-5.5 (Pass@3=68.1%) and GLM-5.2 (Pass@3=68.1%, tied) differ sharply in consistency: 44.5% vs. 41.5% Pass³.
- **Multimodal subset (Table 4, 91 tasks, 11 models):** GPT-5.5 leads all metrics with Pass@3=**53.3%**, Pass³=17.8%, Avg Proc=0.617 — substantially higher than the best non-GPT baseline, Claude-Opus-4.8, at Pass@3=**46.2%** (Pass³=16.5%). Notably, Claude-Opus-4.7, the top non-multimodal model, drops to rank 8 on multimodal tasks (Pass@3=25.6%), showing text-centric strengths do not transfer to vision-intensive workflows.
- **Process-outcome correlation:** Process scores correlate with task success at Pearson **r=0.466** (Cohen's **d=0.945**) across N=13,641 turn-level trials — a moderate, non-trivial correlation that the paper argues justifies treating process as a genuinely separate signal rather than a redundant one.
- **Trajectory filtering for post-training (Table 5, 229 tasks × 3 trials):** On Qwen3-14B, Filtering-5k reaches Pass@3=44%/Pass³=12% vs. Random-5k baseline Pass@3=34%/Pass³=6% (a **+10 point Pass@3, +6 point Pass³** gain) and vs. Base (no SFT) Pass@3=26%/Pass³=2. On Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking, Filtering-5k reaches Pass@3=73%/Pass³=21% vs. Random-5k Pass@3=57%/Pass³=16% (**+16 points**). On Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Thinking, Filtering-5k reaches Pass@3=86%/Pass³=30% vs. Random-5k Pass@3=67%/Pass³=22% (**+19 points**), with gains scaling up with model capacity.
- **Inter-judge robustness:** Re-grading identical trajectories with four different judge LLMs (Claude-Opus-4.8, Gemini-3.1-Pro, Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-5.5) gives mean pairwise task-level Pearson r=0.81 (same-family judges, e.g. Gemini-3.1 vs. 2.5, agree most at r=0.871), and human-vs-LLM-judge agreement on 200 sampled score pairs reaches Pearson r=0.912, Cohen's κ=0.851.
- **Domain difficulty hierarchy:** Legal (47.5%) and Media (48.7%) are the hardest domains even for the best models (Table 9), while DevOps and Retail are comparatively easy (top models exceed 80% Pass@3).

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty**: The core idea — decoupling outcome from process scoring via trajectory-level, task-specific rubrics rather than binary checkpoints — is a genuine and well-motivated extension over prior process-aware work (TheAgentCompany's sub-task checkpoints, UniClawBench's hidden step-completion supervisor, OpenClawBench's outcome-process mismatch analysis), none of which quantify *how well* an agent reasons at each step (Table 1's comparison is honest about this gap). The four-dimension rubric taxonomy (goal alignment, efficiency, information utilization, result verification) is a reasonable but not deeply justified choice — the paper states it is "grounded in prior work" on tool-using agents without a systematic ablation of alternative dimension sets.

**Rigor**: The empirical validation of the rubric-based grader itself is genuinely strong — 3 independent human annotators, a calibration round, 200 human-graded score pairs achieving Pearson r=0.912/Cohen's κ=0.851 against the LLM judge, and a 4-judge cross-validation (mean r=0.81). The case study (Appendix I, task EDU_002) concretely shows the Process Grader catching a mid-trajectory "detour" (score drops to 0.066 at turn 3) invisible to outcome-only grading, which is a compelling qualitative demonstration.

**Reproducibility**: A specific, paper-acknowledged weakness (Appendix A, Limitation 1): "ClawTrack relies on deterministic mock services rather than live APIs... frozen snapshots may not capture the full variability of real-world services" — this is an honest limitation for a benchmark claiming to measure "real-world autonomous agents." Limitation 4 is similarly candid: the rubric-generation pipeline bootstraps from only 40 expert-authored seed tasks (5 per domain), so "domains or task types poorly represented in the seed set may receive lower-quality generated rubrics" — a concrete, paper-specific quality-control gap rather than a generic caveat. Also, the default Process Grader judge is a single LLM (Claude-Opus-4.8); although 4-judge agreement is reported as evidence of robustness, the main leaderboard and all headline numbers (Tables 3–4) use only this one judge, so any systematic bias in that judge would silently propagate through the entire ranking (Limitation 2, self-acknowledged).

**Significance / red flags**: Half of the 320 tasks are adapted (not originally authored) from four existing benchmarks (Claw-Eval, WildClawBench, PinchBench, GAIA), which limits the claim of a fully novel task suite, though the process-rubric annotation layer on top of them is new. The paper does not report a direct quantitative comparison of its rubric-anchored Process Grader against the simpler binary/checkpoint-based process signals used by TheAgentCompany or UniClawBench — it argues qualitatively that fine-grained rubrics are superior but never runs both signal types on the same tasks to show the fine-grained version is more predictive. The dual-threshold hyperparameters ($\tau_{task}=0.75$, $\tau_{proc}=0.60$) and the score-weighting constants (α=0.80/β=0.20, $w_e=w_i=0.40$/$w_o=0.20$) are set by the authors without a reported sensitivity analysis, so it is unclear how much the headline Pass@3/Pass³ rankings would shift under different threshold choices.

## Strengths

- Clear, well-motivated dual-assessment design that concretely demonstrates its value: the Claude-Opus-4.7-vs-4.8 Pass@3/Pass³ divergence (76.4% vs. 51.1%) is exactly the kind of signal outcome-only evaluation would miss.
- Strong grader validation: human-LLM judge agreement (r=0.912) and 4-judge cross-validation (mean r=0.81) go well beyond what most agent benchmarks report for judge reliability.
- Practical downstream utility shown, not just claimed: process-based trajectory filtering produces consistent, scaling SFT gains (+10 to +19 Pass³ points) across three real model sizes, closing the loop from diagnostic benchmark to actual training signal.
- Transparent self-reported limitations (Appendix A) that name concrete weaknesses (mock-service determinism, single default judge, seed-rubric coverage gaps) rather than glossing over them.

## Limitations

- Deterministic, frozen mock services rather than live APIs — acknowledged by the authors as potentially missing real-world service variability.
- Headline results rely on a single default judge LLM (Claude-Opus-4.8); the 4-judge robustness check is a secondary analysis, not the basis for the main leaderboard.
- Rubric generation is bootstrapped from only 40 seed tasks; under-represented domains/task types risk lower-quality auto-generated rubrics.
- The four process dimensions, while empirically shown to be moderately orthogonal, are fixed by design and explicitly do not capture other potentially relevant qualities (e.g., creativity, communication style), per the authors' own broader-impacts discussion.
- No ablation isolating the benefit of fine-grained (5-tier, task-specific) rubrics over the coarser binary/checkpoint process signals used by prior benchmarks, so the magnitude of improvement from "fine-grained" specifically (versus "process-aware at all") is not directly quantified.
- Benchmark task/environment release status is unclear beyond a leaderboard link, which may limit independent verification of the 320-task suite.

## Relevance

对关注 LLM Agent 评测方法论、过程奖励建模或后训练数据筛选的研究者，ClawTrack 提供了一套可复用的细粒度过程评分框架和实证证明——过程分能够独立于结果分捕捉推理质量差异，并可直接用作 SFT 数据筛选信号带来可衡量的性能提升，这与"如何用过程信号改进 Agent 训练"这一方向高度相关。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- How would ClawTrack's rankings change if the four process dimensions were re-weighted or if a fifth dimension (e.g., communication clarity, safety-specific reasoning) were added — is the current taxonomy actually complete, or just the set the authors happened to distill from 40 seed tasks?
- What happens if the Process Grader judge itself is swapped as the *production* default (not just for a robustness check) — would Pass@3/Pass³ rankings across the 21 evaluated models meaningfully reorder under, say, GPT-5.5 as default judge instead of Claude-Opus-4.8?
- Does process-based trajectory filtering still yield gains if the training and evaluation domains do not overlap (e.g., filtering trajectories from ToolBench/τ-bench/WildClawBench but evaluating generalization on entirely unseen agent tasks outside ClawTrack's 320)?
- How sensitive are the dual-threshold pass/fail criterion ($\tau_{task}=0.75$, $\tau_{proc}=0.60$) and the Task/Process Score weighting constants (α, β, $w_e$, $w_i$, $w_o$) to different settings — would a sensitivity sweep change which models are ranked "most reliable"?
- Since mock services are frozen and deterministic, how well would ClawTrack's process-quality rankings transfer to agents operating against live, non-deterministic real-world APIs with genuine latency, rate limits, and service failures?
