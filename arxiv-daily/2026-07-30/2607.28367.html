CUA Benchmark Audit · Evaluator Reliability

15.3% of FAIL verdicts across five CUA benchmarks are wrong, and real failures are mostly verification blindness, not clicking errors.

How Benchmarks Mis-Score Computer-Use Agents
Zihan Dong, Zhiyuan Ma, Zekun Wang, Yunqing Li, et al. 2026-07-30 arXiv:2607.28367
Computer-Use Agents Benchmark Evaluation Evaluator Reliability Failure Taxonomy LLM-as-Judge
Key Metric
15.3%
Of 150 audited FAIL/zero-reward trajectories, this share is wrong (95% Wilson CI [10.4, 22.0]) — 10.7% evaluator false negatives, 4.7% broken tasks. Per-benchmark rates range from 0.0% (WorkArena) to 21.7% (WebArena, AssistantBench).
Why This Matters

Anyone citing OSWorld, WebArena, or AssistantBench leaderboard numbers is trusting evaluators that misjudge outcomes in roughly one of every seven FAILs.

Critical Edge

The tier breakdown behind the paper's headline claim — 39.3% verification failures vs. 13.9% execution failures — rests on a diagnostic taxonomy with only moderate LLM–LLM agreement (κ=0.41).

Methodology

Reframes a CUA benchmark score as the output of a four-stage measurement pipeline, not a direct read on agent capability:

task construction→execution environment→scoring→reporting

The audit samples 150 publicly released FAIL/zero-reward trajectories (deterministic seed 20260717) across OSWorld-Verified and AgentRewardBench's WebArena, VisualWebArena, WorkArena, and AssistantBench subsets — no new agent rollouts were run.

  • Stage 1: label each FAIL as genuine failure, evaluator false negative, broken task, or unclear.
  • Stage 2: for genuine failures only, assign the earliest decisive failure to a three-tier codebook adapted from MAST (multi-agent → single-agent CUA) — Tier 1 planning/specification, Tier 2 execution/grounding, Tier 3 verification/feedback.

Two vision-enabled LLM judges (GPT-5.5 via Codex CLI, Claude Sonnet) label all 150 trajectories blind to each other; two human groups blind-review the 74 disagreement rows plus a 30-row agreement sample via screenshot replay.

Strengths vs. Limitations

Strengths

  • Statistically transparent audit: deterministic stratified sampling, two LLM judges plus two human groups, Wilson CIs, and an explicit "earliest decisive failure" boundary rule.
  • A falsifiable, actionable finding — screenshot removal flips 12–13 verdicts and nearly halves detected false negatives — that benchmark designers can act on directly.
  • Unusually candid self-reported limitations (small per-benchmark n, no PASS-side audit, unvalidated diagnostic-probe proposal) instead of overclaiming.
  • Stage-mapped design guidelines (Table 3) translate audit findings into checklist recommendations for benchmark authors.

Limitations

  • Per-benchmark samples are small (23–57 trajectories), so per-benchmark rates carry wide CIs and shouldn't be read as precise rankings.
  • Only reviews trajectories already scored FAIL — cannot detect false positives (reward hacking), leaving that failure mode entirely unmeasured.
  • The Stage 2 three-tier taxonomy — source of the 39.3% vs. 13.9% headline split — has only moderate inter-rater reliability (LLM–LLM κ=0.41, four-rater Fleiss' κ=0.36).
  • The benchmark-landscape literature survey is a non-systematic "scoping review" without PRISMA-style counts.
  • The proposed automatic no-op detection probe is conceptual only, never validated against the paper's own human-labeled data.

Questions for Further Exploration

  1. What would the complementary audit of PASS-scored trajectories reveal about evaluator false positives (reward hacking), and how would that shift the net wrong-verdict rate?
  2. Would validating the proposed no-op/feedback-blind detection probe against the paper's own 150 human-labeled trajectories confirm its zero-cost value, or misfire on legitimate no-change intervals (e.g. async page loads)?
  3. How does the 15.3% wrong-verdict rate and Tier 3 dominance change on newer long-horizon benchmarks the paper discusses but doesn't audit (OSWorld 2.0, Agents' Last Exam, WeaveBench)?
  4. Would a stronger or larger panel of LLM judges raise the only-moderate κ=0.41 Stage 2 diagnostic agreement enough to trust the tier-share breakdown as a design signal?
  5. Can the stage-specific design guidelines (Table 3) be operationalized into an automated "benchmark reliability score" computed before trusting a new CUA leaderboard?
