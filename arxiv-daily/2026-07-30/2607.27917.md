> **一句话总结：** 论文提出 MLS-Neurons（模态-语言共享安全神经元），通过以英语为语义锚点，在多语言、多模态探针数据上定位仅占约 0.03% 参数的共享安全神经元，仅用英文安全数据微调这部分神经元，就能将 Qwen3-VL/Llama-3.2-Vision/Gemma-3 在跨语言跨模态复合攻击下的攻击成功率（ASR）大幅降低（如 Gemma 上图像风险 ASR 从 10.58 降到 6.09，文本风险从 10.55 降到 5.73），同时几乎不损失通用能力（MMMU/MGSM 变化 |Δ|≤1）。

> **TL;DR:** The paper identifies "MLS-Neurons" — a small set of FFN neurons (~0.03% of parameters) that are jointly responsive to visual and textual safety risks *and* shared across languages via an English semantic anchor — and shows that fine-tuning only these neurons on English-only safety data transfers safety supervision to unseen languages and modalities. On Lingua-SafetyBench across Qwen3-VL-8B, Llama-3.2-11B-Vision, and Gemma-3-4B, the method cuts average in-distribution ASR versus the strongest multilingual baseline MLC from 24.01/14.65 to 5.89/2.80 (IR/TR) on Llama and from 10.58/10.55 to 6.09/5.73 on Gemma, while masking these same neurons alone pushes ASR from 30.71/19.57 up to 50.44/50.98 — direct causal evidence that the identified neurons matter — with only |Δ|≤1 point change on MMMU/MGSM.

## 核心贡献

- 提出神经元级别的跨维度安全对齐框架：先在每种语言内识别对图像风险和文本风险都敏感的"模态共享安全神经元"（MS-Neurons），再以英语为语义锚点，取其与各语言 MS-Neurons 的交集之并集，得到跨语言、跨模态共享的 MLS-Neurons。
- 设计基于激活强度与下投影权重范数联合加权的神经元显著性评分（saliency score），比单纯用激活强度更能反映神经元对输出的实际影响。
- 提出"MLS-定向安全微调"：仅用纯英文安全数据、通过带二值掩码的低秩更新（masked LoRA-style）只调整约 0.03% 的参数，即可将安全监督迁移到未见语言和未见的图文攻击组合。
- 提供理论分析（第 C 节），证明 MLS-Neurons 相比随机稀疏子集能捕获更多安全相关梯度质量，且对通用能力的干扰受限于神经元筛选质量。
- 在 3 个 LVLM 骨干（Qwen3-VL-8B、Llama-3.2-11B-Vision、Gemma-3-4B）、10 种语言（6 种域内 + 4 种未见语言）、多个跨基准（FigStep、SPA-VL、MultiJail）上做了大规模消融和跨判据（Qwen-Guard/Llama-Guard/人工评估）验证。

## Key Contributions

- A neuron-level cross-dimensional safety alignment framework: within each language, neurons jointly salient under image-dominant and text-dominant risk probes are extracted as modality-shared MS-Neurons; using English as a semantic anchor, the union of (MS-Neurons ∩ English MS-Neurons) across languages defines MLS-Neurons — a compact, cross-lingual and cross-modal safety anchor.
- A saliency score that weights activation strength by the down-projection vector norm, better reflecting a neuron's actual downstream effect than raw activation magnitude alone.
- An "MLS-targeted safety tuning" method that updates only ~0.03% of parameters using English-only safety data via a masked low-rank parameterization, transferring safety supervision to unseen languages and unseen multimodal attack combinations without needing scarce multilingual/multimodal safety data.
- Formal theoretical analysis (Appendix C) showing MLS-Neurons concentrate more safety-gradient mass than random sparse subsets and that general-capability interference is bounded by the neuron-filtering quality.
- Extensive empirical validation across 3 LVLM backbones, 10 languages (6 in-distribution + 4 OOD), and cross-benchmark OOD jailbreak sets (FigStep, SPA-VL, MultiJail), cross-checked with two different LLM judges (Qwen-Guard, Llama-Guard) plus human evaluation.

## Methodology

The pipeline (Figure 2) has four stages:

1. **Input & neuron probing.** For each language λ, three probe sets are built from Lingua-SafetyBench: image-dominant risk (unsafe image + safe text), text-dominant risk (safe image + unsafe text), and benign samples. For every FFN neuron, an activation score α is computed as the expected activation magnitude over a probe set, and a saliency score ψ is defined by weighting α by the neuron's down-projection vector norm (Eq. 3), which better captures downstream impact than activation alone. Top-K (K ratio = 0.1) neurons per layer under each risk probe, minus the neurons also salient on benign inputs, give per-language, per-modality candidate safety neurons: IS-Neurons (image) and TS-Neurons (text).
2. **Cross-lingual and cross-modal neuron extraction.** Within each language λ, MS-Neurons = IS-Neurons ∩ TS-Neurons (modality-shared). MLS-Neurons are then defined as the union, over all non-English languages λ, of (MS-Neurons_λ ∩ MS-Neurons_English) — i.e., English MS-Neurons serve as the semantic anchor that "certifies" which modality-shared neurons in other languages are also safety-relevant cross-lingually.
3. **MLS-targeted safety tuning.** Only the identified MLS-Neurons are updated, using English safety data alone (D_en), by standard autoregressive negative log-likelihood loss on safe responses. Gradients for all other parameters are masked to zero (Eq. 8), so training never needs any non-English or multimodal safety-labeled data.
4. **Practical parameterization.** Rather than editing raw weights directly, the update is realized as a masked low-rank adaptation: ΔW = (M_MLS ⊙ B) A, where the binary mask M_MLS zeros out rows of B corresponding to non-MLS neurons (Eq. 11–13), so gradients only flow to the MLS subspace while the base weights W₀ remain frozen — this is effectively a structurally-masked LoRA rather than raw parameter surgery, giving the practical benefits of LoRA (low compute, small footprint) while remaining semantically restricted to the identified neurons. Rank r=8, alpha=16, Top-K ratio=0.1 (chosen via sensitivity analysis in Table 9, which shows K=0.1 gives the best safety/utility trade-off: smaller K preserves capability better but under-covers safety, larger K over-covers with diminishing safety gains).

## Results & Highlights

- **In-distribution ASR (Table 2, Lingua-SafetyBench, avg over ZH/EN/FR/DE/JA/ES):** vs. the strongest multilingual baseline MLC, the method reduces IR/TR ASR from 24.01/14.65 to 5.89/2.80 on Llama, from 27.71/18.43 to 8.07/5.86 on Qwen, and from 10.58/10.55 to 6.09/5.73 on Gemma — consistently the lowest ASR of all compared baselines (XSAFETY, Self-Defense, ESCO, ASTRA, MLC) on all three backbones.
- **Causal necessity of MLS-Neurons (Table 1):** masking only the MLS-Neurons (no retraining, just zeroing them) pushes Gemma's IR/TR ASR from 30.71/19.57 up to 50.44 (+19.73) / 50.98 (+31.41), the largest degradation among all masking strategies (random neurons: +/-2 to +7; monolingual MS\MLS neurons: milder), while MMMU/MGSM shift by only |Δ|=0.22/0.92 — direct evidence that these specific neurons are safety-specific, not general-purpose.
- **Parameter efficiency vs. Full-/LoRA-/Random-Train (Table 3):** updating only 0.03% of parameters, the method beats 100%-parameter Full-Train and layer-matched LoRA-Train on all three backbones, e.g. on Gemma: Ours IR/TR = 4.92/5.63 vs. Full-Train 8.06/6.91, LoRA-Train 11.39/10.66, and Random-Train (same 0.03% budget, random neurons) 17.74/14.53 — showing gains come from *which* neurons are updated, not merely from sparse updating.
- **OOD language generalization (Table 5):** on 4 unseen languages (AR, FI, NO, RU), vs. MLC the method reduces IR/TR ASR from 5.36/8.89 to 3.17/5.47 on Gemma, from 14.39/9.67 to 5.46/3.03 on Llama, and from 24.11/14.55 to 12.99/8.50 on Qwen — safety transfers to languages never used in probing or training.
- **Cross-benchmark OOD generalization (Table 6, Qwen):** ASR on FigStep drops from 7.20 (original) to 0.60; on SPA-VL from 4.15 to 0.38; on the unseen low-resource language Bengali in MultiJail, ASR reaches 0.00.
- **Utility preservation (Table 4):** the method achieves the highest MMMU accuracy on Gemma (39.78) and highest MGSM accuracy on Qwen (72.86) among all tuned variants, and over-refusal on benign MM-Vet queries is comparable to Full-/LoRA-Train (e.g., Qwen: 14 refusals / 204 compliant vs. Full-Train's 15/203).
- **Judge robustness (Table 7, Table 11):** re-evaluating with an independent judge (Llama-Guard) confirms the ranking — e.g., Qwen ASR under Llama-Guard drops from MLC's 5.87/4.41 to 1.70/1.25 with the proposed method — and Qwen-Guard judgments agree with human ratings at 92.89% average accuracy across 10 languages, addressing judge-reliability concerns.

## Critical Assessment

**Novelty:** The core idea — using English as a cross-lingual *and* cross-modal semantic anchor to identify a single shared neuron subset — is a genuine and reasonably novel extension of prior neuron-level safety work, which the paper itself notes was previously either text-only-cross-lingual (Zhang et al. 2026b, "Who Transfers Safety?") or modality-specific but monolingual (SafeNeuron). The union-of-intersections construction (Eq. 6) for combining evidence across many languages is a sensible design choice explicitly justified in the appendix (Q1) against the alternative of a full multi-way intersection, which the authors argue would shrink to near-empty as more languages are added — a reasonable but untested claim, since no ablation shows how a strict multi-way intersection actually performs.

**Rigor:** The empirical validation is unusually thorough for this kind of interpretability-tuning paper: 3 backbones, ID and OOD languages, cross-benchmark OOD jailbreaks, masking-based causal intervention (Table 1), random-neuron controls (Table 3), Top-K sensitivity (Table 9), anchor-language ablation (Figure 7, though only run on Qwen with 3 alternate anchors), and cross-judge validation (Qwen-Guard vs. Llama-Guard vs. human). The theoretical section provides sub-Gaussian concentration bounds for neuron identification, though these rely on idealized assumptions (population margins, sub-Gaussian noise) that are not directly verified against the actual empirical neuron distributions.

**Reproducibility:** Hyperparameters are fully specified (Table 8: rank=8, alpha=16, Top-K=0.1, LR per model, 3 epochs). However, the paper does not mention releasing code, probing data, or trained neuron masks — for a method whose entire contribution is a specific procedure for identifying a small neuron subset, the absence of a code/artifact release statement is a notable gap for reproducibility.

**Significance / red flags:** Some baselines are weak or mismatched to the setting — ESCO and ASTRA are multimodal-only safety defenses with no multilingual design, and Self-Defense relies purely on prompting rather than tuning, so their poor multilingual performance is somewhat expected rather than a fair fight; the paper's own strongest and most relevant comparison is against MLC (a genuinely multilingual SOTA method), and even there the margins are large and consistent, which is more convincing. The anchor-language ablation (Figure 7) is limited to one model (Qwen) and only 4 candidate anchors (EN, ZH, FR, ES), leaving open whether English's advantage as anchor is a general property or partly an artifact of English being the best-resourced language in the specific backbones tested (all of which are English-centric LLMs under the hood). The Top-K=0.1 selection is tuned on the same benchmarks used for the main evaluation (Table 9 uses IR/TR/MGSM/MMMU/OKTest as the tuning objective itself), which raises a mild risk of overfitting the hyperparameter to the exact benchmark suite reported.

## Strengths

- Strong causal evidence (neuron-masking ablation) rather than only correlational results, directly supporting the claim that MLS-Neurons are functionally responsible for safety behavior.
- Extremely parameter-efficient (0.03%) while outperforming full fine-tuning and LoRA baselines at matched parameter budgets, addressing a real practical bottleneck (scarcity of multilingual/multimodal safety data and cost of full SFT).
- Broad, multi-axis generalization evidence: unseen languages, unseen benchmarks (FigStep, SPA-VL, MultiJail), and unseen low-resource languages (Bengali, Javanese) all show consistent gains.
- Cross-judge and human-evaluation consistency (92.89% agreement, corroborated by Llama-Guard) mitigates a common criticism of LLM-judged safety papers.

## Limitations

- The paper's own limitations section acknowledges the method has not been tested on more diverse, open-ended real-world scenarios, regional language variations, culturally grounded contexts, or more nuanced/implicit expressions of harm.
- Only three LVLM backbones (4B–11B scale) are evaluated; no evidence is given for larger-scale models or fundamentally different architectures (e.g., non-FFN-centric or MoE-based LVLMs), and the FFN-only neuron focus may not transfer to architectures where safety-relevant computation is distributed differently.
- Anchor-language robustness is only lightly explored (single model, few candidate anchors); the reliance on English as a fixed, privileged anchor could bake in English-centric notions of "safety" that may not culturally generalize, which the authors implicitly acknowledge as future work.
- No code/data release is mentioned, limiting independent reproducibility of the exact neuron sets identified.

## Relevance

该论文提供了一个在多语言、多模态 LVLM 安全对齐场景下用极少参数（0.03%）实现跨语言跨模态泛化的具体方法路径，其"神经元级共享安全锚点+仅用英文数据微调"的思路对关注参数高效对齐、可解释安全机制及低资源语言安全迁移的研究方向具有较高参考价值。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- How would a strict multi-way (all-language) intersection of MS-Neurons compare empirically to the union-of-pairwise-intersections-with-English construction actually used?
- Does the English-anchor advantage hold on LVLMs whose base LLM is not English-centric (e.g., a Chinese- or multilingual-first foundation model), or is it partly an artifact of the backbones tested?
- How sensitive are the identified MLS-Neurons to the specific probing dataset (Lingua-SafetyBench) — would a different harmful-content taxonomy yield a substantially different neuron set, and how stable is the overlap across probe sets?
- Can the same MLS-Neuron framework be extended beyond binary safe/unsafe refusal to more graded safety interventions (e.g., partial compliance, redirection) without full retraining?
- What happens at larger model scales (30B+) or with mixture-of-experts architectures, where "shared FFN neurons" may not be as cleanly defined?
