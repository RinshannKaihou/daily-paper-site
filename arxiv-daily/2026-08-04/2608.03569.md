> **一句话总结：** 作者提出以"对抗性、快速演变的真实世界领域"作为 AI 科学家能力评测框架，并在 F1 2026 赛车设计（共生成 881 条想法，匹配 40 条真实创新中的 48%）与万智牌套牌构筑（108 副套牌匹配 74% 的 Pro Tour 新卡）两个结构差异巨大的领域落地，发现前沿模型的主要瓶颈不在产生想法，而在于筛选、优先级排序与"涌现于组合上下文的价值"的识别。

> **TL;DR:** The paper proposes a benchmarking framework that evaluates AI scientist capabilities by having models generate structured artifacts (F1 2026 car innovations; Magic: The Gathering decks for the new Lorwyn Eclipsed set) from a pre-cutoff corpus, then scoring them against post-cutoff real-world expert outputs (40 real F1 innovations; 19 Pro Tour decks). Six frontier LLMs produced 881 F1 ideas recovering 19/40 (48%) of real innovations (GPT-5.2 best with 10/40 matches vs Qwen3-235B-thinking's 3/40) and 108 MTG decks recovering 14/19 (74%) PT new-set cards, with card-level choices strongly tracking Pro Tour adoption (Spearman ρ = 0.74, p = 0.0003). The headline finding is that the key gap for AI scientists is not idea generation but filtering, prioritization, and meta-alignment — generic-effect ideas are over-predicted while build-around cards requiring surrounding context are systematically missed.

## 核心贡献

- 提出一个通用的"对抗性快速演变真实世界领域"评测框架：以信息截断点之前的语料为输入，让模型前瞻性地生成结构化产物，再用截断点之后由人类专家独立产生的真实成果作为延迟揭晓的"ground truth"，从根本上规避了回顾式基准的数据污染问题。
- 在两个结构截然不同的领域做了概念验证：F1 2026 技术规则下的自由形式车辆设计创意，以及万智牌《Lorwyn Eclipsed》扩展下的离散化套牌构筑。两个语料（FIA 264 页技术规则、267 张新卡）均在所有参与模型训练截断之后才公开。
- 设计了双轴评测：结果相似度（与真实专家创新对齐）与内在质量（规则合规、引用准确、工程合理性），并配套了可解析+蒙特卡洛模拟的随机基线（超几何均值、聚合期望）。
- 系统识别了一类贯穿两个领域的一致失败模式：模型擅长产出"价值立即可见"的候选，但不擅长产出"价值依赖于周围组合上下文才涌现"的候选；在 F1 中表现为 INTERFACE COUPLING 类型规则漏洞被频繁标记（28.8%）但匹配率最低（5.0%），在 MTG 中表现为通用强力卡被高估（Spell Snare 出现在 33/108 生成套牌但仅 9/19 PT 套牌）而 build-around 卡被系统性忽略（Formidable Speaker 出现在 7/19 PT 套牌但 0 生成套牌）。
- 框架可移植、可重现：两个领域均开放了代码、数据、提示词与生成产物（F1: github.com/WilliamBolton/f1-ai-scientist-paper；MTG: github.com/WilliamBolton/mtg-AI-scientist-paper）。

## Key Contributions

- A general framework that recasts AI scientist evaluation as a prospective, time-delayed generation task: models see only a pre-cutoff corpus, generate structured candidate artifacts, and are scored against independently produced post-cutoff expert outputs — eliminating the data-contamination risk that plagues retrospective benchmarks such as DiscoveryBench, PaperBench, and ScienceAgentBench.
- A proof-of-concept instantiation in two structurally distinct adversarial domains — free-form F1 2026 car-design ideation (continuous design space) and MTG Lorwyn Eclipsed deck construction (discrete card-selection task) — chosen so that both input corpora post-date every participating model's training cutoff.
- A two-axis evaluation design: outcome similarity to real expert artifacts plus intrinsic quality (engineering plausibility, citation accuracy, rule compliance), with calibrated hypergeometric and Monte-Carlo random baselines to distinguish signal from chance.
- Identification of a cross-domain failure mode: models produce candidates with immediately obvious value far more readily than candidates whose value emerges only through composition with surrounding context (e.g., the most-flagged F1 loophole type INTERFACE COUPLING had the lowest match rate at 5.0%, while the less frequent BROAD FREEDOM had the highest at 7.7%; build-around MTG cards were systematically missed).
- Public, reproducible artifacts: prompts, corpora, generated outputs, and full pipelines for both domains are released on GitHub.

## Methodology

**Framework.** For each domain the authors fix an information cutoff *t* and build an input corpus containing only material available before that point (FIA 2026 Section C Technical Regulations PDF, dated 10 Dec 2025, for F1; the 267-card Lorwyn Eclipsed set, released 23 Jan 2026, for MTG). The model generates structured candidate artifacts, which are then evaluated against post-cutoff artifacts produced independently by human experts (40 curated real F1 2026 pre-season innovations; 19 Pro Tour Lorwyn Eclipsed decklists). A domain is admissible if it satisfies four criteria: (1) constrained but non-trivial design space, (2) adversarial (genuine expert innovation under competition), (3) publicly observable downstream expert artifacts, and (4) a clear information cutoff.

**Models.** Six frontier LLMs across three providers: GPT-5.2 (Aug 2025 cutoff), o3 (Jun 2024 cutoff), Gemini 3 Flash and Gemini 3.1 Pro (both Jan 2025 cutoff), Qwen3 235B and Qwen3 235B-thinking (both Jun 2025 cutoff) — all accessed via OpenAI API or OpenRouter, and every cutoff predates both corpora.

**F1 pipeline.** Three-agent pipeline: agent 1 indexes the 264-page regulation PDF, agent 2 maps regulatory loopholes across article sections (tagged with one of five categories: INTERFACE COUPLING, DEFINITION EDGE CASE, BROAD FREEDOM, NOTPROHIBITED, EXCEPTION ZONE), agent 3 proposes technical innovations. Each model runs three "general" (full-car) runs plus one run per car area (component-focused), yielding 881 ideas total. Three quality judges score each idea on citation accuracy (PASS/PARTIAL/FAIL), rule compliance (PASS/GREY/FAIL), and engineering plausibility (binary). An LLM shortlist filter then surfaces candidate pairs, which a single non-expert human reviewer adjudicates as MATCH / PARTIAL / NOMATCH against the 40 real innovations.

**MTG pipeline.** Three-agent pipeline producing three decks per run under different strategic angles, with three runs per model per configuration × six models × two configurations (one-shot vs tool-use) = 108 decks. Tool-use shares agents 1 and 2 with one-shot, but agent 3 builds each deck via an add-card / remove-card / finish-deck loop. Ground truth = 19 PT decks (Top 15 Day-2 finishers + 4 featured innovative decks), canonicalized against Scryfall.

**Random baselines.** Per-pair hypergeometric expectation E[overlap] = kK/N over the N=261 non-land new-set card pool, plus an aggregate expectation E_agg = T·(1 − Π(1 − k_i/N)) across the nine decks per configuration, validated by 10,000 Monte-Carlo resamples.

**Statistical tests.** Fisher's exact test for F1 quality-vs-match associations (odds ratios with 95% CIs); paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test for one-shot vs tool-use new-set recall; Friedman test for main-deck legality; Spearman's ρ and Kendall's τ for card-level correspondence between generated and PT adoption breadth. Tests are explicitly framed as exploratory (outputs clustered by model/run/config), not causal.

## Results & Highlights

- **F1 coverage (best model):** GPT-5.2 matched 10 of 40 real 2026 F1 innovations across 166 generated ideas — the highest of any model — while the weakest model, Qwen3 235B (thinking), matched only 3 (across 155 ideas). Pooled across all six models, 19 of 40 (48%) real innovations were recovered by at least one model.
- **F1 volume-normalized coverage:** GPT-5.2 also leads on matches per 100 ideas (6.0/100); idea count is not significantly associated with distinct matches across the six models (Spearman ρ = 0.33, p = 0.52, n = 6), so coverage is not just a volume artifact.
- **F1 quality-vs-match signal:** Rule-compliant or grey-zone ideas (n = 542) had 9× higher odds of matching a real innovation than non-compliant ideas (n = 339) — OR = 8.92, 95% CI [2.74, 29.08], p = 2.9×10⁻⁶; citation accuracy OR = 3.11, p = 0.007; engineering plausibility OR = 4.65, p = 0.022.
- **F1 quality pass rates (GPT-5.2):** 83% all-pass / 99% engineering / 93% citation / 88% compliance — far ahead of every other model (next best Gemini 3 Flash at 49% all-pass; Qwen3-235B-thinking last at 12%).
- **F1 loophole match rates:** INTERFACE COUPLING, the most frequently flagged loophole type (378/1311 = 28.8%), matched real innovations at only 5.0%; BROAD FREEDOM, less frequently flagged (20.7%), produced the highest match rate at 7.7%.
- **MTG best single deck:** Gemini 3 Flash (one-shot) recovered 5 of 7 new-set cards (71%) from the third-place PT Izzet Elementals deck, vs a per-pair random expectation of 0.32 cards — a 15.5× uplift.
- **MTG aggregate coverage:** 14 of 19 (74%) PT new-set cards were selected by some generated deck across 108 decks; Gemini 3 Flash was the only model whose nine-deck total exceeded the upper quartile of its random-baseline band in both configurations (8/19 one-shot, 1.34× expected; 3/19 tool-use, 2.13× expected).
- **MTG card-level correspondence:** The number of generated decks playing a new-set card correlated strongly with the number of PT decks containing it (Spearman ρ = 0.74, p = 0.0003, n = 19; Kendall τ = 0.62, p = 0.0008) — models converge on PT staples in proportion to their real meta footprint.
- **MTG tool-use hurts discovery:** Tool-use scaffolding significantly reduced new-set discovery for every model (paired Wilcoxon p = 0.031; 45/54 = 83% one-shot decks beat random vs only 15/54 = 28% tool-use decks).
- **MTG tool-use helps legality (heterogeneously):** Main-deck validity jumped from 11% to 100% for GPT-5.2 and from 0% to 89% for Qwen3 under tool-use, but the aggregate Friedman test across all six models was non-significant (p = 0.24).
- **Cross-domain failure mode:** Generic-effect MTG cards were over-predicted (Spell Snare in 33/108 generated decks vs 9/19 PT decks; Sear in 14 vs 4), while build-around cards were systematically missed (Formidable Speaker in 0 generated decks vs 7/19 PT decks) — the same pattern as F1's INTERFACE-COUPLING over-flagging.
- **Best-model idea convergence:** GPT-5.2 was the most narrowly clustered (cross-run best-match cosine µ = 0.70) while o3 explored most widely (µ = 0.62); within-model match-count standard deviation was 0.78, and five of six models had at least one zero-match run.

- Statistical analysis is unusually careful for an LLM-evaluation paper: explicit odds-ratio CIs, paired Wilcoxon tests, Spearman/Kendall with n reported, and Monte-Carlo-validated random baselines rather than hand-waved "above chance" claims. The authors openly label their tests as exploratory rather than causal given the clustered structure of model/run/config outputs.

## Critical Assessment

- **Novelty.** The framing — use adversarial fast-moving public domains with periodic reveal events (F1 regulation cycles, MTG set releases) as naturally re-instantiating, leakage-resistant benchmarks — is genuinely fresh relative to DiscoveryBench/HypoBench/PaperBench/ScienceAgentBench, all of which rely on retrospective or decomposed targets and admit data contamination. The closest prior work is forecasting (ForecastBench, Halawi et al.), which the authors cleanly differentiate: forecasting scores predefined probabilistic questions, whereas this scores open-ended ideation against an emerging expert reference set.
- **Rigor.** The evaluation design is strong: pre-cutoff corpora verified to postdate every model's training cutoff, calibrated hypergeometric + Monte-Carlo baselines, multi-axis quality rubrics, a human-adjudication step with the reviewer blinded to the LLM judge's discrete verdict, and matched statistical tests with reported effect sizes and CIs. The honest reporting of negative results (e.g., tool-use *hurting* MTG discovery; five of six models having at least one zero-match F1 run; 89% of LLM-judge-flagged F1 pairs being downgraded on human review) is commendable.
- **Reproducibility.** High. Both pipelines are fully open-sourced with prompts and outputs. Caveat: the LLM-API dependency means exact replication is bounded by provider model-version stability (GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Flash, etc.), and the authors do not pin specific API snapshot dates beyond knowledge cutoffs.
- **Significance.** Moderate-to-good as a workshop paper. The cross-domain failure-mode finding (over-emission of generically valuable candidates, systematic blindness to context-dependent value) is the most actionable contribution and translates cleanly to scientific hypothesis generation. The framework's portability claim is plausible but only demonstrated on two domains, both of which are proxies for science rather than science itself.
- **Red flags / weaknesses worth naming.**
  - **Single non-expert human reviewer.** All 519 shortlisted F1 candidate pairs were adjudicated by one author who explicitly self-describes as "not a domain expert in either Formula 1 engineering or competitive Magic." This is a non-trivial threat to the validity of the MATCH/PARTIAL/NOMATCH labels, particularly for F1 where the distinction between "same physical mechanism, similar geometry" and "same problem framing" is subtle and the reviewer admits semantic similarity can confuse superficial overlap with shared mechanism. No inter-annotator agreement, no second reviewer, no sensitivity analysis.
  - **Incomplete ground truth.** The authors own this honestly but it bears emphasis: public F1 coverage overrepresents visible aerodynamics while many decisive performance sources (power-unit internals, suspension kinematics) remain confidential, so "unmatched" model ideas are not necessarily wrong — they may simply be unobservable. Likewise MTG decklists reveal successful choices but not all viable decks.
  - **Not actually prospective.** Despite the framework supporting pre-registration, the authors acknowledge that "the human artifacts in this study had already been generated at the time of evaluation" — so this particular run is still a retrospective evaluation, undercutting the headline leakage-resistance claim for this specific instantiation even if the framework supports it in principle.
  - **Small model set, weak causal claims.** Six models, three providers, with no observed consistent differentiation between reasoning and non-reasoning variants (Qwen3 235B thinking actually performs *worse* than its non-thinking counterpart on F1 quality at 12% vs 39% all-pass). The authors flag this as requiring further investigation but it limits strong claims about which capabilities matter.
  - **Aggregate discovery barely above random in MTG.** Despite the per-deck 15.5× headline, "aggregate new-set discovery within any single configuration only modestly exceeded its random baseline" (the authors' own framing), and only one model (Gemini 3 Flash) beat the upper quartile of its random band. The Spearman ρ = 0.74 card-level result is the more robust signal and is appropriately emphasized.
  - **Cherry-picking risk in F1 qualitative examples.** The GPT-5.2/Ferrari MGU-H and gearbox-differential anecdotes are compelling but illustrative; the quantitative match-rate normalization (6.0/100 ideas) shows even the best model is mostly generating non-matching ideas.

## Strengths

- Conceptually clean framework with four crisp admissibility criteria and a portable recipe (constrained design space, adversarial, observable artifacts, clear cutoff).
- Leakage-resistant by construction — both corpora provably postdate all model training cutoffs — which directly addresses the most serious validity threat to existing AI-scientist benchmarks.
- Two structurally dissimilar domains (continuous engineering design vs discrete combinatorial selection) lending triangulation to the conclusions.
- Calibrated random baselines via hypergeometric mean and 10,000-sample Monte-Carlo percentiles — far more credible than typical "above chance" hand-waving.
- Honest, detailed failure-mode analysis with a unifying cross-domain diagnosis (filtering/prioritization/meta-alignment is the gap, not fluency) that has clear implications for AI-scientist system design.
- Full open-source release of code, data, prompts, and generated artifacts for both domains.
- Genuinely useful negative results: tool-use scaffolding can simultaneously improve formal legality (GPT-5.2 main-deck validity 11%→100%) while *hurting* substantive discovery — a non-obvious trade-off for agent designers.

## Limitations

- **Single non-expert reviewer** for all F1 match adjudications; no inter-annotator agreement, no sensitivity analysis — the central dependent variable rests on one person's judgment.
- **Incomplete expert ground truth** in both domains: F1 public coverage skews toward visible aero; MTG decklists show winning choices but not the full viable deck space or testing process.
- **Retrospective despite prospective framing** — this instantiation evaluated against artifacts that already existed; future work should pre-register prompts, corpus, models, and runs before reveal events.
- **Proxies, not science.** F1 and MTG are adversarial and fast-moving but are not themselves scientific discovery; the leap to "AI scientist capabilities" is an inference, not a measurement.
- **Small model sample (n=6)** and no consistent reasoning-vs-non-reasoning differentiation; the Qwen3-thinking result (worse than its non-thinking counterpart) is under-explored.
- **Aggregate MTG discovery only modestly above random** within any single configuration; the strong result is card-level rank-correspondence, not deck-level recovery.
- **Heterogeneous-domain score aggregation is unsolved** — the authors flag combining metrics from disparate domains into a single interpretable score as an open question, so the framework currently yields domain-specific rather than unified capability scores.
- **Match ≠ same reasoning.** A MATCH label shows convergence with a human idea under shared constraints, not that the model reasoned the same way or that the idea would translate into real value.

## Relevance

对关注"AI 科学家 / 自动化科研 / 假设生成评测"的研究者高度相关：该工作提出的前瞻性、防数据污染评测范式与跨领域一致的"过滤优先级缺失"失败模式，直接呼应了 LLM 能否产出真正新颖可用科研想法这一核心议题，可作为现有回顾式基准（DiscoveryBench、PaperBench 等）的有力补充视角。对评测方法论与 Agent 系统设计（何时该加脚手架、何时反伤探索）也有实操启示。

## Questions for Further Exploration

- Would adding a second domain-expert reviewer (a professional F1 engineer, a competitive MTG pro) materially change the MATCH/PARTIAL/NOMATCH distribution, and what would the inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ) actually be?
- Can the framework be run fully prospectively — prompts, corpus, models, and runs pre-registered *before* the F1 2026 season or the next MTG Pro Tour — to eliminate any residual leakage concern?
- Why does Qwen3 235B (thinking) perform *worse* than its non-thinking counterpart on F1 quality (12% vs 39% all-pass)? Is this a test-time-compute pathology, a prompt mismatch, or a genuine capability inversion?
- The INTERFACE-COUPLING vs BROAD-FREEDOM match-rate gap (5.0% vs 7.7%) and the build-around-card miss pattern both point to a "value from composition" deficit — can an explicit compositional/context-aware scoring agent close this gap without harming fluency?
- How would coupling the ideation pipeline to physical-validation tools (CFD for F1; deck-stress-testing simulation for MTG), as the authors suggest, change the filtering failure mode?
- Is the strong card-level Spearman ρ = 0.74 a real capability signal or partly an artifact of PT staples also being the most generically "powerful" cards that any reasonable card-evaluation heuristic would select?
- Can metrics from heterogeneous domains (F1 continuous design, MTG discrete selection, and candidate future domains like legal/regulatory cycles) be aggregated into a single interpretable AI-scientist capability score, or is domain-specific reporting the ceiling?
